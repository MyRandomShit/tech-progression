## How People Work

### The 3-Day Week

Monday. Wednesday. Friday. Twenty hours total. This is the global standard across developed economies. Most jobs run a 6–7 hour shift with no expectation of overtime. The concept of "working late" still exists but carries a different weight — it's a personal choice, not a survival mechanism.

The 1% who maintain critical infrastructure — power grids, food systems, logistics networks, AI oversight — work conventional schedules. They are well-compensated and quietly resentful of the fact that everyone else gets four-day weekends.

### The Workspace Is Virtual

Physical offices still exist, but they are vestigial — meeting spaces, social hubs, places to eat lunch with someone who has a face. The actual *work* happens in **shared VR environments**.

Every knowledge worker — programmers, designers, analysts, managers, architects — operates inside a collaborative virtual workspace accessed through lightweight AR/VR headsets. The technology matured around 2031 and became workplace standard by 2035. By 2038, "going to work" means putting on a headset at your kitchen table.

**What the workspace looks like:**

A shared three-dimensional space — imagine a room with no walls, floating in a void that defaults to neutral grey but can be skinned to anything (most teams pick something calming; Linus's team uses a low-poly forest). Inside the space:

- **3D infrastructure visualization.** Systems architecture is rendered spatially. Databases are visible structures — tables as literal tables, relationships as glowing connectors, data flow as animated streams. Microservices are floating nodes connected by API pathways. You can walk around your infrastructure, zoom into a service, and see its internal state rendered as a navigable structure. Debugging is *spatial* — you follow the request path through the system like tracing a wire through a building.
- **Floating screens.** Each developer has personal display surfaces that hover in their field of view — customizable, repositionable, stackable. Code, logs, dashboards, documentation — all floating panels that you arrange around yourself however you think. Most developers end up surrounded by a semicircle of screens. Linus keeps his minimal: one primary panel, one secondary, and a small persistent readout in his peripheral vision showing build status.
- **Avatars.** Coworkers appear as stylized avatars — realistic enough to read body language, abstracted enough that nobody spends three hours customizing their face. You can see who's focused (their avatar dims and a "deep work" indicator appears), who's available (full brightness), and who's AFK (avatar goes translucent). Spatial audio means you hear people who are close and can't hear people who aren't — walk over to someone to start a conversation, walk away to end it.

### How Code Is Written

Nobody types code anymore. Not by hand. Not in 2038.

**Code is authored through voice-directed AI assistants** — conversational interfaces that sit between the developer's intent and the codebase. The developer speaks; the assistant writes.

**The workflow:**

1. **Intent.** The developer describes what they want in natural language. "Add a validation check on the order total — if it exceeds the customer's credit limit, reject with a 402 and log the attempt." The assistant generates the code, the tests, and the integration points.
2. **Review.** The generated code appears on the developer's floating panel. They review it — reading, not writing. They can request changes conversationally: "Make the error message more specific." "Use the existing credit service instead of a direct DB call." "Add a retry for transient failures." The assistant revises.
3. **Refinement.** For complex logic, the developer can switch to a visual flow mode — seeing the logic as a diagram, dragging nodes, adjusting conditions. The assistant translates the visual changes back to code. For micro-adjustments, some developers gesture at specific lines and dictate inline changes. A few old-school holdouts still occasionally type — but it's like writing longhand in a world with printers. Functional, slightly eccentric.
4. **Deployment.** CI/CD is fully automated and visualized in the 3D workspace. Push a change and you can *watch* it propagate through the infrastructure — the affected services glow, tests run (green pulses for passing, red for failing), and the deployment rolls out as a visible wave. Rollbacks are a verbal command: "Roll back the last deploy on order-service."

**The assistant is not AI in the coder-world sense.** It's a conventional large language model — sophisticated pattern-matching, no access to the simulation's code layer, no coding ability. It is to `Decompile` what a calculator is to a mathematician: a tool that handles the mechanical work so the human can focus on the thinking.

### Linus's Job in 2038

Linus is a software developer at a mid-size enterprise. He works three days a week in VR — maintaining internal tools that manage inventory workflows. The systems are mundane. The work is steady. His job title hasn't changed since 2025, but what the job *means* has.

He doesn't write code. He *directs* code. He speaks to an assistant, reviews what it produces, adjusts, approves, deploys. His value isn't in syntax or typing speed — it's in *understanding the system*. Knowing which service talks to which. Knowing where the edge cases hide. Knowing that the date picker timezone offset will break again if you don't account for the legacy UTC conversion in the billing module.

The assistant can generate code faster than any human. It cannot understand *why* the billing module uses a legacy UTC conversion, or that changing it will break three downstream reports that finance runs on the second Tuesday of every month. Linus can. That's his job: institutional knowledge, system comprehension, and the judgment to know when the assistant's suggestion is technically correct but operationally catastrophic.

**The irony deepens in 2038:** Linus spends his work days talking to an AI assistant that generates code from his intent — a pattern-matching engine that translates human language into executable instructions. When he enters the coder world and discovers that programs are executed through mental intent directed at the simulation's code layer, the parallel is *exact*. He's been doing this his entire career. The interface just changed.

The VR workspace — with its floating panels, spatial visualization, and layered overlays — is also a direct analog of the coder's interface. The first time Linus's coding HUD activates, he doesn't panic the way other new coders do. He's been working inside overlaid data environments for years. The simulation's interface is just another workspace. Higher resolution. Worse documentation.

## How People Live

### The Four-Day Weekend

Tuesday. Thursday. Saturday. Sunday. These are the days that define 2038 more than the work days do.

Most people don't know what to do with them.

**The purpose crisis:** For the first time in human history, the majority of the population has more free time than they can meaningfully fill. Work — which had been the default answer to "what do I do with my day?" for every previous generation — now occupies less than a third of waking hours. The result is a quiet, pervasive crisis of meaning that manifests differently across demographics:

- **The Builders** found projects. Second careers, creative pursuits, community organizations, open-source contributions, craft skills. They are busy, fulfilled, and slightly evangelical about it. Linus falls here — his off days are for gaming, homesteading research, and the potato farm spreadsheet.
- **The Drifters** didn't. They scroll, they stream, they sleep, they drift through days that feel identical. Depression rates have climbed steadily since the transition. Not because life is bad — by material standards, 2038 is the most comfortable era in human history — but because comfort without purpose is its own kind of suffering.
- **The Hustlers** reject the premise. They work more than three days — freelancing, side businesses, gig work — not because they need the money but because they need the structure. They are loudly critical of "the lazy majority" and quietly terrified of what they'd feel if they stopped.

### The Productivity Paradox

The economists expected output to drop. Twenty hours a week instead of forty. Half the input, half the output. Basic math.

They were wrong.

Global productivity didn't decline after the transition. It **accelerated**. Year over year, output per capita has climbed faster since the 3-day work week than during any equivalent period of the 40-hour era. The curve isn't subtle — by 2038, the world produces more with 20-hour weeks than it did with 40-hour weeks in 2028. Not marginally more. *Significantly* more.

**Why it happened:**

- **Stress was the bottleneck, not hours.** The 40-hour work week (which was, in practice, 50–60 for most knowledge workers) didn't produce 40 hours of output. It produced 15–20 hours of real work padded with meetings, context-switching, recovery from burnout, and the low-grade cognitive fog of chronic sleep deprivation. When the work week shrank to 20 hours, the *productive* hours barely changed — what vanished was the padding. People work less. They produce the same. The math was never about hours. It was about the quality of the hours.
- **Creativity exploded.** This was the surprise nobody modeled. Rested, unstressed brains with four days of unstructured time produce *ideas* at a rate that exhausted, overworked brains cannot. The breakthroughs of the 2030s — in materials science, in biotech, in energy storage, in AI architecture, in VR infrastructure — disproportionately originated from people who were not working when they had the insight. The shower thought, the walk-in-the-park epiphany, the 3 AM notebook sketch — these were always where innovation came from. The 3-day work week didn't invent creative thinking. It *removed the thing that was suppressing it*.
- **The hobbyist-to-innovator pipeline.** Builders — the people who filled their off days with projects — started producing work that rivaled professional output. Open-source contributions surged. Independent research proliferated. Garage startups, which had been declining for years under the weight of credentialism and venture capital gatekeeping, came roaring back. A retired teacher in Cebu built a water purification system in her garage that outperformed the commercial models. A Drifter-turned-Builder in Estonia wrote an open-source logistics algorithm that three national postal services adopted. A fifteen-year-old in São Paulo designed a VR interface paradigm that a major studio licensed for eight figures. None of them were being paid to innovate. They were just people with time, curiosity, and no one telling them to stop.
- **Fewer hours meant higher standards.** When you only work 20 hours, you can't afford to waste any of them. Meetings shrank or disappeared. Bureaucratic overhead was ruthlessly cut. Decision-making compressed. The cultural tolerance for inefficiency — which had been enormous when everyone assumed they had 40 hours to fill — evaporated when every wasted hour was 5% of the week.

**The irony the old guard can't stomach:** Every metric they feared would collapse — GDP, innovation rate, patent filings, scientific publications, infrastructure development — improved. Not despite the reduced hours. *Because* of them. The 40-hour work week wasn't a productivity engine. It was a productivity *ceiling* — and nobody noticed until someone removed it.

The techno-optimists point to this as proof that human potential was always being wasted by the industrial-era work model. The skeptics point out that the acceleration depends on automation doing the baseline work, and ask what happens when the automation breaks. Both are right. Neither has a complete answer.

What nobody debates anymore: the world works less and produces more. The only open question is how long the curve keeps climbing.

### Common Jobs in 2038

Most jobs that existed in the 2020s still exist in name. What changed is the ratio: one person does what ten used to, and they do it from a screen.

#### Delivery — Drone Operators

Human delivery drivers are gone. No couriers, no truckers doing last-mile, no one handing you a package at the door. Everything — groceries, parcels, meals, medicine — arrives by autonomous drone. Fleets of them, managed by **drone operators** who sit in VR control rooms and supervise dozens of deliveries simultaneously.

The drones handle routing, obstacle avoidance, and drop-off autonomously. The operator's job is exception handling: a drone that can't find a landing spot, a delivery address that doesn't parse correctly, a customer who flagged a special instruction the AI can't interpret. Most shifts, an operator touches maybe 5% of their fleet's deliveries. The rest run clean. The job is 90% monitoring, 10% intervention — boring enough that operators develop elaborate side hobbies during their shifts. Some read. Some game on a second screen. Some just stare at the drone grid and zone out.

It pays well for what it is. It requires almost nothing.

#### Transport — Vehicle Recovery Specialists

Taxi drivers, rideshare drivers, bus drivers — all gone. Self-driving vehicles handle urban transit entirely. The fleet runs on optimized routing, dynamic dispatch, and predictive maintenance. No human touches a steering wheel under normal conditions.

**Vehicle recovery specialists** handle the abnormal conditions. A self-driving car that gets physically stuck — wedged on a curb, trapped by construction, high-centered on debris. A routing failure that leaves a vehicle in a dead-end the map says doesn't exist. A sensor malfunction that causes a vehicle to stop mid-intersection and refuse to move. These specialists are dispatched to physically drive or tow the vehicle out of whatever situation the autonomy can't solve. They're part mechanic, part driver, part babysitter for machines that are smarter than them 99% of the time and completely helpless the other 1%.

There aren't many of them. The job is on-call, reactive, and unpredictable — you might go three shifts without a call, then handle five recoveries in an hour during a rainstorm that confuses every sensor in the fleet.

### Housing and Cost of Living

With reduced work hours came reduced income expectations. The economy adjusted — slowly, painfully, but it adjusted. Housing is more accessible than in the 2020s (a combination of policy reform, remote-work decentralization, and reduced demand for urban proximity), but the rural land Linus wants for his farm is still priced at a premium. Homesteading has become aspirational — the 2038 equivalent of the 2010s' tiny-house movement, but with actual acreage.

**Single-income households are normal again.** The 3-day work week, combined with reduced cost of living from automation-driven deflation, means one working adult can comfortably support a family. The dual-income pressure that defined the 2000s–2020s — both partners working full-time just to cover mortgage, childcare, and healthcare — evaporated when the work week shrank and baseline costs dropped. One partner works Monday-Wednesday-Friday. The other doesn't have to. Some do anyway (Hustlers), but the economic *necessity* is gone.

The cultural ripple effects are significant. Families eat together again — not as a performative lifestyle choice, but as a default. Children grow up with a parent consistently home. Divorce rates dropped (less financial stress, more shared time). The stay-at-home parent — a role that had been economically impossible for most families for decades — returned without anyone campaigning for it.

### Population Stabilization

The demographers didn't see it coming.

By the late 2020s, the global fertility crisis was considered irreversible. Every developed nation was below replacement rate. South Korea had hit 0.7. Japan was depopulating visibly. Europe was running on immigration. The models all pointed the same direction: decline, aging, contraction.

Then the 3-day work week hit, and the curve *bent*.

Not immediately. Not dramatically. But by 2035, the trendlines started moving. By 2038, global fertility in developed economies has stabilized at or near replacement rate (~2.1). Some countries have ticked above it. The population isn't growing — it's *holding*. For the first time in decades, the demographic trajectory is flat instead of falling.

**Why it happened:**

Nobody planned it. No government policy said "have more children." The conditions simply changed:

- **Time.** Parents have four days off per week. The brutal arithmetic of the 2020s — where raising a child meant sacrificing career, income, sleep, and sanity simultaneously — no longer applies. There is *time* to be a parent without destroying everything else.
- **Money.** Single-income households work. Childcare costs dropped as one parent is consistently home. The financial barrier to children — which every survey for thirty years identified as the primary reason people weren't having them — fell through the floor.
- **Purpose.** In a world where most people are searching for meaning, raising a child provides it. This is not a cynical observation. It's what the data shows: the cohort most likely to have children in 2038 is the one that reported the highest rates of "purposelessness" in the pre-transition surveys. They wanted something to do that *mattered*. Children matter.
- **Space.** Remote work and decentralized living mean families aren't crammed into unaffordable urban apartments. Suburban and rural housing is accessible. Yards exist again. The physical environment supports children in a way that a Tokyo micro-apartment or a London two-bed never could.

The population stabilization is the quiet miracle of 2038 — the thing that economists and politicians point to when they need proof that the transition worked. It's not a boom. It's not a policy victory. It's just people, given time and security, making the choice that humans have always made when conditions permit.

The coder world doesn't care about any of this. Coders have their own population dynamics — and those are getting worse, not better.

### Government and Transparency

The push to digitalize government operations — which began in the 2010s and accelerated through every crisis since — reached a tipping point around 2032. Budgets, contracts, legislative drafts, regulatory decisions, procurement records, meeting minutes, voting records, departmental KPIs — all of it is publicly accessible in real-time through standardized open-data platforms. Not because politicians wanted it. Because the infrastructure made it inevitable, and the public made it non-negotiable.

**The citizen auditors:**

When you give a population four free days a week, some of them will find hobbies. Some will garden. Some will game. And some — the detail-obsessed, the civically restless, the professionally bored — will start reading government spending reports for fun.

**Citizen auditing** has become a genuine subculture. Tens of thousands of people worldwide spend their off-days crawling through public data, cross-referencing contracts, flagging anomalies, and publishing findings. They are not employed. They are not organized (mostly). They are just people with too much time, an internet connection, and the particular satisfaction that comes from finding a $4.2 million infrastructure contract awarded to a company that doesn't exist.

The ecosystem:

- **Audit communities** formed on VR social platforms — collaborative spaces where citizen auditors share datasets, divide analysis tasks, and peer-review each other's findings. Some of these communities have more analytical rigor than the government oversight agencies they're supplementing.
- **Reputation systems** emerged organically. Auditors who consistently produce accurate, well-sourced findings earn credibility. The best are followed by millions. A top-tier citizen auditor publishing a thread on a suspicious defense contract gets more public engagement than the official oversight committee's annual report.
- **Political consequence is real.** Multiple elected officials across multiple countries have been removed or forced to resign based on citizen audit findings. Governments initially resisted — classifying data, challenging legal standing, dismissing findings as "amateur." They stopped resisting when voter backlash made it clear that the public trusted the auditors more than the institutions being audited.
- **The professionalization gradient.** Some citizen auditors have gotten so good that governments now hire them — contracting the same people who were scrutinizing them from the outside to do it from the inside. Others refuse on principle: the moment you take a government paycheck, you stop being independent. The tension between "effective insider" and "independent watchdog" is the audit community's central debate.

**The net effect:** Government in 2038 is not less corrupt. But corruption is *harder to hide* and *faster to catch*. The feedback loop between action and exposure has shortened from years to weeks. Politicians who grew up in the pre-transparency era are perpetually uncomfortable. Politicians who entered office post-transition treat radical transparency as a given — they don't know any other way.

### Corporate Transparency and the Retail Swarm

The same transparency pressure didn't stop at government. It hit corporations — harder, and from a different angle.

**Everyone is in the stock market now.**

The combination of reduced living costs, single-income sufficiency, and four free days a week meant disposable income went up while spending needs went down. The money had to go somewhere. For hundreds of millions of people, it went into equities. By 2038, retail investor participation in global markets has tripled from 2024 levels. In the Philippines alone, stock market participation jumped from under 2% of the population to over 30%. The barriers — jargon, access, minimum amounts, intimidation — were stripped away by AI-assisted trading platforms that made buying shares as simple as ordering food.

**The watchers:**

Just as citizen auditors scrutinize government, a parallel culture of **corporate watchers** emerged — retail investors who treat publicly traded companies the way gamers treat a raid boss: something to be studied, understood, and exploited.

- **Earnings call communities.** Thousands of people now listen to quarterly earnings calls *live*, not as analysts or fund managers, but as hobbyists. They form VR watch parties — shared spaces where they listen together, annotate in real-time, and debate what the CFO actually meant when she said "temporary headwinds." The quality of analysis in these communities routinely matches or exceeds sell-side research. The professionals have noticed. Some have joined anonymously.
- **Supply chain trackers.** Retail investors with too much time started tracking corporate supply chains independently — monitoring shipping data, satellite imagery of factory lots, port activity, job postings, patent filings. They cross-reference public data to build pictures of corporate health that the companies themselves don't publish. A community of watchers in Manila caught a major semiconductor firm understating inventory losses three weeks before the official disclosure. The stock moved before the press release.
- **Governance activists.** Retail shareholders, in sufficient numbers, have voting power. Corporate boards that spent decades ignoring individual shareholders discovered that ten million individuals voting together are harder to dismiss than one hedge fund. Shareholder proposals on executive compensation, environmental policy, and labor practices now routinely pass — not because institutions pushed them, but because the retail swarm did.
- **The entertainment layer.** For many people, watching the market is simply *fun* — a real-time strategy game with real stakes, real data, and real consequences. It scratches the same itch as RTS gaming: resource management, incomplete information, pattern recognition, and the dopamine hit of a correct prediction. Some watchers don't even trade — they just follow the market the way previous generations followed sports. Leaderboards exist. Prediction accuracy is tracked. The top corporate watchers have follower counts in the millions.

**Corporate response:** Companies hate it. They used to manage their narratives through controlled disclosure, curated PR, and information asymmetry. Now a retired accountant in Iloilo can reconstruct their real margin structure from public filings before the investor relations team has finished their slide deck. The watchers are not always right — crowd analysis has its own biases and blind spots — but they are *always watching*, and the sheer volume of eyes makes sustained opacity nearly impossible.

The irony: corporations pushed for the automation and efficiency gains that created the 3-day work week. The 3-day work week created the population with enough free time to scrutinize them. They built the machine that watches them.

**What the Curia, the Epoch, and the Registries think about this:** They hate it. A population of amateur investigators with unlimited time and sophisticated data analysis skills is a direct threat to any organization that depends on secrecy. The Registries have dedicated teams that monitor citizen audit communities for anyone who stumbles too close to coder-world operations. The Epoch's circular compartmentalization was already robust — but The Thread now actively routes around any data that might appear in public audit databases. The Curia has the easiest time: the Vatican has been hiding things from public scrutiny for two thousand years. A few hobbyist auditors don't change that equation.

### Education

Education in 2038 has been rebuilt from the ground up. The old model — generalist schooling through age 18, then 3–4 years of university, then entry-level employment — is dead. It couldn't survive the transition. When most jobs require exception-handling judgment rather than baseline execution, the training pipeline has to produce something different.

**What changed:**

- **Fully government-subsidized.** All education — primary, secondary, tertiary, vocational, continuing — is free at point of access in every developed economy. Tuition, materials, living stipends during training. The logic was economic: if only 1% of the population needs to work to sustain baseline production, the quality of that 1% matters enormously. Undertrained operators cause cascading failures. A drone operator who can't handle an edge case crashes a fleet. A vehicle recovery specialist who doesn't understand sensor architecture makes things worse. The cost of training is trivial compared to the cost of incompetence.
- **Longer and more specialized.** Generalist degrees are relics. Education tracks are narrow, deep, and long. A drone operations certification takes 3 years. A vehicle recovery specialization takes 2 years after a general engineering foundation of 3. Medical training — which was already long — got longer, because the automation-assisted medicine of 2038 demands understanding of both the biology and the systems managing it. Software development takes 4–5 years of training before anyone trusts you in a production VR workspace.
- **Continuous retraining is standard.** The 3-day work week freed enough time that continuing education is no longer a sacrifice — it's a Tuesday activity. Most professionals cycle through updated certification programs every 2–3 years. The skills required shift as automation capabilities expand, and the training infrastructure is designed to keep pace. Nobody graduates once. You graduate, work, retrain, work, retrain, until you stop.
- **Apprenticeship over theory.** The 2038 education system learned from the failures of the 2010s university model: too much theory, too little application, too much debt, too little relevance. Modern training is apprenticeship-heavy — embedded in real work environments from year one, supervised by active practitioners, assessed on practical output rather than exam scores. Linus went through a 4-year software development track that put him inside a VR workspace in month three. He was directing code by month six. The remaining three and a half years were refinement, edge-case training, and system comprehension — learning *why* things work, not just *how* to make them work.
- **Education as a pastime.** With free access and free time, a significant portion of the population takes courses recreationally — not for career advancement, but for interest. History courses, language courses, art courses, science courses. The university lecture hall, which was dying in the 2020s, has been reborn as a leisure activity. Some people game on their off days. Some people take a semester of marine biology because they felt like it.

### The Gaming Boom

Gaming didn't just survive the transition to the 3-day work week. It became the **largest entertainment industry in the world** — bigger than film, music, sports, and streaming *combined*.

The math was simple: give four billion people four extra days a week with nothing they *have* to do, and the industry that sells structured, purposeful, rewarding experiences will eat everything else. Gaming did exactly that.

**The numbers:** By 2038, the global gaming industry generates more annual revenue than the GDP of most nations. Active daily players exceed 3 billion. The average adult spends 18–22 hours per week gaming — nearly as many hours as they spend working. For Drifters — the cohort with no projects, no second career, no purpose — the number is closer to 40.

**What changed:**

- **VR-native by default.** All major titles are built for full VR. Flat-screen gaming still exists (the retro scene is alive and well), but mainstream releases are immersive environments you physically inhabit. RTS games put you *standing on the battlefield*, issuing commands by voice and gesture, watching your units move across terrain beneath your feet. MMORPGs are indistinguishable from being inside the world — full spatial audio, haptic feedback, persistent social spaces where guilds have physical guild halls you walk through.
- **Gaming as social infrastructure.** For millions of people, their gaming guild is their primary community. Not a supplement to real-world friendships — the *primary* one. Guild events are social events. Raid nights are the 2038 equivalent of Friday night at the bar. Some guilds have their own governance, economies, and internal politics more complex than the cities their members live in.
- **Professional gaming exploded.** Esports was already growing in the 2020s. The 3-day work week detonated it. With four free days, the talent pool deepened, the audience widened, and the sponsorship money followed. Pro gaming is now a legitimate career path — not exceptional, not niche, just a job some people have. Linus is not pro. He's diamond-tier in RTS ladder — good enough to understand the meta, not good enough to compete. He watches pro matches the way his father watched chess tournaments.
- **Game development is one of the few booming job markets.** While most industries shrank their workforces, gaming studios *expanded*. Content demand is insatiable — 3 billion daily players burn through content faster than studios can produce it. Game development, game design, world-building, narrative writing, VR environment architecture — these are growth careers in a world where most careers are contracting. The industry employs millions and is still hiring.
- **The meaning pipeline.** Games provide what the 3-day work week took away: structure, challenge, progression, and the feeling of accomplishment. A Drifter with no purpose can log into an MMO and have a quest, a team, a goal, and a measurable sense of progress within five minutes. This is not a criticism — it is the most effective mental health intervention the transition era has produced. Therapists recommend gaming. Governments subsidize VR hardware. The line between "coping mechanism" and "way of life" is gone, and nobody is sure it matters.

**The uncomfortable question:** The line between "playing a game" and "living in a simulation" is thinner than anyone is comfortable discussing. Three billion people spend half their waking hours inside constructed realities with rules written by developers, governed by systems they can learn but not see, populated by entities (NPCs, AI companions) that behave convincingly but aren't real. They are, without knowing it, rehearsing for a truth about their own world that they will never learn.

### The Simulation Parallel

The 2038 world is, without knowing it, building a *mirror* of the simulation it lives inside:

- VR workspaces overlay data on top of reality — the same way the coder interface overlays code on top of the world.
- AI assistants execute instructions from human intent without understanding them — the same way daemons execute programs without comprehension.
- The 3D infrastructure visualization that developers navigate at work is structurally identical to what a coder sees when they `Inspect` the simulation's code layer.
- Gaming — especially MMORPGs — trains exactly the cognitive skills that interface with the simulation: spatial reasoning, pattern recognition, system comprehension, the instinct to look at a world and ask "what are the rules?"

The population of 2038 is, collectively, training itself to think like coders. They just don't know it. And for most of them, it doesn't matter. The simulation's interface requires a neurological architecture that only a tiny percentage are born with.

But the cognitive *readiness* is higher than it has ever been in history. More people think in systems. More people navigate layered data environments. More people talk to pattern-matching engines and direct code through intent.

If Zero designed the coding interface to activate in minds that think a certain way, then 2038 is the most fertile ground the simulation has ever produced. The number of latent coders — people with the neural architecture but no trigger event — may be higher than at any point in human history.

Nobody knows this yet. The coder world is about to get a lot more crowded.

## The Philippines in 2038

The story is set primarily in the **Philippines** — a country that, by 2038, has transformed more dramatically than most developed economies precisely because it had the furthest to leap.

### Why the Philippines

The Philippines adopted the 3-day work week later than the US or Europe (2034 vs. 2031), but the effects hit harder and faster. The country's economy had already been reshaping itself around BPO, remote services, and tech labor for decades — the transition to VR workspaces and AI-directed workflows was not a disruption but an *acceleration* of what was already happening. By 2038, Manila is one of the top five global hubs for VR workspace infrastructure, and the Philippines produces more software developers per capita than any country in Southeast Asia.

**What changed:**

- **The OFW era ended.** For generations, the Philippines exported labor — millions of overseas Filipino workers sending remittances home. When the 3-day work week and VR workspaces eliminated the need to physically relocate for work, the economic engine inverted. Filipinos who would have gone to the Gulf, to Singapore, to the US, now work from home in Cavite, Cebu, or Davao, logging into VR workspaces hosted in San Francisco or London. The remittance economy was replaced by a remote-work economy. Families stayed together. The diaspora stopped growing.
- **Provincial revival and the township spread.** The decentralization that remote VR work enabled hit the Philippines uniquely hard — in a good way. Metro Manila, which had been swallowing the country's population for decades, *stopped growing*. The national population didn't thin — it **redistributed**. Millions of Filipinos now live in **townships** outside the capital: planned or retrofitted communities in Cavite, Laguna, Bulacan, Batangas, Cebu, Iloilo, Davao, Cagayan de Oro, and dozens of secondary cities that were dormitory towns a generation ago and are now self-contained daily-life hubs. Young professionals didn't just "move to the province." They moved to *townships* — barangay clusters and municipal cores with their own co-working cafés, drone pads, clinics, schools, and weekend markets. Manila remains the prestige address and the corporate registry capital. It is no longer where most Filipinos *live*.
- **The COVID memory.** The pandemic years are still culturally vivid — not as trauma alone, but as **proof**. Every township built or rebuilt after 2030 carries the lesson: when the capital locks down and the ports choke, you survive on what you have *locally*. Barangay-level food gardens, municipal cold storage, water treatment micro-plants, solar+battery backup for critical buildings, and drone-delivery redundancy aren't prepper eccentricity. They're municipal checklist items, the way fire exits were after earlier disasters. Older Filipinos say "after COVID" the way their parents said "after Martial Law" — a shorthand for *we don't assume the center will save us*. Linus's Benguet potato dream fits this logic exactly: land, water, calories, independence from a supply chain that once failed everyone at once.
- **Supply-independent townships.** A typical 2038 township aims for **baseline self-sufficiency**, not autarky. It won't manufacture semiconductors or refine fuel. It *will* cover a week of staples without Manila: rice and vegetables from surrounding farms or vertical plots, protein from regional aquaculture and poultry cooperatives, medicine from a municipal formulary stockpile, and power from a mix of grid, solar, and municipal batteries. Inter-town trade fills the gaps. National logistics still matter for luxuries and specialized goods. The design goal is simple: **no single chokepoint can starve us in seven days**. Drone networks between townships made this achievable in the archipelago faster than road-heavy countries could copy it.
- **Government less centralized.** Manila still hosts Malacañang, the central agencies, and the largest tax base. But governance **followed the population**. Regional and provincial governments gained real budget authority, hiring power, and standard-setting capacity that used to require a trip to the capital or a decade of political patronage. Health, education, zoning, local infrastructure, and disaster response are increasingly **provincial or city-first** — with national policy setting floors, not micromanaging floors. Citizen auditors watch both layers now; a suspicious municipal contract in Iloilo gets the same swarm attention as a national defense line item. The result is messier, slower to coordinate on megaprojects, and harder for a single president to steer by decree — but far more resilient when one island loses contact with the center. The Philippines in 2038 is a **network of townships**, not a wheel with one hub.
- **Infrastructure leapfrog.** The Philippines skipped several infrastructure generations entirely. Fiber-optic and satellite internet blanketed the archipelago by 2033 (a necessity for VR workspaces — latency kills). Drone delivery networks arrived before reliable road logistics did — in many island provinces, drones deliver faster and more reliably than any vehicle ever could, because there are no roads to get stuck on. The joke is that the Philippines solved its infrastructure problems by making infrastructure irrelevant.
- **Gaming culture hit differently.** The Philippines was already one of the most gaming-dense countries in the world before the transition. Internet cafés, mobile gaming, esports — the culture was *there*. When the 3-day work week freed four days and VR gaming went mainstream, the Philippines didn't adopt gaming as a pastime. It *deepened* an existing identity. Filipino esports teams are globally competitive. Filipino gaming communities are among the largest and most organized in the world. The country produces a disproportionate number of pro gamers, game developers, and VR world-builders. Linus is not unusual for being a gamer — he's unusual for *not* being more serious about it.

### Linus's Manila

Linus is an outlier in his own generation: he stayed in the capital when most of his peers moved to townships in Cavite, Laguna, or back to their home provinces. He lives in an above-average condo in Makati — not Rockwell or the Ayala penthouse tier, but a clean two-bedroom in a 2029 building with good internet, a gym he uses twice a week, and a balcony wide enough for a small table and the plastic pots where he grows herbs and a single stubborn sili plant. Twenty-third floor. On clear mornings he can see the Makati skyline blending into BGC, the cranes still building something somewhere. The unit is more space than a single person needs, but the 3-day work week made Makati rent reasonable for his salary, and he likes the quiet efficiency of the neighborhood — walkable, organized, the kind of place where everything works and nobody bothers you. His condo has no week of staples in the pantry. If Manila choked tomorrow, he'd be hungry in three days. He knows that. The Benguet spreadsheet is partly about fixing it.

His company is headquartered in Singapore but he's never been there. His team is scattered across four time zones. His manager is in Kuala Lumpur. His closest work friend is in Osaka. They meet in the low-poly forest every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. The forest has a koi pond that someone added as a joke three years ago. Nobody has removed it.

On work days he sometimes walks to a co-working café on Poblacion — the neighborhood reinvented itself again after the bar scene faded, now it's creative spaces and VR cafés in the same narrow streets. He puts on his headset surrounded by other people putting on theirs. The café sells pourover and pandesal. He doesn't need to be there. He goes because silence in his condo on a work day makes him feel like a Drifter, and he isn't one. Not yet.

## Linus's Day (Pre-Awakening)

**Monday — Work Day:**

- 7:00 — Wake up. Barako coffee from the pot his father gave him. Check the assistant's overnight summary of production alerts (nothing critical, as usual).
- 7:30 — Headset on. Log into the VR workspace. The low-poly forest renders around him. The koi pond is still there. His floating panels materialize.
- 7:45 — Standup. Avatars gather. Status updates. Sprint review. He listens more than he talks. His manager's avatar has a new hat — Kuala Lumpur apparently had a team event.
- 8:00–12:30 — Work. Voice-direct the assistant through three tickets. Review generated code. Adjust. Deploy. Watch the green pulses. Fix the date picker timezone offset again.
- 12:30 — Lunch. Headset off. Leftover adobo at his kitchen counter — the condo has an open layout, no formal dining table, just the counter and two stools. Homesteading video on his phone — a Benguet family growing potatoes at 1,500 meters. He bookmarks it.
- 13:00 — Headset on. Afternoon session. Deeper work — refactoring a legacy integration that the assistant keeps generating wrong because it doesn't understand the billing module's history. He explains it. Again.
- 14:00 — Done. Log out. Headset off.

**Tuesday — Off Day:**

- Sleep in. The balcony herbs need water.
- Morning: Update the potato farm spreadsheet. Check land listings. One new plot in Benguet — 2 hectares, mountain access road, no existing structure. Bookmarked.
- Afternoon: RTS ladder. Three games. Win two. The third loss was a macro mistake at the 14-minute mark — he replays it twice.
- Evening: MMO. Guild event. He runs support. Nobody dies. He reads the patch notes for the upcoming content drop. There's a hidden quest chain hinted at in the NPC dialogue that nobody else has noticed. His guild leader is from Davao. They've never met in person.
- Night: Decision journal. Nothing significant to log. He writes "no major decisions today" and closes the spreadsheet. Through the balcony door, twenty-three floors up, Makati hums — autonomous shuttles on Ayala, the faint bass of a Poblacion bar that survived the transition, the sili plant leaning toward the city light.

This is his life. Quiet, structured, purposeful in its small way. He has a plan. He has a routine. He has a dream with a spreadsheet. The dream is in Benguet. The spreadsheet says seven years out.

It lasts until the first `Decompile`.
