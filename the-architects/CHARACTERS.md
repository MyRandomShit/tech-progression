# Characters

## Emile Kerrigan — Linus's Father

**Age:** Late 50s
**Occupation:** Retired mathematics professor. Spent 25 years teaching game theory and combinatorial optimization at a mid-tier university — not prestigious enough to attract geniuses, not obscure enough to be ignored. He was the kind of professor students remembered a decade later, not for his warmth, but for the fact that he was *right* about things they didn't understand at the time.

**Status at story start:** Alive. Semi-retired. Lives alone in a modest house full of books and half-finished chess problems. Linus visits when he remembers to. They talk comfortably but not often. The relationship is quiet, functional, and deeper than either of them acknowledges.

---

### The Discipline

Emile raised Linus with a parenting style that no manual would recommend and no therapist would endorse. It worked anyway — or it broke him in exactly the right way, depending on who you ask.

**The rules of the Kerrigan household:**

1. **Every decision is a game.** When Linus was seven and wanted ice cream for dinner, Emile didn't say no. He laid out the decision matrix: short-term reward (sugar), long-term cost (stomach ache, poor sleep, diminished performance tomorrow), opportunity cost (the dinner he wouldn't eat), and precedent effect (if he says yes today, what does Linus expect tomorrow?). Then he let Linus choose. Linus chose ice cream. He got a stomach ache. Emile never said "I told you so." He said: "What did the payoff matrix predict?" Linus was seven. He remembered.

2. **Losses are lessons, not punishments.** Emile never grounded Linus. Never took away privileges as retribution. Instead, he made Linus *analyze his own failures*. Bad grade on a test? Sit down, identify the decision point where the outcome diverged from the optimal path. Was it insufficient study (resource allocation error), wrong material studied (information asymmetry), or test anxiety (performance under uncertainty)? Linus learned to dissect his own mistakes with clinical precision before he hit puberty. He hated it. It made him exceptional at post-mortems. As an adult, he runs incident reviews at work with an eerie calm that his coworkers find slightly unsettling.

3. **Never lie. Mislead if you must.** Emile considered dishonesty a strategy failure — not a moral failing, but an *inefficient* one. Lies require maintenance. They compound. They create information asymmetry that eventually collapses. Misdirection, on the other hand, is an art. Emile taught Linus the difference between saying something false and saying something true that leads to a false conclusion. "I didn't eat the cake" is a lie. "The cake was on the counter when I left the kitchen" is technically accurate and lets the listener draw their own wrong conclusion. Linus absorbed this as a communication philosophy: be honest, but control the framing. In the coder world, this makes him dangerously good at navigating conversations with Architects and faction leaders who are used to reading people like open books.

4. **Play to not lose, then play to win.** Emile drilled this distinction relentlessly. Most people play to win — they take risks, overextend, chase the big payoff. Emile taught Linus to play to *not lose first*: eliminate catastrophic outcomes, secure your position, ensure survival. Only then do you look for the winning move. "You can't win from the graveyard," Emile said. Linus heard it a thousand times. He internalized it so deeply that it became his default operating mode — in games, in work, in life. And later, in the coder world, it becomes the reason he survives when more powerful coders don't. They play to win. He plays to not lose. They overextend, burn cycles, stack overflow. He turtles, conserves, and outlasts.

5. **Understand the other player.** Emile never let Linus play a game without modeling the opponent. Chess wasn't about your pieces — it was about predicting theirs. Poker wasn't about your hand — it was about reading what they *think* their hand is worth. Even board games at family gatherings became exercises in behavioral prediction. "What does Uncle Martin want? What does he think you want? What will he do if he thinks you know what he wants?" Linus was twelve. He started winning at poker against adults. They stopped inviting him to the table.

### Game Theory as Parenting

Emile didn't teach Linus game theory as an academic subject. He taught it as a *way of seeing*. Every interaction is a game. Every game has players, information, strategies, and payoffs. Most people play on instinct. Emile raised Linus to play on analysis.

The result is a person who:
- Treats every new situation as a system to be understood before it's engaged
- Defaults to minimizing downside before maximizing upside
- Reads people's incentives faster than he reads their emotions
- Is pathologically patient — willing to wait for the optimal move instead of taking the available one
- Distrusts anyone who offers a deal without visible cost to themselves

**The direct line to `Decompile`:** Emile trained Linus to reverse-engineer *people* — to look at what someone does and work backward to *why*. What are their incentives? What information do they have? What's their payoff matrix? This is, structurally, the same cognitive operation as `Decompile`: observe the output, reverse-engineer the source. Emile didn't know he was training his son to interface with reality's source code. He thought he was teaching him to win at chess.

### The Weird Parts

Emile's discipline wasn't cruel, but it was *unusual* enough that Linus doesn't talk about it casually:

- **Decision journals.** From age ten, Emile had Linus keep a notebook of significant decisions — what he chose, what he expected, what happened. Weekly review. Linus still keeps one as an adult, though it's a spreadsheet now. In the coder world, this habit becomes his memory-loss tracking system — the journal becomes the record of who he was, written by a version of himself that still remembered.

- **Asymmetric games at dinner.** Emile would propose games over meals — negotiations, auctions, allocation problems — where the rules were deliberately unfair. Linus started with fewer resources, less information, or worse position. The lesson: the world is not fair, and complaining about it is not a strategy. Find the edge in the constraint. Linus hated these. He also won more than half of them by age fifteen.

- **Silence as a tool.** Emile would sometimes go an entire dinner without speaking — not as punishment, but as an exercise. "Most people fill silence because it makes them uncomfortable. Uncomfortable people make bad decisions. Practice being comfortable with nothing happening." Linus learned to sit in silence. As an adult, he's the person in the meeting who doesn't speak first, who lets others fill the void, who waits. In the coder world, this patience is mistaken for weakness — exactly once.

- **The No-Rescue Rule.** If Linus got himself into a problem, Emile didn't fix it. Not homework, not social conflicts, not the time he locked himself out of the house in winter. Emile would discuss strategy *after* — but never intervene *during*. Linus learned self-reliance not as a virtue, but as a default. He doesn't ask for help easily. This is both his greatest strength in the coder world — where allies are unreliable and factions have agendas — and his greatest vulnerability, because there are situations where refusing help is indistinguishable from pride.

### What Emile Doesn't Know

Emile is a Natural. He has no coding ability, no awareness of the hidden world, no connection to any faction. He is simply a man who taught his son to think in systems — and the simulation noticed.

Whether Emile's parenting was coincidence or something subtler is an open question. Did Zero's design select for Linus *because* of how Emile raised him? Or did Emile raise Linus this way *because* something in his own neurology — a faint echo of the same interface, too weak to manifest as coding but strong enough to shape cognition — pushed him toward systems thinking without his knowledge?

Emile would reject both interpretations. He'd say he raised his son to think clearly in an unclear world, and that's all there is to it. He'd be wrong. But he'd be wrong in the way that only a man who taught his son to distrust easy answers can be.

### Linus and Emile — The Relationship

They don't say "I love you." They never have. The closest they get is Emile calling to ask if Linus has reviewed his annual budget allocation (he has), and Linus calling to tell Emile about a game theory application he found in a work problem (Emile is pleased but doesn't say so).

When Linus starts losing memories, Emile is the first person he thinks about protecting — not from the coder world, but from *his own erosion*. The memories of his father's lessons are among the most deeply embedded in his neural architecture. They're structural. They're *him*. If `Decompile` ever eats deep enough to reach the decision journals, the dinner games, the silence exercises — Linus won't just lose memories. He'll lose the cognitive framework that makes him *Linus*.

The potato farm spreadsheet has a line item for a second bedroom. He's never told Emile.

## Sera Voss — Linus's Mother ("Deceased")

**Real name:** Sera Voss (birth name, never used the Kerrigan surname in the coder world)
**Cover name:** Sarah Kerrigan (the name Linus and Emile knew her by)
**Age:** Early 50s
**Status at story start:** Alive. Linus and Emile believe she died in a car accident when Linus was ten.

---

### The Death That Wasn't

Linus was ten years old when his mother died. Closed casket. Drunk driver. A Tuesday. Emile handled the logistics — the funeral, the paperwork, the disposal of her belongings — with the grim efficiency of a man solving the worst optimization problem of his life. Linus handled it the way his father taught him: he analyzed it. Short-term grief (unavoidable, allow it). Long-term adaptation (necessary, begin immediately). He wrote it in his decision journal: *Mom died. Can't change the input. Adjust the model.*

He was ten. It was the most brutal thing he'd ever written. He doesn't know how brutal it was for her to read it.

Because she did read it. She reads all of his journals. She has never stopped watching.

**Sera Voss didn't die.** She staged the accident, fabricated the body, `Null`ed the investigating officers' suspicions, and vanished from the Natural world entirely — because staying would have killed her family for real.

### Who She Actually Is

Sera is a Layer 4 Architect and a senior operative in **The Open** — not a cell member, not a field agent, but one of the closest things the decentralized network has to strategic leadership. She doesn't command. The Open doesn't work that way. But when cells need coordination, when operations span multiple regions, when the network needs someone who can see the whole board — Sera is one of the handful of people who gets the call.

She has been in the coder world since she was fifteen. She was identified by The Open before any Registry could find her, trained by a mentor who recognized her unusual ability, and has spent thirty-five years building the network's intelligence infrastructure, running anti-Syndicate operations, and keeping the Registries from achieving the total coder control they want.

She is exceptionally good at what she does. She is also one of the most wanted coders in the world — by the Bureau, by the Ledger, by Die Schicht, and by at least three Syndicate networks that she has personally dismantled.

**She left her family because her enemies were closing in.** A Registry mole inside The Open had been feeding operational data for months. Sera's identity was compromised — not her cover name, but her real operational identity. The people hunting her didn't know about Emile and Linus. Not yet. But they would have. And when they found them, they would have used them: `Inject`ed, `Null`ed, leveraged, or killed. Sera had seventy-two hours between learning about the breach and the point of no return.

She spent the first twelve hours trying to find another way. She spent the next twelve accepting that there wasn't one. She spent the last forty-eight executing the cleanest disappearance the coder world has ever seen.

### The Ability: `Cache`

Sera's unique program — her signature, her edge, the thing that made her invaluable to The Open and terrifying to everyone else.

**`Cache`** is a Layer 4 program that allows her to **extract memories from a mind and store them externally** — outside the brain, outside the person, preserved intact in the simulation's code layer itself.

This breaks the fundamental rule of the cost system. Memories that are overwritten by programs are supposed to be *gone* — erased, irrecoverable. `Cache` doesn't prevent the overwrite. It **copies the memory out first**, storing it in a hidden partition of the local code layer, anchored to a physical object or location. The memory is gone from the brain — the coder still pays the cost — but it *exists* somewhere. Retrievable. Restorable.

**What `Cache` can do:**

| Operation | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Extract and store** | Copy a specific memory from a mind (self or others, with contact) and write it to an external anchor. The person loses the memory normally — they don't know it's been preserved |
| **Retrieve and restore** | Read a cached memory from its anchor and write it back into the original mind. The person *remembers again* — suddenly, completely, as if it had never been gone. The program slot that overwrote it is not displaced; both coexist, though this strains the heap |
| **External archive** | Maintain a library of cached memories across multiple anchor points. Sera has caches hidden across three continents — in books, in walls, in objects buried in locations only she knows |
| **Cache for others** | Store another coder's memories before they compile a new program — a safety net. The coder still pays the cost and feels the loss, but the memory is recoverable afterward. This service alone makes Sera more valuable than most Architects |

**What `Cache` cannot do:**

- It cannot prevent the cost. The brain still overwrites. The loss still feels real.
- It cannot store programs — only memories. `Cache` reads the brain's organic data, not compiled code.
- It requires physical contact to extract. She must touch the person's head, and the process takes minutes of sustained 70%+ CPU load. Not something you do in combat.
- Restoring is disorienting. The person receives a flood of recovered memory with no context for its absence. Some describe it as living a moment twice. Others describe it as a collision between who they are now and who they were before the loss. Restoring critical memories — identity-level, relationship-level — can cause temporary psychological fracture.
- The external caches are *physical*. If the anchor object is destroyed, the memory is lost — permanently, this time for real.

**Why `Cache` made her a target:**

`Cache` is the most strategically dangerous ability in the coder world. Not because of what it does in combat — it's useless in a fight. But because of what it means for the power structure:

- **The Registries** want `Cache` because it would allow them to produce Architects without permanent cost. Compile a Layer 4 program, `Cache` the lost memories before compilation, restore them after. The coder keeps the program *and* their humanity. The entire corruption economy — the thing that keeps Architects rare, costly, and controllable — collapses. The Registries want to *monopolize* this.
- **The Syndicates** want `Cache` because it's the ultimate product. Sell memory insurance to wealthy coders. Offer restoration services at extortionate prices. Create a market where memories have literal monetary value. The Syndicates want to *commodify* this.
- **The Open** wants `Cache` to remain free — available to any coder who needs it, controlled by no faction. This is why Sera chose The Open. And this is why every other faction hunts her.

### How She Protected Her Family

Before she left, Sera spent her final forty-eight hours doing three things:

1. **She `Cache`d every memory Emile and Linus had of her true nature.** Every moment where she'd slipped — a strange look when she saw something no one else could see, a too-fast reaction, an unexplained absence. She extracted them cleanly and replaced the gaps with plausible mundane memories. Emile remembers his wife as a quiet, warm, slightly private woman who worked in consulting and traveled occasionally. That's the edited version. The real version — the one where she came home at 3 AM with a nosebleed and a cracked anchor weapon she hid in the garage — is stored in a cache buried under the floorboards of a house in Bruges that no one has lived in for twenty years.

2. **She `Encrypt`ed both of them.** Emile and Linus are invisible to `Ping` sweeps. Their neural signatures are masked — not because they're coders (Emile isn't, and Linus's ability was dormant), but because Sera rewrote how the simulation reports their existence to any scanning program. To every Registry agent, every Syndicate hunter, and every Open operative who has ever swept their city: Emile and Linus Kerrigan do not register. They are, in the code layer, *commented out*. Invisible. Absent. `//`.

3. **She `Cache`d her own memories of them.** This is the cruelest part. Sera extracted her memories of Linus's first word, his first steps, the way he frowned when he was concentrating, the sound of Emile's laugh when Linus beat him at chess for the first time. She stored them in a cache she buried and *then she deleted her own copies*. She walks the coder world carrying no exploitable emotional data. No one can `Inspect` her and find a son. No one can `Null` her love for Linus because, operationally, it isn't there. She is clean. She is airtight.

   She visits the cache in Bruges once a year. She restores the memories for one night. She remembers her family — fully, completely, agonizingly. Then she extracts them again and walks away.

   She has done this for fifteen years.

### What She Knows About Linus

Sera has watched from a distance. She has operatives — not Open cells, but personal assets, off-book — who monitor Linus's life without his knowledge. She knows about the enterprise Java job. She knows about the RTS games. She knows about the potato farm spreadsheet.

She does not know about `Decompile`.

When Linus's ability awakens and he appears on the grid — faintly, imperfectly, bleeding through the `Encrypt` she laid fifteen years ago — Sera will face the worst decision of her operational career: reveal herself and protect him, or stay dead and let the coder world find her son without knowing whose son he is.

If any faction discovers that the unknown coder with the impossible ability is Sera Voss's child, the calculus changes entirely. He stops being a curiosity and becomes *leverage*. Against her. Against The Open. Against everything she's spent thirty-five years building.

The `Encrypt` she placed on him as a child was designed for a Natural. It was not designed for a coder. It is already fraying.

---

### Sera and Emile — The Truth He Doesn't Know

Emile believes his wife was a Normal woman who died in a car accident. He grieved. He moved on. He raised their son alone with game theory and silence.

He does not know that Sarah Kerrigan was Sera Voss. He does not know she could reshape matter, store minds, and dismantle criminal networks. He does not know that the woman who burned the toast every Sunday morning and couldn't parallel park was also the woman who `Kill`ed a Syndicate Architect in São Paulo and walked away with his daemon weapon.

He does not know she's alive.

The question of whether to tell him — and whether the telling would break him or complete him — is one Sera has run through every game theory framework Emile ever taught her. Every model says the same thing: the expected value of truth is negative. The risk of exposure, emotional destabilization, and operational compromise exceeds the benefit of reunion.

She stays dead. Every model says she should.

She visits the cache in Bruges once a year. Every model says she shouldn't.
