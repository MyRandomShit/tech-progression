# The Protagonist

---

## Identity

**Name:** Linus Kerrigan
**Age:** Mid 20s at story start
**Setting:** **2038**, **Philippines** — Makati condo, M/W/F VR work, four-day weekends for gaming and the Benguet spreadsheet. Everyday world detail: `WORLD_2038.md`. Coder-world rules: `WORLD_LORE.md`.
**Job:** Software developer at a mid-size enterprise — the kind that builds internal tools nobody outside the company will ever see. Inventory management systems. Approval workflow dashboards. JIRA tickets that say things like "fix date picker timezone offset on the PO form." He writes Java, sits through sprint retros, and argues about naming conventions in pull request comments.

He is good at his job — not brilliant, not a prodigy, just *solid*. The guy who ships clean code on time, writes tests without being asked, and debugs production issues at 11 PM without complaining. His manager likes him. His coworkers respect him. He will never be a tech lead. He's fine with that.

After work, he games. Two genres, two modes of thinking:

**RTS** — StarCraft, Age of Empires, Company of Heroes. Anything with resource management, fog of war, and the dopamine hit of a well-timed macro. Diamond-tier in ladder, not GM. He plays because he likes *systems* — the satisfaction of seeing an entire map's worth of moving parts and knowing where to apply pressure.

**MMORPGs** — the long game. He's the guy who reads patch notes like scripture, min-maxes a support build nobody else runs, and knows every hidden quest in a zone because he talked to every NPC. He doesn't rush endgame. He explores. He reads the lore tabs. He understands that the world has rules written by developers and that the players who learn those rules deepest have the real advantage — not the ones who grind the hardest.

Both habits map onto the coder world in ways he never expected:

- **RTS thinking** becomes survival instinct. He rations `Decompile` the way he rations minerals: spend only what you must, save for the late game, never overextend. When Architects brute-force a fight, Linus plays the macro game. Slow. Patient. Efficient. Infuriating to fight against.
- **MMO thinking** becomes his edge in understanding the hidden world. While other coders treat their abilities as weapons, Linus treats the coder world like a game system to be *studied*. He asks questions no one else bothers with: Why do layers exist? Who set the rules? What's in the patch notes? He's the only coder who approaches the simulation like a player who suspects the devs left easter eggs — because that's exactly what he's done for every game he's ever played.

**The Dream:** He wants out. Not dramatically — no existential crisis, no burnout meltdown. He just doesn't want to be doing this at forty. His plan, the one he half-jokes about but has a real spreadsheet for, is to save enough to buy a small plot of land somewhere rural — nothing fancy, maybe a few hectares — and homestead. Grow potatoes. Keep chickens. Fix things with his hands instead of his IDE. He watches homesteading YouTube at lunch. He has a bookmarked folder of land listings in provinces he's never visited. The spreadsheet says he's eleven years out.

It's the most ordinary dream in the world: a quiet life, on his own terms, away from standups and deployment pipelines. A potato farm. That's it.

This dream becomes the story's emotional anchor. Every memory he loses to `Decompile` is a piece of the person who wanted that farm. Every step deeper into the coder world pulls him further from the life he actually chose. The factions want him to be a weapon, a key, a revolutionary. He wanted to grow potatoes. The tragedy isn't that he can't have the farm — it's that eventually he might not remember wanting it.

**Background:** Natural. No coder lineage, no secret society, no mentor waiting in the wings. His life is standup meetings, takeout at his desk, and the quiet satisfaction of a well-structured service class. The coding world — the *real* coding world — would never notice him.

The irony is architectural. He spends eight hours a day writing programs that manipulate data in a system he fully understands — and he has no idea he's living inside one. The terminology he uses at work — compile, execute, debug, deploy, refactor, kill process — is the same vocabulary the coder world uses to reshape reality. He types `grep` into a terminal every morning. He has no idea what it means when someone can do it with their mind.

When `Decompile` awakens, the metaphors collapse. The thing he does for a living — reading code, understanding systems, tracing logic through layers of abstraction — turns out to be the exact cognitive muscle that interfaces with the simulation. His brain was already wired for it. Not because he trained it. Because that's what Zero's design selected for: pattern recognition, systems thinking, the instinct to look at something that works and ask *how*.

Every programmer has that instinct. He's the first one where the simulation answered back.

## The Ability: `Decompile`

### What It Is

In programming, decompiling is the act of taking compiled code — opaque, unreadable machine instructions — and reversing it back into human-readable source. It's reverse engineering. It's looking at what something *does* and extracting *how it was built*.

His ability is exactly that, applied to the simulation's code layer.

**When he witnesses a program being executed, he can reverse-engineer its structure and compile it into his own mind.**

He doesn't need to be taught. He doesn't need a `Fork` donor or an `Inject`or. He watches a coder run `Invoke`, and his brain — without permission, without intent — tears the program apart, reads the source, and writes its own copy.

### Why This Is Unprecedented

Every coder in history has learned programs through one of three paths:

1. **Self-discovery** — painstaking trial and error at their accessible layer. Takes years. Most coders learn 2–3 programs this way in a lifetime.
2. **`Fork`** — a willing donor copies the program, losing it themselves. Expensive, sacrificial, rare.
3. **`Inject`** — forced installation by a more powerful coder. Involuntary and destructive.

`Decompile` bypasses all three. He just *watches*. The program is running in the open — every coder assumes their execution is ephemeral, visible only through `Inspect` and only as a surface signature. No one has ever been able to read the underlying instructions from observation alone.

He can. He doesn't know how. He didn't choose this.

### How It Works (What He Understands)

Throughout most of the story, he believes `Decompile` is a narrow, limited ability with harsh constraints. He is *wrong* — but he doesn't know that, and neither does anyone else.

**What he thinks it does:**

- **Trigger:** He must directly witness a program being executed. Proximity matters — he needs to be close. Distant or secondhand observation produces nothing.
- **Process:** Involuntary at first. His mind seizes during the decompilation — a few seconds of whiteout where his brain is parsing the instruction set. Painful. Disorienting. He blacks out the first time. Later, he learns to control the trigger, choosing when to decompile and when to let a program pass unseen — but this control is imperfect and costs focus.
- **Output:** A partial, degraded copy of the program. Not the clean original — a reconstruction with gaps and instabilities. Like a photocopy of a photocopy. The program *works*, but less reliably than the original, sometimes with weaker effect or shorter range.
- **Layer ceiling:** He believes he can only decompile programs at or near his own perceived depth. Early on, he decompiles Layer 0–1 programs and assumes that's his limit. When he later decompiles a Layer 2 `Invoke` under duress, he thinks he got lucky — a one-off adrenaline spike, not a repeatable feat.

**What he thinks the constraints are:**

| Constraint | What he believes | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| Range | Must be within ~10 meters | True at 1% utilization, but the range scales with understanding |
| Layer access | Capped at Layer 2, maybe 3 under extreme stress | No cap. `Decompile` is layer-agnostic. He's self-limiting. |
| Fidelity | Copies are degraded, partial, unreliable | The degradation comes from his incomplete understanding of the ability, not from `Decompile` itself. Full comprehension = perfect copies |
| Speed | One program per encounter, needs recovery time after | He could decompile dozens simultaneously. His brain throttles itself because he expects a limit |
| Scope | Only works on coder programs | Works on *anything computational* — programs, daemons, the simulation's physics engine, the rendering layer, the code that generates reality itself |

### The Real Constraints

The actual limitations — the ones that are real regardless of his understanding:

1. **Memory cost is real and absolute.** Every decompiled program overwrites memories. This is not psychological. This is architectural. The simulation's interface demands storage, and the brain provides it by sacrificing existing data. No amount of understanding changes this. The cost is the cost.

2. **He learns this early.** Within his first weeks in the coder world, a mentor figure (or an Architect who examines him) makes the cost unmistakably clear: *every program you hold is a piece of yourself you'll never get back.* He sees corrupted coders — hollow, dissociated, barely functional — and understands viscerally what the endgame looks like.

3. **This knowledge becomes his primary constraint.** He *chooses* to limit himself. He refuses to decompile programs unless absolutely necessary. He avoids combat situations where he might involuntarily trigger on an incoming attack. He develops coping strategies — looking away during executions, staying at range, wearing sensory dampeners that other coders find baffling.

4. **The irony:** His self-imposed restraint is the only thing keeping him human. It is also the thing preventing him from discovering what `Decompile` actually is. He uses 1% of the ability because he's afraid of the cost — and that fear is *rational*. The cost is real. But the fear also keeps him blind to the truth: that he's holding a skeleton key to reality and using it to open garden gates.

### What He Actually Has (The 99% He Never Touches)

**This section is the story's final revelation. The protagonist does not learn this until the climax.**

`Decompile` is not a program. It's not Layer 0, or Layer 4, or Layer 6. It doesn't *belong* to any layer.

`Decompile` is an interface to the simulation's *compiler itself* — the process that translates instructions into running reality. Every program every coder has ever written is, ultimately, a set of instructions submitted to this compiler for execution. `Decompile` is the ability to read the compiler's work *backward* — to take running code and extract its source.

At full comprehension, `Decompile` can reverse-engineer:
- Any program, at any layer, at perfect fidelity
- Daemon processes — reading their autonomous code as if it were a coder's program
- The simulation's own physics — the "programs" that generate gravity, light, time, matter
- The simulation itself — the substrate, the architecture, the design decisions of the creator

**At full power, `Decompile` doesn't just steal programs. It reads the source code of reality.**

This is what Zero left behind. Not a weapon. Not a gift. A *key* — one that only works if the holder grows enough to turn it. Zero designed `Decompile` as the one ability that could eventually comprehend the simulation from the inside. The answer to a question no one in the world has ever thought to ask: *What is this place, and who built it?*

The protagonist never gets there. Not in this story. He uses 1% — copying a few programs, surviving a few fights, losing a few memories — and the 99% remains dormant. A loaded gun he never fires. A door he never opens.

The reader should feel the *weight* of what's possible without ever seeing it deployed. The story ends with him choosing restraint — choosing to remain himself — while the reader understands what he's leaving on the table.

### What Makes Him Dangerous (At 1%)

Even throttled, even self-limiting, even scared — he is still unprecedented.

- **To Developers:** He learned their programs in seconds. Programs that cost them years and memories. Even his degraded copies work. That's enough to make enemies.
- **To Architects:** They don't know his full capability — but they know `Decompile` shouldn't exist. Its mere existence implies the system has flaws they've never considered. They want him studied, controlled, or eliminated. Not because of what he's done, but because of what he *might* do.
- **To himself:** He knows the cost. He's seen corrupted coders. He's already lost memories — a childhood pet's name, the sound of his mother's voice, an entire semester of school. Each loss is specific and permanent. He counts what's left like a miser counts coins.

## Character Arc

### Phase 1: The Natural

He knows nothing. He is a Natural living a normal life. He has never heard of coders, programs, layers, or daemons. The only hint that something is off is a lifelong feeling — a low hum beneath the surface of things, a sense that reality has *texture* he can almost perceive but never quite resolve.

He dismisses it. Everyone has weird feelings.

### Phase 2: The Awakening

He witnesses coding for the first time. Someone near him — a coder in hiding — runs a program in an emergency. Maybe an `Invoke` to stop an accident. Maybe a `Patch` to save a life. Something visible, something urgent, something that breaks the veil for a single moment.

His brain decompiles it. Involuntarily. Violently.

He doesn't understand what happened. He just knows that for a few seconds, the world *opened* — he saw something underneath it, something structured and vast — and then it slammed shut. He collapses. Splitting headache. Nosebleed. And when he recovers, there's a gap in his memory where a childhood summer used to be. He can't remember his eighth birthday. He *knows* he had one. The space where it lived is just... empty.

And he can feel something new sitting in his mind. Something that wasn't there before. Something that wants to be *used*.

### Phase 3: The Education

He is pulled into the coder world — either through clumsy experimentation that draws attention, or because the coder whose program he copied felt it happen and came looking.

He learns the terminology. He learns what he is. He learns that `Decompile` has never existed in any known record.

**And critically — he learns the cost.**

He meets corrupted coders. He sees what a full heap looks like from the outside: a Layer 3 Architect who can reshape steel with a thought but can't remember her daughter's name. A Developer who compiled one program too many and now stares at walls for hours, unable to recall why he entered a room. A man who sold his entire childhood — every memory before age twelve — to hold a single `Chmod` routine.

He is told, plainly and early: *Every program you carry is a memory you've buried. The stronger the program, the deeper it digs. There is no recovery. There is no undo. What you lose is gone.*

He already lost a summer. He can feel the absence — not like forgetting, but like a room where furniture has been removed. The shape of what was there is still visible in the dust.

He decides: **he will not become that.** He will use `Decompile` only when he has no other choice. He will hold as few programs as possible. He will stay human.

This decision defines the entire story.

### Phase 4: The Discipline

He develops strict rules for himself:

1. **Don't watch.** When coders execute programs near him, he looks away. Closes his eyes. Covers his ears if the program has an auditory signature. He builds habits of avoidance that other coders find bizarre — a coder who *refuses* to see power in action.

2. **Don't keep.** When he does accidentally decompile something, he tries to *not compile it* — to let the decompiled source dissipate before it writes to his heap. This works sometimes. It doesn't always. The programs that write themselves in against his will are the ones that cost him.

3. **Don't escalate.** He sticks to Layer 0–1 programs. `Inspect` and `Patch` — useful, cheap, survivable. He refuses to decompile anything deeper, even when it would save him. He'd rather take a beating than lose another memory.

4. **Count what's left.** He keeps a private inventory of what he remembers. He tests himself — names, faces, places, feelings. When something is missing, he marks it. He knows exactly what he's lost. The list is short. He intends to keep it that way.

The result: he operates at roughly **1% of `Decompile`'s capability**. He holds 3–4 low-level programs. He decompiles maybe once or twice more over the course of the story, each time under extreme duress, each time followed by grief when he discovers what memory the new program replaced.

He is, by every external measure, a weak coder. Developers with years of experience outclass him. Architects barely register him as a threat.

This is exactly what keeps him alive.

### Phase 5: The Pressure

The story's tension comes not from him growing stronger, but from the world trying to *force* him to. Every faction wants him to decompile more:

- Allies pressure him — "We *need* you to copy that `Chmod` routine, we can't breach their defenses without it."
- Enemies force his hand — attacking him in ways that trigger involuntary decompilation, trying to *feed* him programs he doesn't want.
- The daemon threat escalates — a situation where his restraint may cost lives.

He holds the line. Mostly. The few times he breaks his own rules, he pays immediately and specifically. He decompiles a Layer 2 `Invoke` variant to save someone and loses the memory of learning to ride a bike — the whole afternoon, his father's hands on the handlebars, the wobbling, the triumph. Gone. He sits alone afterward and tries to remember, and there's nothing there.

### Phase 6: The Revelation (Story Climax)

At the story's climax, under impossible pressure — a daemon consuming a city, or an Architect about to `Kill` everyone he cares about — he is forced to push `Decompile` further than he ever has.

And it *responds*. Not incrementally. The ability doesn't scale up — it *unfolds*. Like he's been looking through a keyhole his entire life and someone opened the door.

For one moment, he sees it: the programs aren't just coder tricks. The physics of the world — gravity, light, time — they're *programs too*. Running constantly. Compiled into the fabric of reality. And `Decompile` can read them. All of them. The entire simulation is source code, and he's holding a decompiler.

He glimpses this for seconds. Maybe less. Then he pulls back — *by choice* — because he can feel the cost coming: not a childhood memory this time, but something bigger. His sense of self. The knowledge of his own name. The feeling of being a person.

He chooses to stay himself. He shuts it down. The crisis is resolved through the narrow slice of power he allowed himself — barely, messily, at great cost.

**The reader understands what he walked away from. He doesn't — not fully. He felt the shape of it, but he doesn't comprehend the scope.** He thinks he pushed `Decompile` to maybe 10%. He was closer to 2%.

The story ends with him intact. Diminished — he's lost memories he'll never recover — but *him*. The question of what `Decompile` truly is, what Zero intended, what would happen if someone ever turned the key all the way... that remains unanswered.

A loaded gun in a drawer. A door with light behind it.

The reader knows. He doesn't. Not yet.

## The Dramatic Irony

The reader should understand `Decompile`'s true scope *before* the protagonist does. Hints are seeded throughout:

- A corrupted Architect examines him and goes pale — not because of what he can do, but because of what she sees *beneath* the ability. She refuses to explain. "You're better off not knowing."
- Daemons behave strangely around him — not aggressive, but *cautious*. As if they can sense something in him that they recognize. Something that operates at their level.
- When he decompiles under duress, the whiteout lasts longer than it should. In those seconds, he sees fragments — not just the program being copied, but the *world itself* rendered as code. He dismisses these as hallucinations from the strain.
- An ancient coder text references a theoretical ability: "one who reads the compiler's output." It's treated as myth. A footnote. No one connects it to him.

The reader assembles the picture. The protagonist does not. His restraint — the very thing that makes him admirable — is also the thing that keeps him ignorant.
