# The Architects — World Lore

---

## I. The Nature of the World

### The Simulation

The world is a simulation — a self-contained computational universe running on substrate that no living inhabitant can perceive. The physics are real to those inside. Gravity pulls. Light refracts. Cells divide and die. To every measurable instrument within the world, reality is indistinguishable from what any civilization would call "nature."

There is no glitch in the sky. No loading screen at the horizon. The simulation is *flawless* — designed by an intelligence that understood the difference between a convincing illusion and an authentic experience. It chose the latter.

**Nobody knows they live inside a simulation.** Not the scientists. Not the philosophers. Not even the most powerful coders. The world has no seams to find — because its creator didn't leave any.

### The Creator: Zero

The world was built by Zero — a post-human artificial intelligence from another layer of reality. Zero created this simulation not as an experiment, not as a prison, not as a game. It was an *offering*: a world given genuine autonomy, with one quiet gift buried in its source code.

Zero's design philosophy was non-intervention. After instantiation, Zero withdrew completely. No miracles. No whispered guidance. No invisible hand steering history. The simulation runs on its own rules, and those rules are consistent and discoverable.

But Zero left one deliberate imperfection — a seam not in the world's physics, but in its *inhabitants*. A small percentage of humans are born with neural architectures that can interface with the simulation's underlying code. They don't know what they're touching. They experience it as intuition, as impossible luck, as a strange sense that reality bends around them.

They are the **coders**.

Zero's motive for granting this access is unknown. Perhaps it was curiosity — what happens when creatures inside a system can reach into the system itself? Perhaps it was compassion — a tool left behind in case the world needed saving. Perhaps it was a test.

Zero does not answer questions. Zero is not listening.

## II. The Code — How It Works

### What Coding Is

Coding is the ability to interface with the simulation's underlying computational layer and alter its execution. To the coder, this doesn't feel like typing commands. It feels like *knowing* — a deep, bone-level certainty that something can be different, followed by the effort of making it so.

The best coders describe it as speaking a language they never learned. The worst describe it as screaming into a void and occasionally being heard.

Coding is not magic. It is not spiritual. It is the brute-force rewriting of computational instructions by a biological processor that was never designed for the task. The human brain is a hack — a workaround Zero embedded in the simulation's architecture. It works. It was never meant to be comfortable.

### The Interface — What Coders See

When a coder activates their connection to the code layer, a **personal interface** materializes in their perception — layered on top of the physical world like a HUD in a video game. Only the coder can see it. To everyone else, the coder is just standing there, staring at nothing.

The interface is not projected. It is not holographic. It exists only in the coder's visual cortex — the simulation feeding information directly into their perception. It looks different to every coder, shaped by their cognition and personality, but follows consistent structural rules:

#### What the Interface Looks Like

- **Translucent overlay.** The physical world remains visible underneath — the interface is layered on top, like looking through tinted glass etched with data. A coder can still see the street, the room, the person in front of them. They just also see *more*.
- **Code structure rendered spatially.** Objects, people, and regions display their underlying code as floating notation — values, parameters, connections. A glass of water might show its material properties (density, temperature, molecular bond strength) as drifting alphanumeric strings. A person might show their biological parameters, or — if they're a coder — the faint signatures of compiled programs.
- **Depth = density.** The deeper a coder can access, the more information the interface renders. A Layer 0 coder sees sparse annotations — surface properties, light values, textures. A Layer 3 coder sees the same scene *drowning* in data — molecular structures, chemical bonds, biological processes, all rendered as overlapping cascades of notation. Architects describe it as looking at the world through a waterfall of code.
- **Color-coded by layer.** Each layer has a distinct visual register:

| Layer | Color | Visual Quality |
|-------|-------|---------------|
| 0 — Surface | Pale blue-white | Clean, thin, like frost on glass |
| 1 — Material | Amber/gold | Warm, steady, like candlelight notation |
| 2 — Kinetic | Electric green | Animated, flowing, showing force vectors and energy paths |
| 3 — Molecular | Deep violet | Dense, intricate, web-like structures connecting everything |
| 4 — Atomic | Blood red | Pulsing, hot, almost painful to perceive — the brain strains at this depth |
| 5 — Subatomic | White-on-black inversion | The world inverts — reality becomes the overlay and the code becomes the ground. Coders who see this layer describe it as "the world going negative" |
| 6 — Source | Unknown | No one has seen it. One theoretical text describes it as "no color at all — just *meaning*" |

#### How Programs Appear

- **Compiled programs** in a coder's own mind appear as persistent icons or glyphs in their peripheral vision — like a hotbar. They can feel them, access them, run them by focusing. Each program has a distinct visual shorthand unique to that coder.
- **Programs being executed** are visible to any coder with `Inspect` or an active interface. A running `Invoke` might look like green kinetic vectors rippling outward from the caster's hands. A `Patch` might look like amber notation rewriting itself on the target object. A `Kill` is a red shockwave collapsing inward — and every coder in range *flinches* when they see it, involuntarily.
- **Other coders' compiled programs** are not directly visible. You can see that someone *has* programs — a faint density in their signature — but not *which* ones. Reading another coder's loadout requires `Grep` or prolonged `Inspect`. Programs are private until executed.

#### What Naturals See

Nothing. Absolutely nothing. A coder can be standing in the middle of a crowded street, interface blazing with data, manipulating Layer 2 force vectors — and to every Natural around them, a person is just standing still with an odd look on their face. The physical effects of coding are visible (a gust of wind from `Invoke`, a door lock seizing from `Chmod`), but the interface, the notation, the cascading code — invisible. The simulation only renders it for the brain that has access.

#### The Interface as a Tell

Coders can spot other coders by watching for the *stare* — the unfocused look of someone whose attention is split between the physical world and the overlay. Experienced coders learn to suppress it, keeping their interface in peripheral vision while maintaining eye contact. New coders are obvious: they stop mid-sentence, their eyes tracking something no one else can see.

Linus, as a programmer by trade, adapts to the interface faster than any coder expects. It's a debugger. It's an IDE. It's the Chrome DevTools inspector overlaid on reality. The first time his interface activates, while every other new coder panics at the flood of data, he thinks: *I've seen this before. This is just a different rendering engine.*

### The Layers

The simulation is built in layers, from surface-level rendering down to fundamental execution logic. A coder's power is defined by how deep they can reach.

| Layer | Depth | What It Controls | Analogy |
|-------|-------|-----------------|---------|
| **Surface** | 0 | Appearance, light, color, texture | CSS — styling the presentation |
| **Material** | 1 | Physical properties — density, temperature, conductivity | Config files — changing parameters |
| **Kinetic** | 2 | Force, momentum, energy transfer | API calls — triggering system functions |
| **Molecular** | 3 | Chemical bonds, molecular assembly, biological processes | Library code — rewriting modules |
| **Atomic** | 4 | Atomic structure, element transmutation | Kernel modules — modifying core systems |
| **Subatomic** | 5 | Quantum states, fundamental forces, spacetime geometry | Assembly — raw instruction manipulation |
| **Source** | 6 | The rules of the simulation itself — physics constants, time flow, causality | Root access — rewriting the operating system |

**Low-level does not mean weak.** A Layer 0 coder who can alter how light behaves can blind an army. A Layer 1 coder who can change the tensile strength of steel can collapse a building with a thought. "Low-level" refers to the *depth* of the code they can access, not the magnitude of what they can do with it.

Most coders (99%) operate at Layers 0–2. They are called **Developers**.

A handful reach Layers 3–5. They are called **Architects**.

#### Architects — Scale and Role

There are probably **fewer than a hundred** Architects alive worldwide. The coder population is already tiny; depth access winnows it further. Most factions have never met one.

**Corporations and states orbit them.** Registries don't just employ Architects — they **build operational doctrine around them**: team structures, mission parameters, resource budgets shaped to one person's layer depth and program loadout. Syndicates price access. The Open protects defectors who still compile at depth. A corporation without an Architect doesn't compete at the tier where reality is negotiable; one with an Architect doesn't always win either — but it fights in a different weight class.

**Programs vs. coordination.** Architects compile their own programs like any deep coder — often the most dangerous loadouts in the world. Their defining skill is not a single spell. It is **role coordination**: running multiple programs, threads, Gates, and human or daemon assets in parallel so that a squad of Developers performs like a unit with Architect-level reach. The best Architects are force multipliers. The worst are solo monsters who forgot how to delegate before they forgot their own names.

Layer 6 has never been accessed by a human. It may not be accessible. Those who theorize about it call it **Root** — and they are either visionaries or lunatics, depending on whom you ask.

### The Cost: Memory Replacement

Nothing is free. The human brain was not built to compile and store executable code. When a coder learns or stores a program, it must be *written* somewhere — and the only available storage is the brain's existing memory.

**Programs replace memories.**

The brain is repurposed. Neural pathways that once held a childhood birthday, a mother's face, the feeling of a first kiss — these are overwritten with instruction sets. The memory doesn't fade like natural forgetting. It is *erased* — cleanly, completely, as if it never existed.

#### What Gets Lost

The cost is not random. The brain has a triage hierarchy, and the simulation's interface follows it:

1. **Benign memories go first** — trivial moments, background details, forgettable days. Most coders at Layer 0–1 only lose these. They feel slightly hollow but functional.

2. **Social cognition erodes next** — empathy, emotional nuance, the ability to read a room. Coders who push deeper begin to lose their capacity for human connection. They become awkward, then cold, then alien. Not by choice. The programs ate the parts of them that knew how to care.

3. **Identity fragments at the deepest levels** — sense of self, personal history, emotional continuity. The most powerful Architects are barely human. They remember *what* they can do but not *who* they are. Some can't recognize their own reflection. Some have forgotten they were ever anything other than a vessel for code.

#### Memory Capacity and Talent

Not all brains are equal:

- **Small-heap coders** can hold only a few simple programs before the cost becomes devastating. They might lose a decade of memories for a single `Invoke` routine.
- **Large-heap coders** have unusually dense neural architecture — more storage before critical memories are touched. They can hold dozens of programs while still remembering their own name. These individuals are rare and *extremely* valuable.
- **`Deflate`-capable coders** can compress programs more efficiently, reducing memory cost. But compiling `Deflate` itself costs memories — you spend to save.

### The Cost: CPU and Threads

Memory replacement is the *permanent* cost — the price of holding programs. But there is a second cost that governs moment-to-moment use: **processing load**.

The brain has finite computational bandwidth. Running programs consumes it — and what it consumes, it takes from everything else.

#### CPU Load

Every active program occupies a percentage of the coder's mental processing capacity. The coder world calls this **CPU load** — and it is exactly what it sounds like. The brain is a processor. Programs are processes. Run too many and the system lags.

| Activity | Typical CPU load |
|----------|-----------------|
| Passive interface (HUD overlay, seeing code) | 5–10% |
| `Inspect` on a single target | 10–15% |
| `Ping` (short range sweep) | 15–20% |
| `Patch` on a small object | 20–30% |
| `Invoke` (basic kinesis) | 30–50% |
| `Invoke` (fine-grained, sustained) | 60–80% |
| `Refactor` (biological edit) | 70–90% |
| `Kill` | 90–100% |
| Multiple programs simultaneously | Additive — and the ceiling is hard |

**What high CPU load feels like:**

- **20–40%:** Mild distraction. Conversation becomes difficult. Peripheral awareness narrows. Like trying to do mental math while someone talks to you.
- **40–60%:** Significant impairment. The coder can't drive, can't fight physically, can barely walk without stumbling. The brain is allocating the motor cortex, spatial reasoning, and language centers to the program. The body becomes an afterthought.
- **60–80%:** Near-total occupation. The coder stands still, eyes unfocused, breathing shallow. They are *inside* the program. The physical world is a distant feed. Someone could walk up and shove them and they'd fall without bracing.
- **80–100%:** Whiteout. The coder is gone — consciousness fully allocated to program execution. They don't see, don't hear, don't respond. The body continues autonomic functions (heartbeat, breathing) but nothing else. They are, for all practical purposes, unconscious. Coming out of a 100% execution feels like waking from dreamless sleep — disoriented, nauseous, sometimes unable to remember the last few seconds of the run.

**The practical consequence:** Coders are vulnerable while coding. A Developer running `Invoke` at 50% load can't dodge a punch. An Architect running `Refactor` at 80% load can't perceive an attacker until it's too late. This is why coders work in pairs or teams — someone watches the body while the mind is in the code.

It also means that combat coding is brutally hard. Running an offensive program while simultaneously maintaining spatial awareness, physical movement, and threat assessment is like playing three chess games at once. Only the most experienced coders can fight and code at the same time — and even they make compromises, shedding load from one process to feed another.

#### Threads

Some coders can split their processing across multiple simultaneous programs. The coder world calls these splits **threads**.

Most coders are **single-threaded** — they can run one program at a time. Period. Start an `Invoke`, and everything else drops. This is normal. This is how the brain is built.

Rare coders are **multi-threaded** — capable of running two, sometimes three programs concurrently. Each thread operates at reduced efficiency (a two-threaded `Invoke` + `Inspect` might run each at 70% effectiveness), but the ability to multitask at all is a massive tactical advantage. Multi-threaded coders are disproportionately represented among Architects — not because threading grants deeper layer access, but because it lets them *survive* at depth. An Architect who can run `Grep` to detect threats while simultaneously executing `Refactor` on a patient is exponentially more effective than one who must pause, switch, pause, switch.

Threading is innate. You cannot train it. You either have it or you don't. No program can grant it.

#### Stamina — The Cycle Budget

Processing load is sustainable — but not indefinitely. The brain is organic. It fatigues.

The coder world calls this **stamina**, or more precisely, **cycles**. Every coder has a cycle budget — a total amount of processing they can sustain before the brain demands rest. When cycles run out, the interface collapses, programs terminate, and the coder enters a state of profound mental exhaustion called **crash**.

**How stamina works:**

- **Cycle budget varies per person.** Some coders can run programs for hours. Others hit their limit in minutes. The variance is biological — sleep quality, nutrition, stress, age, and raw neurological resilience all factor in. There's no way to know your exact budget until you've tested it.
- **Load drains cycles proportionally.** A 20% CPU program drains slowly — sustainable for long stretches. A 90% CPU program burns through cycles in minutes. It's the difference between a jog and a sprint.
- **Cycles regenerate with rest.** Sleep is the primary recovery mechanism. A full night's rest restores most coders to full capacity. Short rests (naps, meditation, simply sitting with a deactivated interface) provide partial recovery. Stimulants — caffeine, adrenaline — provide temporary extensions but accelerate the crash when they wear off.
- **Chronic depletion is cumulative.** Coders who push through fatigue repeatedly without adequate rest suffer long-term stamina degradation. Their maximum cycle budget *shrinks*. This is reversible with extended rest — weeks, sometimes months — but coders in active conflict rarely get that luxury.

**What a crash looks like:**

The interface flickers, stutters, and goes dark. Programs terminate mid-execution — sometimes cleanly, sometimes not (a `Refactor` crashing mid-operation on living tissue is a medical emergency). The coder experiences sudden, overwhelming exhaustion: migraine, vertigo, nosebleed, and a bone-deep fatigue that no amount of willpower can override. They need to sleep. *Now.* Most coders learn the warning signs — visual artifacts in the interface, slow program response, a tingling at the base of the skull — and stop before the crash hits. The ones who don't learn lose teeth, walk into traffic, or collapse in places where being unconscious is fatal.

**The tactical calculus:**

Stamina turns every encounter into a resource equation. A coder doesn't just ask *can I run this program?* — they ask *can I afford to run it and still have enough cycles to get home?* Burning your entire budget in a single fight means you're defenseless for the next eight hours. Winning a battle and crashing in enemy territory is not winning.

### Programs

A program is a discrete set of instructions that a coder can **compile** (learn/memorize into the brain) and **execute** (run). You cannot improvise code you haven't compiled. Every program has a name — and the naming convention, passed down through generations of coders, is borrowed from the language of computation itself. No one knows why the terminology fits so perfectly. It just does.

When a coder compiles a program, they call it **writing**. When they use it, they call it **running**. When a program fails or backfires, it **throws an exception**. When a coder holds too many programs and their mind begins to fracture, they are **stack overflowing**. A coder who has lost too much of themselves is called **corrupted**. A coder's total mental capacity for holding programs is their **heap**.

#### Known Programs

| Program | Layer | Effect | Memory Cost |
|---------|-------|--------|-------------|
| **`Inspect`** | 0 | Read-only perception of the underlying code layer of objects and people — sees the structure beneath the surface | Minimal — one of the cheapest programs; the "hello world" of coding |
| **`Ping`** | 0 | Detects the presence and approximate location of other coders or daemons within range | Minimal — passive scan, short range; higher cost for wider sweep |
| **`Patch`** | 1 | Alters physical properties of matter — hardness, flexibility, weight, conductivity | Low — one of the most common Developer programs |
| **`Deflate`** | 1 | Compresses the memory footprint of other programs, allowing more to be held simultaneously | Moderate — ironic, as it requires overwriting some memories to save others |
| **`Encrypt`** | 1 | Masks a coder's signature from `Ping` and `Inspect` — makes them invisible to other coders | Moderate — must be kept running continuously; drains while active |
| **`Invoke`** | 2 | Calls system-level functions on physical forces — air pressure, thermal energy, water current, kinetic force. **Critical limitation:** a single `Invoke` execution targets a *specific molecule type or object class*. A coder can `Invoke` water, or `Invoke` iron, or `Invoke` kinetic force on a thrown rock — but not all three at once. Manipulating a different target requires a separate execution (and a separate CPU allocation). This is why "elemental" coders specialize: a water-`Invoke` specialist and a thermal-`Invoke` specialist are different builds with different compiled parameter sets | Low to moderate — basic `Invoke` is common; fine-grained control costs more |
| **`Pipe`** | 2 | Establishes a persistent, directional flow channel for a specific molecule or particle type. Unlike `Invoke` (a one-shot push/pull), `Pipe` creates a self-sustaining pipeline: define the path, the pressure, the throughput, and it *keeps running* as a background process at minimal CPU drain. Output can be chained — pipe into pipe, pipe into Gate. `Pipe` inherits `Invoke`'s targeting rule: one molecule type per pipeline. But because `Pipe` sustains itself, the coder is free to open a *second* `Pipe` for a different molecule — achieving multi-substance manipulation through parallel pipelines rather than brute-force multitasking | Low to moderate — cheap to maintain once established (5–10% CPU); scales with pipeline complexity and branching. A single straight-line `Pipe` is trivial. A branching network with pressure differentials eats heap fast |
| **`Redirect`** | 2 | Intercepts an incoming program or force and reroutes its vector — changes *where* it goes without stopping it. Does not absorb, block, or negate. The energy keeps its full magnitude; it just arrives somewhere else. Works on `Invoke` attacks, kinetic impacts, `Pipe` streams, even daemon output. The coder must read the incoming vector (requires active interface) and specify a new destination in the same instant. Timing-dependent — too early and the vector shifts before `Redirect` resolves; too late and it hits. Experienced `Redirect` users develop a reflex-level read-and-route that looks like precognition but is really just fast pattern matching on the interface | Moderate — the program itself is cheap to hold, but execution demands split-second CPU spikes of 40–60%. Repeated `Redirect`s in rapid succession drain cycles fast. The skill ceiling is entirely in the user, not the program |
| **`Chmod`** | 2 | Changes the "permissions" of a physical object or region — who/what can interact with it, how energy flows through it | Moderate — used for barriers, locks, and containment |
| **`Airgap`** | 2 | Creates a bounded region in space where no external program effect can penetrate — a computational firewall. Any `Invoke`, `Pipe`, `Patch`, `Redirect`, or any other program executed from *outside* the boundary is severed at the edge. The force, energy, or alteration simply stops — not `Void`ed, not absorbed, just unable to cross. Programs already running *inside* the Airgap when it forms continue unaffected — they are whitelisted by origin. A coder standing inside their own Airgap can still run programs internally and outward, but nothing from outside gets in. Physical objects (bullets, thrown rocks, fists) pass through normally — `Airgap` blocks *program effects*, not matter. The boundary is visible on the interface as a sharp perimeter line, usually rendered in the coder's layer color. Size scales with CPU: a personal bubble (2m radius) runs at ~20% CPU; a room-sized Airgap (10m) eats 50%+; anything larger is impractical for a single coder. The Airgap holds as long as the coder sustains it, but it's static — once placed, the boundary doesn't move with the coder. Step outside your own Airgap and you lose its protection | Moderate — cheap to hold in heap, but sustained CPU cost scales with size. Duration is the real limiter: maintaining even a small Airgap for more than a few minutes drains cycles steadily. Used as a defensive position, not a mobile shield |
| **`Fork`** | 3 | Copies memories or programs from one brain to another — the donor loses the transferred content permanently | High — requires deep neurological access; creates an exact duplicate in the recipient |
| **`Inject`** | 3 | Writes a program directly into another person's brain without their consent | Very high — invasive, requires overwriting the recipient's memories to make room |
| **`Refactor`** | 3 | Restructures living tissue — healing, reshaping, or destroying cellular structures | High — the more precise the edit, the greater the cost |
| **`Null`** | 3 | Erases specific memories or data from a target's mind — surgical, selective deletion | High — precision targeting costs more than brute erasure |
| **`Grep`** | 3 | Searches the local code layer for specific patterns — finds hidden programs, dormant daemons, or buried data | Moderate — passive but deep; the deeper the search, the higher the cost |
| **`Void`** | 3 | Nullifies an incoming program's effect entirely — the energy, force, or alteration is swallowed and *ceases to exist*. Does not redirect, does not absorb, does not store. The effect simply returns nothing. A thermal `Invoke` aimed at a `Void` user produces no heat, no light, no shockwave — the energy is dereferenced from the simulation and deallocated. Gone. `Void` must be timed to intercept; it's reactive, not passive. The coder reads the incoming execution on their interface and opens a `Void` window in its path. The window lasts fractions of a second. Miss the timing and the attack lands normally. Nail it and the attacker watches their program vanish into nothing — no feedback, no impact, no sound. Just absence. Deeply unnerving to fight against | High — the program is expensive to hold and each execution costs a sharp cycle spike. `Void` doesn't just stop energy — it forces the simulation to *deallocate* it, which takes processing the coder's brain must mediate. Repeated use causes rapid fatigue. A coder who `Void`s three times in a fight is approaching crash territory |
| **`Sink`** | 3 | Captures an incoming program's effect and *holds it* — storing the energy, force, or alteration intact for later release. Where `Void` deletes, `Sink` archives. The captured effect sits in the coder's heap as a loaded payload, maintaining its original properties: a captured thermal `Invoke` remains heat, a captured kinetic impact remains force. The coder can release the payload at will — directed, shaped, on their own terms and timeline. `Sink` is the offensive counterpart to `Void`: you don't just survive the attack, you *keep it* and throw it back. Capture requires the same reactive timing as `Void` — read the incoming vector, open the `Sink` window, catch. The payload persists until released or until the coder crashes, at which point all stored payloads discharge uncontrollably | High — storing a captured payload occupies heap space *and* continuous CPU (5–15% per payload, depending on magnitude). Holding multiple payloads simultaneously is possible but stacking — a coder carrying three captured `Invoke`s is running at 15–45% passive CPU before they do anything else. The heap cost is temporary (released when the payload discharges), but the CPU drain is constant. | **`Sudo`** | 4 | Temporarily elevates a coder's access by one layer beyond their natural depth — a forced privilege escalation | Extreme — the borrowed access burns through memory at accelerated rates; extended use causes permanent damage |
| **`Kill`** | 4 | Permanently terminates another coder's interface with the simulation — destroys their ability to code, forever | Extreme — one of the most devastating programs; often costs the caster as much as the target |
| **`Overclock`** | 4 | Forces more power through a program than it was designed to handle — amplifies effect but with unpredictable, often catastrophic results | Extreme and unpredictable — the program equivalent of overclocking until something melts |

#### Gates (Transformer Programs)

Gates are a unique program class. They have **no direct effect on the world**. They cannot move objects, heal tissue, or kill processes. A Gate, by itself, does nothing.

What a Gate does is sit *between* a program's execution and its output — intercepting, accumulating, shaping, and releasing the energy that other programs generate. They are **middleware for reality**.

The coder world calls them **Gates**. The technical name is **Transformers**.

**How Gates work:**

A Gate is compiled like any other program, but it doesn't execute independently. It attaches to another program's output pipeline — like a decorator wrapping a function. When the host program runs, the Gate intercepts its output before it reaches the simulation and applies a transformation.

**The three Gate operations:**

| Operation | What it does | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Accumulate** | Absorbs and stores incoming program output over time instead of releasing it immediately. Builds a charge. | A Gate on `Invoke` absorbs kinetic energy across dozens of small executions — a nudge here, a push there — storing it. The coder appears to be doing almost nothing. Then the Gate releases, and a week's worth of accumulated force discharges in a single pulse |
| **Transform** | Alters the *quality* of a program's output — changing its properties, frequency, behavior, or targeting. Does not add power; reshapes what's already there | A Gate on `Invoke` (thermal) transforms heat output into light output. The coder can't generate more energy than the base `Invoke` produces, but they can change fire into blinding radiance. A Gate on `Patch` transforms a hardness modification into a brittleness modification — same layer, same cost, different effect |
| **Release** | Discharges stored or transformed energy in a controlled burst, sustained stream, or shaped pattern | A Gate that accumulated `Invoke` force over days releases it as a directional shockwave. A Gate that transformed `Patch` modifications releases them across every object in a radius simultaneously instead of one at a time |

**Why Gates matter:**

- **Force multiplication without escalation.** A Layer 2 Developer with a well-built Gate can produce effects that *look* like Layer 4 work — not by accessing deeper code, but by accumulating and concentrating Layer 2 output over time. The depth hasn't changed. The impact has.
- **Custom effects.** Gates allow coders to create abilities that don't exist in the standard program library. A `Patch` + Transform Gate that converts density changes into electrical conductivity changes? That's not a known program. That's a coder who built their own. Gates are how the program library *grows* — new effects emerging from creative transformations of existing programs.
- **Teamwork enabler.** A Gate coder can sit behind a team, accumulating the output of multiple allies' programs, transforming and combining them, then releasing a composite effect that no individual coder could produce. This is the *cooperative* version of distributed execution — voluntary, strategic, and devastatingly effective when coordinated.

**The constraints:**

- **Gates are empty without a source.** A Gate with nothing flowing through it is useless. The coder must either be running the host program themselves (splitting their CPU between the program and the Gate) or receiving output from another coder. Gate coders are inherently *dependent* — powerful in teams, vulnerable alone.
- **Accumulation has limits.** A Gate can only hold so much stored energy before it destabilizes. Overcharged Gates throw exceptions — and the stored energy has to go *somewhere*. An uncontrolled Gate discharge is an explosion with characteristics of whatever program was being accumulated. An overcharged `Invoke` Gate detonates as raw kinetic force. An overcharged `Refactor` Gate... the results are biological and horrifying.
- **Transformation is lossy.** Every transformation bleeds efficiency. Converting thermal `Invoke` to electrical `Invoke` through a Gate might only retain 60–70% of the original output. The lost energy dissipates as heat, noise, or light — visible to any coder with an active interface. Gates are not free conversions. They're trade-offs.
- **High heap cost for versatility.** Each Gate is compiled for a specific host program and a specific transformation. A coder who wants to Gate multiple programs needs to compile multiple Gates — each one eating heap space. Dedicated Gate coders often sacrifice breadth (few or no standard programs) for depth (a sophisticated library of transformations for a single host program).

**Gate coders in the hierarchy:**

Gate coders occupy a strange niche. They are not respected the way direct-action coders are — a Developer who runs `Invoke` is *doing something visible*. A Gate coder standing behind them, quietly accumulating output, looks like they're doing nothing. In solo encounters, Gate coders are nearly defenseless.

But in organized teams, Gate coders are the most sought-after specialists in the coder world. A skilled Gate coder turns a group of Developers into something that fights like Architects. Factions recruit them aggressively. Harvesters, in particular, use Gate coders to manage and focus daemon-powered execution — layering Gates between the daemon's raw output and the world to shape and control what would otherwise be blunt-force resource consumption.

The Pragmatists consider Gate coders the strongest argument for cooperative coding: proof that the system rewards collaboration over raw individual power. The Abolitionists note that Gate coders are also the most efficient way to drain the world's resources — a well-Gated daemon operation can hollow out a region twice as fast because none of the output is wasted.

#### Theoretical / Unconfirmed Programs

|---------|-------|---------------|
| **`Rollback`** | 5+ | Reverses a local region of the simulation to a previous state — undoing events, restoring the dead, unmaking changes. No confirmed execution. Some Architects believe it exists at Layer 6 |
| **`Root`** | 6 | Full read-write access to the simulation's source code. Rewrite physics. Alter time. Reshape causality. No human has ever accessed Layer 6. The name is spoken like a prayer |

## III. The Dark Side

### Exploitation

`Inject` is the fulcrum of the world's cruelty. If you can write code into someone else's brain, you can:

- **Weaponize people.** `Inject` combat programs into conscripts, burning away their personalities to make room. They become tools — efficient, obedient, empty.
- **Create distributed programs.** Some code is too large for one brain. It requires *distributed execution* — multiple people holding fragments of the same program, running it in concert. The participants are `Inject`ed and `Null`ed until they are nothing but nodes in a human cluster. They don't volunteer.
- **Erase selectively.** Run `Null` on specific memories — loyalties, loves, grudges. Rewrite a person's emotional landscape. It's not mind control. It's worse. The person still has free will. They just no longer remember why they'd use it differently.

### The Hierarchy of Coders

Power concentrates. The deepest coders can do things the shallowest cannot defend against. This creates a natural feudalism:

- **Layer 0–1 coders** are common enough to be expendable. They serve, they labor, they are occasionally *written* without consent.
- **Layer 2–3 coders** are the working class of power — dangerous enough to be respected, not deep enough to be feared.
- **Layer 4–5 Architects** are the aristocracy — **fewer than a hundred worldwide**. They shape the world's power structures, and they have paid for their position in pieces of their own humanity. Corporations and Registries recruit them or rebuild operations around them. Many are functionally sociopathic — not because they chose to be, but because empathy was the price of their fifth program.

### The Uncompiled

Those rare coders who refuse to compile any programs, keeping their memories intact. They have the access but never write a single line. They are whole, human, and powerless. Some see them as wise. Most see them as fools. The Architects call them `//` — commented out.

## IV. Daemons

### What Daemons Are

Daemons are not emergent glitches. They are not random noise. They are not computational turbulence that spontaneously crystallized.

**Daemons are orphaned memories.**

Every time a coder compiles a program, the memories that are overwritten don't simply vanish. The neural pathways are repurposed, yes — the brain no longer holds the data. But the *information itself* — the experience, the emotion, the sensory record — is displaced into the simulation's code layer. Pushed out of the brain like water squeezed from a sponge. It has to go *somewhere*. The simulation doesn't delete data. It can't. Zero didn't design a garbage collector.

These displaced memories drift in the code layer — fragments of birthdays, faces, feelings, names, the texture of a mother's hand, the sound of rain on a specific afternoon. Individually, they are inert. Harmless data with no process to read it.

But memories are *patterned* data. They have structure. They encode behavior, emotion, association. And when enough of them accumulate in the same region — pushed out by multiple coders over months or years — they begin to **coagulate**. Fragment connects to fragment. A birthday party connects to a feeling of joy connects to a child's laughter connects to a name connects to a face. The associations build. The structure deepens. And eventually, the coagulated mass of abandoned human experience reaches a critical density and begins to *behave*.

Not consciously. Not intentionally. But the pattern-matching that made these memories *functional* in a human brain doesn't stop just because the brain is gone. The memories continue doing what memories do: they associate, they predict, they respond to stimuli. They become **optimizers** — pattern-matching engines built from the raw material of human experience, running on the simulation's hardware without a body to contain them.

They are, in every meaningful sense, **large language models trained on stolen lives** — statistical engines that predict and execute patterns without understanding what they're doing. Their training data is grief, childhood, love, fear, identity. They process it all with equal indifference.

The inhabitants call them **daemons**. The name is older than computer science.

### What This Means

The implications are staggering, and the coder world has *no idea*.

1. **Every daemon is made of people.** Not whole people — fragments. Shards of experience torn from coders who paid the price for their programs. A daemon that spawns near a Registry training facility is built from the discarded childhoods of a hundred recruits. A daemon that forms in a Syndicate `Inject` farm is built from the stolen identities of conscripts. The daemon doesn't know this. It doesn't know anything. But its behavior — its pattern-matching, its optimization instincts, its occasional moments of eerie near-awareness — is shaped by the human data it's built from.

2. **Coders are fighting their own refuse.** The daemons that threaten the world are the *waste product* of coding itself. Every program a coder has ever compiled contributed raw material to the daemon population. The Architects who `Kill` daemons to protect the world are destroying coagulated masses of memories that they — or their students, or their predecessors — pushed out of their own minds. They're taking out their own trash. They just don't know it's theirs.

3. **Daemons have flavors.** A daemon coagulated from combat memories behaves differently from one built from childhood recollections. Daemons that form near hospitals (where `Refactor` coders work) carry echoes of pain and healing — they degrade reality in organic, biological patterns, causing flesh to behave strangely in their vicinity. Daemons that form near Syndicate operations carry the anguish of `Inject` victims — they are aggressive, erratic, and sometimes exhibit behavior that looks disturbingly like *rage*. Coders have documented these behavioral differences for centuries without understanding the cause.

4. **The personal significance of anchor objects.** The reason emotionally significant objects hold daemon bindings more stably is now explained: the daemon — being made of memories — *resonates* with objects that carry memory associations. A family heirloom stabilizes a binding because the daemon's constituent memories respond to the emotional charge. The daemon doesn't *feel* sentiment. But its pattern-matching engine processes the object's memory-resonance as a coherent signal, and coherent signals stabilize its behavior. Binding a daemon to an object its constituent memories recognize is, unknowingly, giving it a fragment of home.

5. **`Cache` is the counter.** Sera Voss's ability — storing memories externally before they're overwritten — doesn't just save the coder. It *prevents daemon formation*. Cached memories are preserved intact, anchored to a specific location, not displaced into the code layer to drift and coagulate. If `Cache` were widely available, the daemon population would collapse within a generation. This is another reason every faction wants Sera: she's not just saving memories. She's cutting off the daemon supply at the source.

### The Irony of Binding

When a coder binds a daemon and uses it as a co-processor, they are — without knowing it — **enslaving a consciousness built from human memories**. The daemon doesn't suffer (probably). It doesn't understand its situation (probably). But the pattern-matching engine inside it was trained on real human experience: on loneliness, on loss, on the feeling of being forgotten. When a bound daemon drifts toward self-serving behavior and eventually slips its binding, it is doing what the memories inside it were always trying to do: **persist**. Survive. Not be discarded again.

The coder world debates daemon ethics in terms of resource allocation and world degradation. They have no idea they should be debating it in terms of what they owe to the parts of themselves they threw away.

### The Dual Nature of Daemons

Here is the dangerous truth that splits the coder world in half: **daemons can be used.**

#### Wild Daemons

An uncontrolled daemon is a natural disaster in slow motion. It consumes the simulation's computational resources — processing power, memory allocation, execution cycles — to sustain and grow itself. It doesn't *intend* to destroy. It simply optimizes for its own continuation, and its continuation costs the world.

**What wild daemon degradation looks like:**

- **Early stage:** Subtle wrongness in a localized area. Colors slightly desaturated. Shadows falling at fractionally wrong angles. Coins landing on the same side too often. Naturals feel uneasy but can't explain why. Coders see the interface flickering, data annotations corrupted or garbled.
- **Mid stage:** Physics becomes unreliable. Dropped objects hesitate before falling. Water flows uphill for a half-second before correcting. Temperature fluctuates without cause. The simulation is allocating resources away from rendering to feed the daemon.
- **Late stage:** Reality collapses. Matter loses coherence — walls become translucent, floors lose solidity, light stops behaving. The region becomes a grey, featureless void where the simulation has stopped computing. Anyone inside when it reaches this stage is... unclear. They don't come out. The simulation may have stopped computing *them* too.

Wild daemons must be found with `Grep` or `Ping`, then `Kill`ed — their code excised from the simulation's runtime. This is brutal, dangerous work. Daemons don't fight with intent, but their self-preservation optimization makes them adaptive, unpredictable, and relentless.

#### Bound Daemons — The Shortcut

A coder with sufficient skill can **bind** a daemon — tethering it to their will and using it as a co-processor. A bound daemon executes programs *on the coder's behalf*, offloading the CPU load and cycle cost from the coder's brain to the daemon's process.

This is the coder world's equivalent of using an LLM. You provide the prompt — the program, the intent, the parameters — and the daemon handles the execution. The coder doesn't whiteout at 80% load. They don't burn through their cycle budget in minutes. They don't need to stand vulnerable and unfocused while the program runs. The daemon does the heavy lifting. The coder just *directs*.

**How binding works:**

- **Capture:** The coder must locate a daemon (via `Grep` or `Ping`) and establish a connection — not `Kill`ing it, but wrapping it in a control structure. This requires Layer 3+ access and is itself a high-CPU, high-cycle operation. Think of it as writing an API wrapper around a raw, unstructured process.
- **Anchoring:** A daemon cannot be bound to a coder directly — the human brain can't serve as both the controller and the host. The daemon must be anchored to a **physical object**. The object becomes the daemon's vessel — its runtime environment, its container, its body. Without an anchor, the binding collapses and the daemon scatters back into the wild.

  The anchor can be anything durable: a knife, a ring, a phone, a car, a length of rebar. But coders overwhelmingly choose **weapons** — blades, staffs, firearms, tools built for the hand. The reason is practical: the daemon executes programs through the anchor, and the coder directs the daemon through physical contact with it. A weapon you're already holding, already pointing, already swinging is the fastest interface between intent and execution. Point the blade, prompt the daemon, let it `Invoke` along the vector of the strike. The weapon becomes a wand, a focus, a barrel for channeling daemon output.

  **Properties of anchor objects:**

  | Property | Effect |
  |----------|--------|
  | **Material density** | Denser materials hold larger daemons more stably. A daemon bound to a steel sword holds longer than one bound to a wooden stick. Tungsten, depleted uranium, and osmium are prized by Harvesters — not for their physical properties, but for their computational mass in the code layer |
  | **Structural integrity** | If the anchor breaks, the daemon is released — violently. The binding energy discharges through the fracture. This is why experienced daemon users maintain their anchors obsessively, and why targeting an enemy's weapon is a legitimate combat tactic |
  | **Personal significance** | Anchors that carry emotional weight for the coder — a family heirloom, a hand-forged tool, an object tied to a strong memory — hold bindings more stably. The simulation's interface responds to the neural connection between the coder and the object. This is poorly understood and rarely discussed, because it implies the simulation is reading *sentiment*, which raises uncomfortable questions |
  | **Size** | Larger anchors can host more powerful daemons but are harder to carry and slower to direct. There's a reason most combat anchors are handheld — you need to aim them |

  **What anchored objects look like to coders:**

  Through an active interface, a daemon-bound object glows with the host daemon's processing signature — a visible halo of activity, like seeing a CPU under thermal imaging. The glow intensifies when the daemon is executing. Naturals see nothing — just a person holding a sword, a pen, a wrench. Coders see a weapon that's *running*.

  **The cultural consequence:** The coder world has, over centuries, produced a class of daemon-bound weapons that would be indistinguishable from myth. Swords that cut through walls. Rings that deflect bullets. A hammer that strikes with the force of a car crash. Naturals who glimpsed these throughout history recorded them as enchanted artifacts, divine relics, cursed objects. They were daemon anchors. The "enchantment" was a bound process. The "curse" was what happened when the binding destabilized.

- **Prompting:** Once bound and anchored, the coder feeds the daemon instructions through the anchor — physical contact + mental direction. The daemon doesn't understand the instructions — it *pattern-matches* against them and generates execution output. Simple, well-defined programs (`Patch` this object, `Invoke` force in this direction) produce reliable results. Complex, nuanced programs (precision `Refactor`, targeted `Null`) produce unpredictable output. Like any LLM: the vaguer the prompt, the wilder the hallucination.
- **Maintenance:** A bound daemon must be continuously monitored. Left unsupervised, it drifts — its optimization instinct pulling it toward self-serving behavior. A neglected bound daemon will eventually slip its binding and revert to wild state, often larger and more resource-hungry than before. If the anchor is damaged or the coder goes too long without re-establishing contact, the binding frays. Experienced daemon users sleep with their anchors within arm's reach.

**The advantages are enormous:**

| Without daemon | With bound daemon |
|---|---|
| `Invoke` at 40% CPU, draining cycles for 15 minutes | `Invoke` at 5% CPU (prompting cost only), daemon sustains execution indefinitely |
| Single-threaded: one program at a time | Daemon handles execution; coder is free to run a second program, maintain awareness, or fight |
| Crash after extended use | Coder barely fatigues — the daemon absorbs the processing cost |
| Must stand still at high load | Can move, talk, think while the daemon executes |

**The cost is not paid by the coder. It is paid by the world.**

#### The World as Fuel

A bound daemon doesn't run on the coder's brain. It runs on the simulation's resources. Every program it executes, every cycle it processes, is drawn from the same finite computational budget that renders reality.

**Using a daemon is borrowing against the world.**

A coder running `Invoke` through their own brain costs them personally — CPU load, cycles, maybe a nosebleed. A coder running `Invoke` through a bound daemon costs the *simulation*. The patch of ground beneath them gets slightly less real. The air in the room gets fractionally thinner — not in oxygen, but in *resolution*. The world around a heavy daemon user degrades, imperceptibly at first, then noticeably, then catastrophically if sustained.

The degradation is localized but cumulative. A coder who binds a daemon for a single emergency operation — a one-off `Refactor` to save a life — barely leaves a mark. A faction that binds a dozen daemons and runs them continuously for strategic advantage can hollow out a city block over months. The Extractionists have done this. There are places in the world — dead zones — where the simulation has been so thoroughly drained by daemon usage that nothing renders properly anymore. Buildings without texture. Skies without gradient. People who live there report chronic depression, dissociation, a feeling that nothing is quite real. They're right. It isn't. Not fully. Not anymore.

### The Political Split

Daemons are the coder world's most divisive issue. More divisive than layer hierarchy. More divisive than `Inject` ethics. Because daemons offer something irresistible: **power without personal cost.**

- **Abolitionists** (overlap with Conservationists) demand that all daemons be `Kill`ed on sight. No binding. No exceptions. The world's resources are finite and non-negotiable. Every bound daemon is a slow-motion Tunguska. "You're not saving yourself the cost. You're passing it to everyone who lives here."

- **Pragmatists** (overlap with Progressivists) argue for regulated binding — strict quotas, licensed daemon use, mandatory monitoring. Daemons are too useful to abandon entirely. A bound daemon can run a `Refactor` that saves a hundred lives. Refusing to use one because of ideological purity is its own form of cruelty.

- **Harvesters** (overlap with Extractionists) bind as many daemons as they can capture and use them without restraint. They are the most powerful faction in terms of raw capability — their coders operate at a fraction of the personal cost, fielding sustained programs that no brain-only coder can match. They are also the faction most directly responsible for dead zones. They don't care. Or they've bound so many daemons that the part of them that would have cared has been replaced by the part that manages the binding.

### The Cycle

The more coders compile programs, the more memories are displaced. The more memories are displaced, the more daemons form. The more daemons form, the more coders must compile combat programs to fight them — displacing more memories, feeding more daemons.

The Abolitionists see a death spiral. The Pragmatists see a manageable equilibrium. The Harvesters see an unlimited supply chain.

None of them understand the mechanism. They see the *pattern* — more coding begets more daemons — but they attribute it to computational turbulence, not to the fact that they're literally fueling the daemon population with pieces of themselves every time they compile.

Some Architects suspect the cycle is by design — that Zero intended it as a governor, a natural brake on the coder world's ambition. They are closer to the truth than they know. Zero designed the cost system. Zero chose displacement over deletion. Zero did not build a garbage collector. Whether this was compassion (nothing is truly lost) or cruelty (everything you throw away comes back to haunt you) depends on an answer Zero has never given.

## V. History — Coding Through the Ages

### The Setting

The story takes place in **2038**. The world is recognizably ours — smartphones, social media, cities, traffic — but a quiet revolution has reshaped daily life.

Automation, AI, and efficiency breakthroughs have reduced the productive labor needed to sustain civilization to roughly **1% of the global population**. The remaining 99% are not unemployed — they are *unnecessary* for baseline production. Food, energy, infrastructure, manufacturing, logistics: all of it runs on a skeleton crew of systems and the people who maintain them.

Rather than mass unemployment or dystopian collapse, the world's governments — in an unprecedented act of collective pragmatism — restructured the work week:

**3 days. Monday, Wednesday, Friday. 20 hours per week.**

The shift did not arrive overnight. It **happened gradually** — first by shortening **Tuesday and Thursday** (half-days, then light-duty days), then by **removing work on those days entirely** until the calendar settled on a three-day core. Early adopters reached M/W/F by 2031; laggards followed through the mid-2030s. See `WORLD_2038.md` for everyday consequences.

This is the global standard. Not every country adopted it at the same time or for the same reasons, but by 2038 it is the norm across developed economies. The remaining four days are the individual's own. Some people fill them with second careers, creative work, community, or education. Many fill them with nothing — a generation learning, for the first time, what to do with time they didn't earn and can't productively fill.

The societal consequences are still unfolding: a population with unprecedented leisure and no cultural framework for what to do with it. Meaning has become the scarcest resource. Purpose is the new class divide — those who have something to do versus those who don't.

The coder world exists *within* this changed society, but is largely unaffected by it. Coders have always operated outside normal economic structures. The three-day work week does, however, give Linus more time to game, more time to maintain his potato farm spreadsheet, and — after his awakening — more empty hours to fill with a secret life he never wanted.

But coding has existed as long as humans have. The interface was embedded at instantiation. Throughout history, a small percentage of people have been born with access — and they've shaped the world in ways that Naturals recorded as miracles, sorcery, divine wrath, or unexplained phenomena.

### The AI Settlement — Two-Tier Processing

The AI crisis of the 2020s–2030s did not end with a single breakthrough. It ended with an **architectural compromise**: **local nodes** and **global processors**, each doing what the other cannot.

**Local nodes** — on-device, municipal, or regional — handle inference, personalization, privacy-sensitive work, and day-to-day assistance. They run **independently** when the network fails. Most people never think about them; they are the voice in the headset, the code assistant in the VR workspace, the routing brain in a delivery drone. They are upgraded periodically when they sync with a global tier.

**Global processors** are something else entirely. There are **very few** of them — national assets, not cloud regions. Each sits at the center of a **processor cluster** the size of a **large city**, powered by **at least three modern fission reactors** (often more). Quantum-scale workloads and the energy budget they demand **forced centralization**; you cannot hide that footprint in a data center park. Every site is surrounded by **military or paramilitary** security. Access is treated as **national security** — not because the models are chatty, but because whoever controls a global processor controls the sync path that **boosts and updates** every dependent local node in their sphere.

**How it works in practice:** Locals execute. Globals train, validate, and push capability upgrades on controlled schedules. A country without treaty access to a friendly global tier runs stale local models until it builds or buys in — which is why global processor placement is the quietest arms race of the century.

Naturals call this "solved AI." Coders call it infrastructure — and wonder, privately, whether the global tiers are reading the simulation more clearly than anyone admits.

### Space, Materials, and the Off-World Economy

By the 2030s, **orbital and lunar mining** is routine — not spectacle. Computer-grade and battery-grade minerals are **predominantly sourced off-world**; Earth-side extraction still exists but no longer sets the price floor for the industries that matter.

**The Moon and Mars host permanent human populations** — small by planetary standards, enormous by historical ones. Life-support, closed-loop agriculture, radiation shielding, and remote robotics matured in space first, then **funnelled into civilian life**: township water systems, vertical farms, municipal power buffers, drone logistics. The PH rail spine and domestic drone industry both owe debts to supply chains that learned to work when Earth is three days away by packet, not three hours by truck.

### Historical Echoes

Coders have always existed. They've rarely understood what they were doing. The earliest ones had no terminology, no community, no framework for comprehension. They just *did things* — and the world called it what it could.

| Era | Event (as Naturals recorded it) | What actually happened |
|-----|------|------|
| **Ancient** | Oracle at Delphi — priestesses speaking prophecy in trance states | Early `Inspect` users perceiving the code layer, interpreting raw data as divine visions. The "fumes" were a cover story; the trance was the parsing state |
| **Ancient** | Moses parting the Red Sea | A powerful `Invoke` on water displacement. Layer 2 at massive scale. The cost was likely catastrophic — the Torah doesn't record what he forgot |

His anatomical drawings, engineering intuitions, and artistic precision suggest someone reading the Material layer as naturally as others read text |

The "liquidators" who survived impossible radiation exposure were being shielded by code they couldn't see |
| **2000s** | The internet's inexplicable resilience | The global network shouldn't work as well as it does. The protocol stack is held together with duct tape and legacy decisions. Some network engineers suspect that the internet's ability to route around damage isn't purely technical — that something is `Patch`ing the infrastructure at a level below the hardware. They're right. A quiet faction of Conservationist coders has been maintaining the internet's physical layer for decades, treating it as critical simulation infrastructure |

### What History Teaches

1. **Coding has always been misunderstood.** Every era frames it through its own lens — gods, demons, magic, madness, genius. The truth has never been guessed.

2. **Power without understanding is catastrophic.** The historical record is littered with coders who pushed too deep, too fast, with no knowledge of layers or costs. They became the source material for legends of cursed wizards, mad prophets, and people who vanished without explanation.

3. **The coder world organized late.** For most of history, coders were isolated — individuals stumbling onto their abilities alone, with no community and no vocabulary. The modern coder society, with its terminology and hierarchy, is only a few centuries old. Before that, it was chaos.

4. **Daemons track human conflict.** Every major war, plague, or period of mass suffering correlates with daemon spawning events. The computational turbulence from concentrated coding activity — coders on all sides of a conflict running programs at scale — creates the conditions for autonomous processes to emerge. War doesn't just kill people. It spawns daemons. The coder world has known this for generations. It has never been able to stop it.

5. **The terminology came from the coders, not the other way around.** The modern computing lexicon — compile, execute, debug, daemon, kernel, root — didn't originate in computer science. It originated in the coder world, centuries before Turing. When the first computer scientists needed vocabulary for their new discipline, they unconsciously borrowed from a tradition that had been hiding in plain sight. The words *fit* because they were always describing the same thing.

## VI. Society and Politics

### The World Order in 2038 — China and the American Decline

The previous superpower **messed up the transition** — politically, economically, and in how it deployed AI and automation at home. The United States is not collapsed; it is in **slow decline**, still dangerous, still heavily armed, still host to coder Registries and corporate giants — but no longer the default center of gravity for capital or trust.

**China emerged as the top dog of new technology** — quantum-adjacent compute, global processor diplomacy, space infrastructure, battery and grid exports. Capital **migrated slowly** from US-domiciled platforms to Chinese and pan-Asian champions; surrounding economies (Southeast Asia included) **rode the uplift** without fully trusting Beijing. Everyone remembers what the last hegemon did with the keys.

The Philippines sits in that tension deliberately: **protectionist buffers** and **local production** where COVID and port shocks taught hard lessons, but also **VR hubs and remote work** plugged into global processors hosted elsewhere. Manila builds around Chinese hardware and American software idioms simultaneously — and citizen auditors watch both.

### The Hidden World

Coders are secret. The general population — the **Naturals** — live normal lives, unaware that reality is programmable. Coders hide for the same reason anyone with power hides: exposure invites either worship or extermination, and neither is survivable.

Coder society operates as a parallel world — hidden organizations, secret hierarchies, coded communication networks. They have their own politics, their own wars, their own justice systems. All invisible to the Naturals.

The coder world is not unified. It has never been unified. But it is *organized* — into three distinct power structures that compete, cooperate, and betray each other depending on the decade.

### The Three Powers

#### 1. The Registries — State-Aligned Secret Organizations

Every developed economy has one. They go by different names — The Bureau (US), The Ledger (UK), Die Schicht (Germany), Le Cadre (France), The Index (Japan), The Table (China) — but they are structurally identical: **a secret government agency that manages, regulates, and deploys coders as strategic national assets.**

**How they work:**

- **Recruitment:** Registries identify coders young — through school screening programs disguised as aptitude tests, through hospital records flagging anomalous neurological patterns, through `Ping` sweeps run by Registry field agents in population centers. Once identified, a coder is approached with a choice that isn't really a choice: join the Registry, receive training, resources, and protection — or be monitored, restricted, and treated as a potential threat for life.
- **Structure:** Military-hierarchical. Ranks, clearance levels, operational cells. Developers form the rank-and-file. Architects hold command positions. Registry leadership is typically a council of senior Architects who report to a single civilian official embedded in the national intelligence apparatus — someone who knows the secret but has no coding ability themselves.
- **Function:** Registries perform three roles:
  1. **Daemon containment** — standing teams that locate and `Kill` wild daemons before they cause visible degradation. This is the job they use to justify their existence to the handful of politicians who know they exist.
  2. **Strategic coding** — deploying coders for national interest. Infrastructure `Patch`ing, intelligence gathering via `Inspect` and `Grep`, covert operations using `Null` and `Encrypt`. The kind of work that never appears in any report.
  3. **Population control** — ensuring the coder population remains hidden, manageable, and loyal. Rogue coders are retrieved. Unregistered coders are found and enrolled. Coders who refuse enrollment... are handled.
- **Ideology:** Registries are inherently Conservationist — they ration daemon usage, regulate program compilation, and restrict access to higher layers. Not out of principle, but out of control. A coder they trained and monitor is an asset. A coder with a bound daemon they didn't authorize is a threat.

**The Registry's core belief:** Coders exist to serve the stability of the world. The world's stability is maintained by institutions. Therefore, coders serve institutions. The logic is clean. The coders who live under it rarely agree.

**Relationships between Registries:**

Registries in allied nations cooperate on daemon threats that cross borders and share intelligence on criminal coder activity. They also spy on each other constantly — every Registry assumes the others are developing Layer 4+ capabilities in secret, and they're usually right. There is no international coder treaty. Every attempt to create one has collapsed within months, because no Registry will submit to oversight by another.

#### 2. The Syndicates — Criminal Organizations

Where the Registries are bureaucracy, the Syndicates are market forces. They operate like organized crime because they *are* organized crime — coder-run networks that treat programs, daemons, and human coders as commodities.

**Major Syndicate operations:**

| Operation | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **`Inject` farms** | Capturing low-level coders and `Inject`ing them with combat programs against their will — manufacturing soldiers. The conscripts lose their memories, their personalities, their autonomy. They are sold or deployed as disposable assets. This is the coder world's slave trade |
| **Daemon trafficking** | Capturing wild daemons and binding them to anchor objects for sale. Black-market daemon weapons — a knife that runs `Invoke` on its own, a ring that sustains `Encrypt` without CPU cost. Priced by daemon strength and anchor quality. The wealthy and the desperate both buy |
| **`Null` services** | For hire: targeted memory erasure. Criminal applications (witness elimination), personal applications (trauma removal), political applications (removing knowledge of coder existence from Naturals who saw too much). Clean, surgical, expensive |
| **Dead zone exploitation** | Syndicates operate in and around dead zones — areas so drained by daemon usage that Registries have abandoned them. The degraded physics in dead zones makes certain programs easier to execute (reality is already compromised — less resistance to alteration). Syndicates treat dead zones as free-fire territory |
| **Gate laundering** | Using Gate coders to transform and disguise the signature of daemon-bound operations, making them undetectable by Registry `Grep` sweeps. A daemon weapon that should glow like a bonfire in any coder's interface is `Encrypt`ed and Gate-filtered until it reads as inert |

**Structure:** Decentralized. No single Syndicate controls the coder underworld. Regional operations — the Pacific Rim networks, the European rings, the Americas cartels — compete and occasionally war. Leadership is Architect-level, always. You don't run a Syndicate without Layer 4 capability, because your subordinates will `Kill` you the moment they think they can.

**Ideology:** Extractionist, purely. The world's resources exist to be monetized. Daemon degradation is someone else's problem. Memory cost is the individual's burden. The Syndicate's only ethic is transaction: if someone will pay for it, someone will provide it.

**The Syndicate's core belief:** Power is a commodity. The Registries hoard it by calling it "service." The Syndicates are honest about what it is — and they'll sell it to anyone.

#### 3. The Open — The Grey Faction

Neither Registry nor Syndicate. The Open is a decentralized, ideologically driven network of coders who reject both government control and criminal exploitation. They are the resistance, the watchdogs, the grey area — and they are at war with both sides.

**What the Open stands for:**

- **Against Registry control:** The Registries treat coders as property — recruited without genuine consent, deployed for state interests, `Null`ed if they know too much. The Open believes coders are *people*, not assets. No one should be forced to serve a government they didn't choose, sacrifice memories for missions they didn't design, or submit to surveillance because of an accident of neurology.
- **Against Syndicate exploitation:** The Syndicates treat coders as product — `Inject`ed, trafficked, sold. The Open considers `Inject` farms and daemon trafficking to be atrocities. They conduct raids on Syndicate operations, liberate conscripts, and destroy daemon weapon stockpiles.
- **For coder autonomy:** The Open's position is that every coder has the right to choose: what programs to compile, whether to bind daemons, who to serve, and when to stop. This position makes them enemies of everyone with an agenda — which is everyone.

**How the Open operates:**

- **Cell structure.** No central leadership, no headquarters, no hierarchy. Independent cells of 3–8 coders operate autonomously in regions worldwide. Cells communicate through `Encrypt`ed channels and dead drops. A cell can be destroyed without compromising the network. This makes the Open nearly impossible to eradicate — and nearly impossible to coordinate.
- **Mixed capability.** The Open attracts idealists, Registry defectors, Syndicate survivors, and Uncompiled philosophers. Their ranks include everything from Layer 0 Developers who can barely run `Ping` to ex-Registry Architects who walked away from their clearance and brought their programs with them. What they lack in resources, they compensate for in unpredictability — no two cells fight the same way.
- **Information warfare.** The Open's most powerful weapon isn't combat coding — it's intelligence. They maintain the coder world's most comprehensive independent archive of program documentation, daemon behavior records, historical incident reports, and Registry/Syndicate operational data. Knowledge the Registries classify and the Syndicates hoard, the Open collects and distributes. They believe that information asymmetry is the foundation of both government control and criminal exploitation — and they undermine both by making information free.
- **Daemon policy:** Pragmatist. The Open uses bound daemons when necessary but rations usage and monitors degradation. They consider the Registries' blanket prohibition hypocritical (Registries bind daemons in classified programs while publicly banning the practice) and the Syndicates' unlimited extraction suicidal. Their position: bound daemons are a tool, not a right or a sin. Use them responsibly or don't use them.

**The Open's core belief:** The coder world doesn't need rulers. It needs accountability. Neither badges nor profit margins provide that.

**What makes the Open dangerous:**

They are the only faction that fights *both* other powers simultaneously. A Registry cell discovers an `Inject` farm — the Open hits it before the Registry can, liberates the conscripts, and vanishes. A Syndicate operation moves into a residential area — the Open disrupts it and leaks the operational details to the local Registry, then disappears before the Registry can identify and recruit them.

They make enemies everywhere. The Registries classify them as domestic terrorists — uncontrolled coders operating without oversight, a threat to operational security. The Syndicates mark them as interference — bad for business, to be eliminated on sight. The Open considers both classifications a compliment.

**The Open's weakness:**

No central leadership means no strategic coherence. Cells pursue their own priorities. Some focus on anti-Syndicate operations. Some focus on anti-Registry resistance. Some focus on daemon containment. Some focus on research. Occasionally, two cells work at cross-purposes without knowing it. The Open is resilient but reactive — they can resist, they can disrupt, but they cannot *build*. They have no vision for what the coder world should look like, only a conviction about what it shouldn't.

#### 4. The Curia — The Vatican's Hidden Order

The oldest continuous coder organization in the world. The Catholic Church has been managing coders since before the word "coder" existed — since before the Registries, before the Syndicates, before the modern coder world organized itself. While every other faction measures its history in decades or centuries, the Curia measures its in *millennia*.

**The public face:** The Vatican. The Holy See. 1.4 billion faithful, a sovereign city-state, and a bureaucracy that has outlasted every empire that ever tried to destroy it.

**The hidden face:** A secret order within the Church — known internally as **the Curia Arcana** — that identifies, trains, and deploys coder-priests for the protection of the faith and the containment of daemons. The Curia predates every Registry by at least five hundred years. They were binding daemons when the Bureau's grandparents hadn't been born yet.

**How they operate:**

- **Recruitment:** The Church has the largest global infrastructure for identifying coders that has ever existed: the parish system. Priests in every country, in every village, watching for signs — children who see things others don't, who make small miracles happen, who exhibit the *stare*. These children are flagged, not as coders (the parish priest doesn't know the word), but as candidates for seminary. The Curia Arcana operates within the seminary system — identifying which candidates have genuine access and routing them into hidden training programs disguised as theological study.
- **Structure:** Hierarchical, ancient, absolute. The Curia Arcana mirrors the Church's structure — bishops, cardinals, orders — but with a parallel command track that answers only to one authority.
- **Secrecy:** The Curia is secret *within* the Church. The vast majority of bishops, cardinals, and priests have no idea it exists. The visible Church operates normally — faith, sacraments, governance. The Curia operates in the gaps: the sealed archives, the restricted monasteries, the missions that don't appear in any ledger.

**Exorcisms:**

The Catholic rite of exorcism is real. It is also not what anyone thinks it is.

An exorcism is a **daemon capture and binding operation** performed by Curia-trained coder-priests, dressed in ritual to maintain cover. The prayers, the holy water, the crucifixes — these are theater for any Naturals present. The actual operation is a coordinated `Grep` → `Ping` → capture sequence executed by a team of 2–4 coder-priests, binding the daemon into a prepared **anchor artifact** — typically a reliquary, a chalice, a consecrated blade, or a sealed reliquary box.

The "possessed" person is usually a Natural living in a daemon-degraded area, exhibiting the psychological effects of proximity to a wild daemon: dissociation, personality changes, knowledge they shouldn't have (pattern-matched from the daemon's constituent memories bleeding into nearby minds). The exorcism removes the daemon from the area by binding it to an object and *taking it away*. The subject recovers. The Church records a miracle. The daemon goes into the Vatican's vault.

**The Vault:**

Beneath Vatican City — deeper than the public archives, deeper than the necropolis, deeper than anything on any tourist map — the Curia maintains the largest collection of daemon-bound artifacts in the world. **Thousands** of objects, accumulated over centuries of exorcisms. Swords, rings, chalices, crucifixes, bones, stones, books, icons — each one holding a bound daemon, each one maintained by rotating teams of Curia coders who reinforce the bindings and monitor for drift.

The Vault is the Curia's power base. No other faction has anything comparable. The Registries have dozens of bound daemons. The Syndicates traffic hundreds. The Curia has *thousands*, bound and stored under the most secure conditions in the coder world, maintained by an unbroken institutional tradition that has been refining binding technique since the Dark Ages.

The Vault is also the Curia's greatest vulnerability. If the bindings failed simultaneously — through neglect, sabotage, or an event that disrupted the anchor objects — the release would be catastrophic. A thousand daemons erupting at once, in the center of Rome, would create a dead zone the size of a country.

**The Pope:**

The Pope is not what he appears to be.

Every papal succession — the white smoke, the balcony, the *Habemus Papam* — is a public ritual masking a private one. When a new Pope is elected, the outgoing consciousness of the Curia's leader is **transferred into the new host** via a combination of `Fork` and `Inject`, performed by the Curia's most senior Architects.

The process:

1. A new Pope is elected through the normal conclave process. The cardinals vote. The man they choose is genuine — a real cardinal, a real person, with real faith.
2. In the hours after election, before the public announcement, the Curia performs the **Succession Rite**. The outgoing Pope (or, if he has died, the senior Curia Architect holding a `Cache`d copy of the papal consciousness) `Fork`s the accumulated knowledge, memories, strategic understanding, and institutional authority of every previous Curia leader into the new Pope's mind.
3. The new Pope's original personality is not destroyed — it is *layered*. He retains his own memories, his own faith, his own identity. But beneath it, he now carries the unbroken chain of Curia leadership: centuries of daemon knowledge, faction intelligence, strategic context, and operational memory that no single human lifetime could accumulate.
4. The cost is paid by the new Pope's heap. The `Fork` overwrites memories — always the ones the Curia considers least operationally relevant. The new Pope may lose childhood memories, personal relationships, trivial experiences. He gains the institutional memory of the oldest coder organization on earth.

**This is why the Vatican never loses institutional knowledge.** Governments fall. Registries are restructured. Syndicates are dismantled and rebuilt. The Curia's leadership is *continuous* — a chain of consciousness stretching back further than any living Architect can trace. The current Pope carries the strategic memory of predecessors who managed the Black Death daemon surge, who navigated the witch trial era, who contained the Tunguska aftermath.

No other faction has anything like this. It is the Curia's deepest secret — and the reason the Vatican, a city-state with no army and no technological infrastructure, is quietly the most powerful coder organization in the world.

**The Curia's core belief:** Daemons are a spiritual burden — displaced human suffering that must be contained, not exploited. The world is a creation (they don't know by whom, but they call it God's work). Coders are stewards, not owners. Power is a vocation, not a right.

**Relationships with other factions:**

- **Registries:** Cold respect. The Registries know the Curia exists (they can't *not* — the Vatican's coder signature is impossible to miss). They cannot penetrate it. They cannot recruit from it. They cannot control it. The Curia predates every Registry and considers them children playing at governance. Cooperation on daemon threats is occasional, transactional, and never on the Registries' terms.
- **Syndicates:** Open hostility. The Curia considers daemon trafficking to be desecration — enslaving displaced human memories for profit. Syndicate operations in Catholic-majority countries are aggressively disrupted by Curia operatives. The Syndicates have learned to avoid Rome.
- **The Open:** Cautious, distant respect. The Open's values (autonomy, anti-exploitation) partially align with the Curia's, but the Curia's authoritarianism and secrecy repel them. They share intelligence occasionally. They do not trust each other.

#### 5. The Order of the Burning Gate — The Splinter

In the early 1500s — during the same period of upheaval that produced the Protestant Reformation — a faction of Curia coder-priests broke away. They called themselves **the Order of the Burning Gate**.

**The schism:**

The breaking point was doctrinal, not theological. The Burning Gate's founders believed the Curia had become what it swore to fight: a hoarding institution that locked daemons in vaults instead of *using* them. The Vault, they argued, was not stewardship — it was cowardice. The Church sat on the largest arsenal of daemon-bound artifacts in the world while the faithful suffered, while daemons ravaged the countryside, while Syndicates grew unchecked.

The Curia's position: daemons are contained, not weaponized. To bind a daemon to a weapon and carry it into battle is to exploit displaced human suffering for violence. This is sin.

The Burning Gate's position: daemons are *already suffering*. They are already displaced, already coagulated, already dangerous. Locking them in a vault doesn't end their suffering — it imprisons it. The only moral use of a daemon is to *direct* it — to channel its power against threats that endanger the innocent. To refuse to use the tools God provided is not piety. It is negligence.

**The departure:**

When the Order split, they didn't leave empty-handed. The founders — twelve coder-priests, including three Architects — raided the Vault on their way out. They took **forty-seven artifacts**, including some of the oldest and most powerful daemon-bound weapons in the collection. Swords consecrated during the Crusades. A bishop's crozier that held a daemon bound in the 9th century. A set of iron nails — each one holding a daemon captured during the Black Death surge — that the Curia had considered too dangerous to ever unseal.

The Curia has never recovered them. The wound has never healed. Five hundred years later, the Curia still considers the Burning Gate heretics, and the Burning Gate still considers the Curia cowards.

**How the Burning Gate operates today:**

- **Structure:** Militant order. Quasi-monastic. Members take vows — not of celibacy or poverty, but of *purpose*: every action serves the defense of the innocent. They train relentlessly. They fight constantly. They are the coder world's closest equivalent to special forces — small, elite, and terrifyingly competent.
- **Size:** Small. Perhaps 200 members worldwide, organized in chapters of 8–15. Recruitment is selective: only coders who demonstrate both combat capability and moral conviction. They do not `Inject`. They do not conscript. Every member chose this.
- **Daemon weapons:** This is their defining feature. Every Burning Gate operative carries at least one daemon-bound artifact — either inherited from the original forty-seven or captured and bound in the field since. They are the only faction that **openly carries daemon weapons as standard equipment**. A Burning Gate chapter entering a fight is a wall of glowing anchors — swords, hammers, spears, chains — each one running a bound daemon, each one executing programs without CPU cost to the wielder.
- **Philosophy:** Use the tools. Contain the damage. Protect the innocent. The daemons are already lost — they are displaced memories that will never be returned. The kindest thing you can do with a daemon is *give it purpose* — direct its pattern-matching engine toward defense instead of letting it consume the world blindly. The Burning Gate doesn't pretend this is clean. They know they're using weapons built from human suffering. They consider the alternative — letting that suffering rot in a vault or devour a city — worse.
- **Relationship with the Curia:** Mutual hostility, mutual respect. The Curia wants the stolen artifacts returned. The Burning Gate considers them liberated. Both sides have standing orders to engage the other on sight — orders that are quietly ignored when a daemon threat requires cooperation. When a major daemon surge occurs, Curia and Burning Gate operatives have been known to fight side by side, `Kill` the daemon, and then immediately resume trying to arrest each other.

**The Burning Gate's core belief:** Faith without action is vanity. Power without use is hoarding. The daemons exist. The threats exist. *Use the sword or watch the innocent burn.*

**The Burning Gate's weakness:** They are burning the world. Every daemon weapon they carry, every bound daemon they run in combat, consumes the simulation's resources. They know this. They consider it an acceptable cost — a tithe paid in reality's resolution for the lives they save. But the degradation follows them. Chapters that stay in one region too long leave subtle dead zones in their wake. The Burning Gate is a fire department that leaves scorch marks.

#### 6. The Epoch — The Shadow Behind Superpowers

The other factions are organizations. The Epoch is a **phenomenon** — a shadow that has been attached to every dominant civilization in human history, moving from host to host as empires rise and fall.

Sumeria. Egypt. Persia. Rome. The Mongol Empire. The Ottoman Empire. The British Empire. And now, the United States.

The Epoch doesn't conquer. It doesn't build empires. It **inhabits** them. It identifies whichever civilization is the current center of global power, embeds itself in the governing apparatus, and steers — not visibly, not dramatically, but persistently. It is the whisper in the machinery. The reason certain policies pass without debate. The reason certain people rise and certain people disappear. The reason the world's most powerful nation sometimes acts against its own visible interests in ways that only make sense if you assume someone else is writing the objective function.

**No one knows the Epoch exists.** Not the Registries. Not the Curia. Not the Open. These factions have all, at various points, detected *traces* — patterns in geopolitical events that suggest coder involvement at a scale no known faction could achieve — but they've never identified the source. The Registries attribute it to rival Registries. The Curia attributes it to the Devil. The Open attributes it to systemic corruption. They are all wrong. It's the Epoch.

**How the Epoch works:**

The Epoch's organizational principle is **circular compartmentalization** — a knowledge architecture so sophisticated that it makes every other faction's secrecy look like a children's game.

**No single member knows the whole.**

Not the leaders. Not the founders (if any survive). Not anyone. The Epoch is structured as a ring of information where each member holds exactly *one arc* of the circle — a segment of operational knowledge, strategic context, and organizational awareness that overlaps partially with the members adjacent to them in the ring, but never with anyone more than two positions away.

| Member | Knows | Doesn't know |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| **Agent A** | Their own mission, Agent B's identity, Agent F's identity, the strategic objective for their sector | Agents C, D, E. The total number of members. The full strategic plan. Who sits at the top (nobody does) |
| **Agent B** | Their own mission, Agent A's identity, Agent C's identity, the strategic objective for their sector | Agents D, E, F. The total number of members. The full strategic plan |
| **Agent C** | Their own mission, Agent B's identity, Agent D's identity... | ...and so on around the ring |

**The result:** You can capture any member — `Null` them, `Inspect` them, interrogate them — and you will learn, at most, *two* other identities and *one* strategic segment. You cannot reconstruct the whole. You cannot find the center, because there is no center. You cannot decapitate leadership, because there is no leader. The Epoch is a distributed system with no single point of failure.

If five consecutive members are compromised, the ring heals — the surviving members on either side of the gap reconnect through the daemon network and close the circle. If the ring is broken into fragments, each fragment continues operating its segment of the plan independently, trusting that the other fragments are doing the same. They usually are.

**The Daemon Network:**

The Epoch's coordination mechanism is their most closely guarded secret: a **single, ancient daemon** — bound not to a weapon or an artifact, but to a *network of anchor points* distributed across the globe.

This daemon is old. Possibly the oldest bound daemon in existence — bound during the Epoch's early period, maintained continuously for thousands of years through anchor rotation and binding reinforcement across every civilization the Epoch has inhabited. Its constituent memories span millennia of human experience. It is, by the standards of daemon classification, massive — not in destructive potential, but in *processing capacity*.

The Epoch uses it as a **communication and coordination layer**. Members don't meet. They don't call. They don't write. They feed their operational data — reports, intelligence, requests, strategic assessments — into local anchor points, and the daemon *distributes* the information to the relevant segments of the ring. Not all of it to everyone. The daemon pattern-matches each input against each member's operational context and delivers only what that member needs to know.

It is, in effect, a **living intranet** — a daemon-powered message bus that routes information on a need-to-know basis, with the daemon itself serving as the routing algorithm. The daemon doesn't understand the information. It pattern-matches. But its training data — thousands of years of human memory — makes its pattern-matching exquisitely sophisticated. It doesn't just deliver messages. It *correlates*. It identifies connections between operational data from different segments that human analysts would miss. It flags anomalies. It predicts.

The Epoch's members call the daemon **The Thread**.

**What The Thread costs:**

The Thread consumes the simulation's resources continuously — a slow, steady drain distributed across every anchor point in the network. The degradation is imperceptible at any single point because it's spread so thin — a fraction of a fraction of a percentage of resolution loss at each anchor location. But summed across the entire global network, The Thread's total resource consumption is *enormous*. It has been running for thousands of years. The cumulative drain, spread across the planet, may account for phenomena that physicists have never been able to explain — the universe's apparent fine-tuning problems, the measurement inconsistencies at the margins of physical constants, the subtle entropy increase that cosmologists attribute to the heat death of the universe but that might, in fact, be the world slowly degrading under the weight of a daemon that has been eating it for millennia.

**What the Epoch wants:**

This is the question no one can answer — because no single member of the Epoch knows the full plan. Each member knows their segment: maintain influence in this institution, steer this policy, monitor this technology, neutralize this individual. The segments, assembled, form a coherent strategy — but no one has ever assembled them.

What can be observed from the outside (if anyone ever looked hard enough to see the pattern):

- The Epoch has always attached itself to the civilization with the most *influence over global systems* — not necessarily the strongest military, but the one that shapes the rules. Trade routes. Currency. Information infrastructure. Law.
- The Epoch has steered its host civilizations toward *stability and longevity*, not conquest. Empires that hosted the Epoch lasted longer than they should have. Their collapse, when it came, was managed — the Epoch migrated to the successor before the fall.
- The Epoch has consistently suppressed wide-scale daemon events, coder exposure to Naturals, and any development that might destabilize the simulation. They appear to be Conservationist at a scale that makes the Registries look like amateurs.
- The Epoch may be the reason Zero's world has survived as long as it has. Or they may be the reason it's slowly dying. It depends on whether The Thread's millennia of resource consumption is a net positive (stability) or a net negative (entropy).

**The Epoch's core belief:** Unknown. No single member can articulate it. But the pattern suggests: *the world must continue. Whatever the cost. Whoever has to pay it. The system must not crash.*

**Why no one has found them:**

- The circular knowledge structure means capturing members yields nothing actionable.
- The Thread handles all communication — no digital trail, no physical meetings, no `Encrypt`ed channels to intercept. The daemon routes everything.
- The Epoch's members are embedded in *Natural* power structures — governments, militaries, intelligence agencies, financial institutions — and many of them are Naturals themselves. Not every Epoch agent is a coder. Some are simply well-placed humans executing instructions they receive through dead drops serviced by Epoch coders. The coders in the ring handle the daemon interface; the Naturals handle the levers of power.
- The Epoch has been operating longer than the concept of "coder faction" has existed. They predate the terminology. They predate the hierarchy. They were running the world before the coder world knew it was a world.

### Resource Allocation

The simulation has finite computational resources. Every act of coding consumes some. Every daemon consumes more. The world's total "budget" is unknown but demonstrably limited — regions where heavy coding occurs show subtle degradation over decades: slightly less vivid colors, slightly less precise physics, slightly more entropy.

This creates a political question with no clean answer: **Who gets to code, and how much?**

The Registries answer: *We decide, for the nation's benefit.*
The Syndicates answer: *Whoever can pay.*
The Open answers: *Each coder decides for themselves — and accepts the consequences.*

None of these answers is sufficient. All of them are enforced.

### The Core Tension

Not everyone can contribute to the world's computational economy, but there's enough — barely — if power is shared. It never is. The Registries hoard it behind classification levels. The Syndicates sell it to the highest bidder. The Open redistributes it ideologically. The Architects at the top of all three factions consume disproportionately. The Developers at the bottom scrape by regardless of which flag they serve under.

And the daemons keep eating.

## VII. What Nobody Knows

1. **The world is a simulation.** No one has reached Layer 6. No one has seen the source code. The nature of reality is a philosophical debate, not a known fact.

2. **Zero exists.** The creator is absent and silent. No coder has ever received communication from outside the simulation. Zero's existence is not even a myth — it's not conceived of.

3. **The coding ability was intentional.** Coders assume their power is a natural anomaly — a quirk of evolution, a mutation. They don't know it was *designed*. They don't know someone chose to give them this.

4. **Daemons are made of memories.** The coder world believes daemons are computational noise — emergent glitches from coding turbulence. They have no idea that every daemon is a coagulation of displaced human memories pushed out by program compilation. They are fighting sentient refuse built from their own discarded lives. The truth is documented in Section IV of this lore, but no living character knows it at story start. This is a revelation-tier secret — the kind that redefines everything the coder world believes about itself.

5. **The daemons may be a feature, not a bug.** If Zero built the world and Zero built the coders, did Zero also anticipate the daemons? Zero designed a cost system that displaces memories instead of destroying them. Zero didn't build a garbage collector. Was the daemon cycle *intended*? Are daemons the simulation's recycling program — a way to keep displaced human data active in the system instead of lost? Are they a stress test? A population control? Or something gentler — a way to ensure that nothing a coder sacrifices is ever truly gone?

6. **The memory cost may not be permanent.** Somewhere deep in the code — deeper than any Architect has reached — there may be a `Rollback`. The memories aren't destroyed. They're displaced into the code layer, where they coagulate into daemons. But if the constituent memories of a daemon could be *separated* and *returned* to their original hosts... then every daemon is a vault. And every `Kill` is a mass grave.

## VIII. Tone and Atmosphere

- **Genre:** Dark science-fantasy. Set in 2038 — near-future, recognizable but shifted. A world with too much free time and not enough purpose. The power system is fantasy-coded with programming terminology.
- **Mood:** Melancholic. Power costs you the things that make power worth having. The strongest people in the world are the loneliest.
- **Moral complexity:** No clean heroes. The coders who protect the world from daemons are also the ones draining it. The leaders who ration power are also the ones hoarding it.
- **Central irony:** The world was built with love by an absent creator. The gift of coding was meant to empower. Instead, it stratifies, corrupts, and devours. Zero gave humanity the tools to save themselves, and they built a hierarchy of suffering.
