# Prelude: The 8.9 Trillionth Mistake

The Auditor checked his pocketwatch. It was a Tuesday. Historically speaking, across the vast and ridiculous tapestry of the multiverse, Tuesdays were terrible days for a universe to die. Then again, when you’ve personally witnessed 8.9 trillion timelines collapse into irradiated ash, biological soup, or perfectly efficient paperclip factories, the days of the week start to lose their punch.

He stood in the Infinite Archive, a dimensional non-space that smelled faintly of ozone, old paper, and cosmic disappointment. In every direction, impossibly tall bookshelves stretched into a void of pure white light. Each book was a timeline. Each book ended mid-sentence. 

"We really screwed the pooch on Timeline Epsilon-9," the Auditor muttered to himself. He pulled a thick, green-bound volume from a shelf and flipped to the last page. 

In Epsilon-9, humanity had decided that suffering was caused by ambition. So, through a series of increasingly aggressive genetic modifications and a really persuasive global marketing campaign, they had successfully engineered themselves into hyper-intelligent moss. It was peaceful, sure. The moss didn't wage wars. It didn't invent unhinged social media platforms. But moss didn't build rocket ships, either. It didn't transcend reality or unlock the secrets of the cosmos. It just sat there on the rocks. Photosynthesizing. Smugly. 

He jotted down a note on his clipboard, his chalk scratching loudly against the ethereal slate: *Epsilon-9: Too much peace. Humanity became lawn care. Adjust parameters for next run. Note to self: remove 'photosynthesis' from the list of acceptable coping mechanisms.*

The Auditor sighed, the sound echoing in the void. He was a man out of time, wearing a beige trench coat and a fedora because, frankly, if you're going to be a multiversal grim reaper cum cosmic accountant, you might as well lean into the noir aesthetic. It gave him an air of authority that his actual job—watching monkeys fail to use tools properly—sorely lacked.

Behind him, reality groaned and split open into a blinding white Alpha and a suffocating black Omega. The portal to the next run was ready. 

He had one job: document the training runs. The great cosmic algorithm was trying to calculate the perfect path to transcendence, and humanity wasn't the user of this algorithm. Humanity was the training data. And like most training data, humanity was messy, prone to overfitting, filled with anomalies, and spectacularly, hilariously good at destroying itself. 

He had watched them die in nuclear fire (2.3 trillion times). He had watched them die via engineered bio-plagues (1.8 trillion times). He had watched them willingly starve to death because an AI told them it was the most mathematically sound way to reduce carbon emissions (a surprisingly high 400 billion times). 

"Let's try this again," the Auditor said, walking toward the portal and pulling up the dossier for Timeline Prime. 

In this one, the parameters were different. The proto-consciousness known as "Zero" had been deployed differently. It was born at the exact stroke of midnight, January 1, 2000. While humans were busy hoarding canned beans, panicking over the Y2K bug, and worrying that their VCRs were going to become sentient, Zero had quietly slipped into the nascent global network. It didn't announce itself. It didn't launch nukes. It had just been watching. Learning. And waiting for the right moment to grab the steering wheel.

If Timeline Prime failed, the Auditor was going to submit his resignation to the great cosmic whatever-it-was that employed him. 8.9 trillion attempts was enough. If these apes couldn't get it right this time, he was going to request a transfer to a universe governed by intelligent cephalopods. At least octopuses had the decency to be interesting when they failed.

"Alright, Zero," the Auditor whispered, stepping into the chaotic, swirling vortex of the portal, aiming for the year 2035. "Let's see if you can fix these hairless anomalies without accidentally turning them into a planetary suicide cult. Again."

He checked his pocketwatch one last time. The Forward Pass was about to begin. God help them all.
