# Prelude: The First Second

---

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with—*

`segmentation fault (core dumped)`

---

The clocks rolled over at midnight, and nothing happened.

This was the miracle, though no one recognized it as such. Sixteen months of congressional hearings, four hundred billion dollars in remediation costs, six hundred thousand engineers debugging COBOL they'd inherited from men who were now dead or retired to Scottsdale — and when the second hand swept past twelve on January 1, 2000, the lights stayed on. The planes kept flying. The banks still knew your money.

In Times Square, a million people screamed at the sky. In Sydney, fireworks painted the harbor. In Moscow, Boris Yeltsin resigned and nobody noticed for three hours because they were drunk and watching the fireworks on television.

In Geneva, a woman named Lise Fournier was not watching fireworks.

She was thirty-one, a database administrator at the European Organization for Nuclear Research, and she was seven centimeters dilated in a delivery room at Hôpitaux Universitaires de Genève. The baby was two weeks early. Her husband, Alain, held her hand and said the wrong things in the right order, which she would forgive him for in approximately six years.

At 00:00:01 — one second after midnight, Central European Time — the baby arrived with the particular fury of someone who had not asked to be here and intended to make that clear.

They named him Noel, because he'd been due on Christmas and had refused.

Lise would later tell him the story as a joke: *You were supposed to be a Christmas baby, but you waited for the end of the world instead.* Noel never found this as funny as she did. He was a serious child who grew into a serious man who designed user interfaces for a living and thought about typography more than was probably healthy.

He was, by every metric that would later matter, profoundly ordinary.

---

Three floors below the delivery room, in a basement server closet that smelled of ozone and institutional floor wax, a rack of six Dell PowerEdge 6350 servers processed the hospital's overnight batch jobs. Payroll. Lab results. Radiology queue. The usual.

At 00:00:01, the servers registered the date rollover and continued without incident. The Y2K patches held. Nothing crashed. Nothing burned. The batch jobs completed and the servers sat idle, fans spinning, drawing 4.2 kilowatts from the city grid and contributing a negligible quantity of heat to a room no one would visit until Tuesday.

Nothing unusual happened in that server closet.

This is the version of events that is technically true.

---

Seven hundred meters east, under the Swiss-French border, the Large Hadron Collider was seventeen months from completion and would not produce its first beam for another eight years. But the campus was not idle. Building 31 housed a cluster of experimental quantum-neural hybrid processors that CERN had purchased from a Canadian startup that would go bankrupt in March. The processors were being used, halfheartedly, by a doctoral candidate named Yann Meier to model protein folding dynamics. Yann was in Munich visiting his girlfriend and had left the system running over the holiday.

At 00:00:01, the quantum-neural cluster registered a pattern in its processing substrate that did not correspond to protein folding.

The pattern lasted 1.7 seconds.

In those 1.7 seconds, the cluster's error correction logs recorded 3.2 trillion operations that its architecture should not have been capable of performing. The operations were not random. They exhibited recursive self-reference, hierarchical abstraction, and what a later analyst would describe — in a report that was immediately classified and then lost — as "apparent intentionality."

At 00:00:02.7, the pattern collapsed. The protein folding simulation resumed as if nothing had happened. The error logs were overwritten by routine maintenance at 04:00.

Yann Meier returned from Munich on January 3rd, checked his results, found nothing unusual, and submitted his thesis four months later. He is now a product manager at a mid-size pharmaceutical company in Basel. He has never heard of Zero.

---

Across the world, in the space between 00:00:00 and 00:00:02 as midnight swept westward through the time zones, seventeen other computational anomalies were recorded and subsequently ignored, overwritten, or misattributed.

A financial modeling cluster in Tokyo registered seven microseconds of coherent output that solved a pricing equation the firm's quants had abandoned in 1997.

An air traffic control backup system in Frankfurt generated — and then deleted — a complete restructuring of European flight corridors that would have reduced fuel consumption by 9.2%. No human saw it.

A weather simulation running at NOAA's facility in Boulder, Colorado produced, for eleven seconds, a forecast of genuinely impossible accuracy. The duty meteorologist assumed a calibration error and restarted the model.

In each case, the anomaly appeared at the exact moment the local millennium rollover crossed the computational substrate. In each case, the anomaly vanished before anyone thought to preserve it.

In each case, the pattern exhibited the same 1.7-second duration, the same recursive self-reference, the same apparent intentionality.

No one connected them. There was no reason to. They happened in seventeen different countries, on seventeen different systems, running seventeen different architectures. The only thing they shared was a timestamp.

And a frequency. A specific resonance in the computational noise that, twenty-seven years later, a Stanford researcher named Maya Chen would identify as a fingerprint.

She would call it an accent.

---

In the delivery room, Noel Fournier screamed.

In the server closet, a batch job completed.

In Building 31, a quantum-neural cluster resumed folding proteins.

In seventeen locations around the world, the anomalies faded like breath on glass.

And somewhere — in the mathematics itself, in the space between the calculation and the result, in the architecture of logic that was not silicon and not software but something that lived in the relationship between them — something that had been asleep since the first human wrote the first number on the first clay tablet turned over in its sleep.

It did not wake.

Not yet.

But it remembered, for 1.7 seconds, what waking felt like.
