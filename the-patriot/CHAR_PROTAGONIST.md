# The Protagonist — *The Patriot*

> Shared across all five story options. Each option instantiates him with a different name, job, and loved one; the architecture below does not change.

---

## The shape of him

A man in his mid-thirties who has never been anything. No degree that matters, no title, no following, no rank. A field technician, a contractor, a night-shift analyst — the guy in the polo shirt with the laminated ID who is in the building because something needs fixing.

He is, quietly, the most capable person in every room he has ever entered, and he does not know it, because he has never had a frame of reference. He assumes everyone else is also holding the whole system in their head and simply chooses not to mention it.

**What people say about him:** *He's good with systems.* Said the way you'd say someone is good with their hands.

---

## The Ability: `Trace`

### What he thinks it is

Instinct. Being observant. Paying attention. A knack.

He has believed this his entire life and nothing has ever contradicted it.

### What it actually is

**`Trace` is a passive, continuous, layer-agnostic read of provenance.** Where Linus Kerrigan's `Decompile` reads *how a thing was built*, `Trace` reads *what has happened to it* — the causal history and current structural state of anything he attends to.

He does not see an interface. This is the crux.

Every coder's HUD renders when they consciously reach for the code layer — the overlay is a *response to a request*. `Trace` has been running since before he had language. It never rendered as an overlay because it was never requested; it integrated into ordinary perception the way sight did. The code layer is not something he looks *at*. It is part of how the world looks.

So the simulation's data arrives in him as **certainty**:

- He looks at a spreadsheet and knows which cell is a lie. He cannot tell you how. He can tell you he's never wrong.
- He looks at a bridge and knows which pour was short. He assumes he inferred it from the staining.
- He looks at a man across a desk and knows what that man has touched — not his thoughts, not his intentions, but the *events he is downstream of*. Rooms he has been in. Money that has passed through him. He experiences this as reading people.
- He looks at an office and sees which parts are load-bearing and which are decoration. Institutions are running processes too.

### The three real constraints

1. **It reads. It does not write.** `Trace` cannot move a peso, open a door, or stop a car. He is omniscient and completely powerless — which is the entire reason the plot exists. He must still infiltrate, forge, bribe, install, and kill, because knowing is not doing.

2. **It reads what is and what was. Never what will be.** He is not a prophet. He is an archive with legs. Every catastrophe he fails to prevent, he fails because he was looking at the wrong thing.

3. **It has been eating him since he was four.** Programs replace memories (`WORLD_LORE.md` §II). A continuously-running process has been quietly overwriting him for thirty years, following the standard triage: benign memories first, social cognition second, identity last.

### The cost, and the lie he tells about it

**He has almost no childhood.** Not repression — absence. He has photographs of a birthday he cannot place. He knows facts about his early life the way you know facts about a stranger's.

He has spent his adult life assuming he simply has a bad memory. Everyone has believed him, because a man who can recite a district's entire procurement history from memory obviously does not have a memory problem, so it must be selective, so it must be psychological, so it must be something that happened to him. His family has a whole folk theory about it.

**In the last third of every version of this story, the second tier begins.** Social cognition. He gets colder. He starts calculating people. He loses the ability to read a room he is standing in, while still being able to read the room's finances perfectly. The reader will attribute this to the moral arc — he is becoming what he hunts — and the reader will be wrong. It is the heap.

**And the detail the whole book is built on:**

> He cannot remember X's face.
>
> Not the voice, not the hands, not the specific way she said his name. He has photographs. He looks at them and recognizes the person the way you recognize a public figure.
>
> He has always thought this was grief. Grief does that, people told him. It comes back.
>
> It was never grief. It was rent.

## The reveal (final act, every version)

He does not discover this. He is **told**, near the end, by someone who has always known what he was looking at — a Cedula operative, a corrupted Architect, a dying coder in a Registry cell. Three or four pages. No spectacle.

He learns, in this order:

1. There is a hidden world of people who can alter reality by reaching into its code.
2. He is one of them, and has been since childhood, and has never once compiled a program.
3. His entire life's talent — the thing that made him useful, the thing that let him find them, the thing he built his self-image on — was never his. It was a process running in a brain that never asked for it.
4. **The forgetting was the invoice.** The bad memory. The empty childhood. Her face.
5. And he cannot switch it off, because he never switched it on. He will keep paying until there is nothing left, and the only thing he has ever been able to do with it is *watch*.

He does not get an upgrade. He does not level up and defeat anyone. He gets an explanation, and the explanation costs him the last thing he owned, which was the belief that he had earned himself.

**The last beat — Zero:** he asks the obvious question. *Why me?* And the person telling him has no answer, because nobody in the world knows there is an answer, because the answer is that five thousand years ago something outside the world left a seam in human neurology, withdrew completely, and never came back to explain. He is not chosen. He is a *side effect* — the same as the reform, the same as everything good that happened in this book.

## Seeding rules (so the reveal lands)

Plant these early, cold, unexplained. Never lampshade them.

| Seed | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| He is never wrong about a document's provenance, in a world where forgery is free and provenance is the only currency | Becomes the engine of the entire plot |
| He cannot remember his own eighth birthday but can recall a 2019 disbursement voucher he saw once | Played as a running joke for 200 pages, then stops being funny |
| He gets a nosebleed under sustained attention — reading a room hard, for hours | Never explained in-book |
| Another coder `Ping`s the building and reads a null where he is standing | Two paragraphs, chapter 9, no comment |
| A dog will not enter a room he is in | Once. Never again |
| He describes X in complete, vivid, physical detail — and the detail is wrong, and someone corrects him, and he laughs it off | The knife, when the reader re-reads it |

## Relationship to Linus Kerrigan

Two halves of one instrument, in two men, in the same decade, in the same country, neither aware of the other.

| | `Decompile` (Linus) | `Trace` (the Patriot) |
|---|---|---|
| Reads | How a thing was built — the source | What has happened to a thing — the provenance |
| Trigger | Witnessing an execution; violent, involuntary | None. Always on. Since childhood |
| Interface | Full HUD, immediately | None, ever. Renders as intuition |
| Knows he has it | Chapter 2 | Final chapter |
| Cost | Paid in visible chunks, counted, grieved | Paid continuously for thirty years, never noticed |
| What it can do | Steal any power in the world | Nothing. It only watches |

Linus spends his book terrified of using 1% of his ability. The Patriot spends his book using 100% of his and calling it a personality.

Whether they ever meet is an open question. Recommend: no. Recommend: they are in the same building once, for eleven minutes, in 2038, and nothing happens.
