# Story Option 05 — *Kartilya*

> **Engine:** He dies at the 40% mark. Before he does, he cuts the file into nine pieces and hands them to nine strangers who will never meet — and the country is changed by a ring of people who each know one ninth of the truth and one name on either side of them.
>
> **Register:** Mosaic. Mythic. Ten narrators, one dead man. The most overtly *Zero* of the five, and the only one where "the Patriot" is not a person.

---

## Logline

An ATM repairman robs a Sampaloc branch to buy his aunt a heart valve, and takes home the Standing Instructions — the ninety-year continuity file that tells each Philippine administration what it is not permitted to investigate. When they burn her alive he does not publish it, and he does not use it. He cuts it into nine and gives it away to nine strangers, and dies eleven weeks later, and the country spends the next decade being saved by people who have never heard his name.

---

## Cast

## Structure

Ten voices. Part One is Dan, close third, eleven weeks. Parts Two through Four are the Nine and the people who hunt them, each chapter a different holder, each holder knowing one ninth.

The reader is the only one who ever sees the whole file. **This is the point of the book.**

---

## The robbery

Dan services cash recyclers. He has legitimate keys, a legitimate route, and eleven years of watching a machine count money that is not his.

He does not break the machine. He breaks the **reconciliation**: a recycler's internal count and the branch's ledger reconcile on a nightly cycle, and Dan knows — because he has filed four ignored bug reports about it — that a specific firmware version handles a specific cassette-jam recovery by trusting the machine over the ledger. He induces the jam. ₱2.8M becomes, to the bank's books, money that was never in the machine.

He is caught within six days. He knew he would be. He had eleven weeks and he needed nine.

The vault trip is incidental: the branch's recycler spares are stored in the vault annex, and while he is in there he opens a legal-size box that has been mislabeled since a 2011 merger, expecting spare cassettes.

---

## The document — the Standing Instructions

Ninety pages. Ring-bound. Retyped every administration since 1946, each version bearing the initials of the outgoing and incoming Continuity officer.

It is not a record of crimes. It is a **list of what must not be examined.**

Thirty-one standing instructions, some added, some retired, most inherited. Files that may not be opened. Cases that must not be revived. Three families that must not be prosecuted. A geological survey that must not be repeated. A stretch of coastline that must not be developed. Two clergy who must never be reassigned. A 1971 casualty list that must remain sealed. An instruction, added in 1986 and never removed, to ensure that no future administration audits the one before it too thoroughly, for the stability of the succession.

Each instruction has a rationale. Most rationales are one line. Several are *blank* — inherited so long ago that no one alive knows why, obeyed anyway for ninety years.

**And Standing Instruction #1**, unchanged since the first typed copy, the only one with no rationale, no date, and no initials:

> *Nothing may be permitted to reach Layer Six.*

Nobody in the Continuity Staff has ever known what that means. Boy Tandoc has spent thirty-one years assuming it is a reference to a document classification scheme that was discontinued before he was born.

---

## The murder

They do not arrest Nanay Lucing. They do not question her. They burn the compound.

The fire starts at 2:40 AM in an electrical panel that had been inspected in March. It takes eleven houses. Four people die, including a woman with a bad heart who could not get down the stairs.

It is investigated properly. It is ruled accidental, correctly, on the evidence. The block is cleared and rezoned within a year for a mid-rise, which is the second reason it happened, and neither reason cancels the other. **In this country a message and a business opportunity are frequently the same event.**

---

## The turn — eleven weeks

Dan is out on bail his cousin cannot afford, with a file that describes ninety years of officially sanctioned blindness, and here is what he works out in the first week:

- He cannot publish it. It is retyped, unsigned, unverifiable, and in 2038 a perfect forgery of a 1974 mimeograph costs forty pesos. It is a rumor with a ring binder.
- He cannot use it. He is an ATM technician with a criminal case and a burned house. He has no leverage, no institution, no money, and no time.
- He cannot keep it. They will find him. He is not hard to find. They found his aunt in six days.

So he does the only thing a man with eleven weeks and no power can do, and it is the idea the whole book is built on:

**He makes the file survivable by making it useless to any single person.**

He photographs it, splits it into nine sections, and gives one section each to nine people chosen for one quality: *they are not connected to him or to each other.* The girl who sold him lunch. The referee. The pharmacist. Nine strangers, nine envelopes, nine conversations lasting under four minutes.

He gives each of them exactly two other names — the holder before and the holder after — and one instruction: *if you ever need help, and only then, contact one of them. Never both at once. Never tell them what you have.*

He has just built a ring.

He does not know he has built a ring. He built it because it was the only shape that made sense with the materials he had — nine people, no trust, no time — which is precisely how the first one got built, five thousand years ago, by someone with the same problem.

---

## His death

Week eleven. Not dramatic: two men, a stairwell in Sampaloc, four minutes. He has already sent the ninth envelope. The reader knows this; the two men do not; Boy Tandoc will not know it for three years.

He dies believing he failed, which he did, and which is not the same as being wrong.

**Part One ends here. Sixty percent of the book remains.**

---

## The ladder — how a country improves without a hero

Each of the Nine holds one ninth and can act only on that.

- **The Bacolod teacher** holds the instruction about the 1971 casualty list. It takes her four years, a freedom-of-information suit, and the deaths of two of the three people who could have blocked it. Nine hundred families learn where their dead are.
- **The customs broker** holds the three families that must not be prosecuted. He does not have the standing to prosecute anyone. What he *can* do is stop clearing their cargo, correctly, on paper, for three years, and teach forty other brokers how. It is the most boring resistance in literature and it costs one family ₱11B.
- **The drone mechanic in Zamboanga** holds the coastline that must not be developed, finds out why, and the reason is the best single chapter in the book.
- **The Baguio accountant** holds the 1986 succession-audit instruction, and understands immediately that she is looking at the reason no Philippine administration has ever seriously audited the one before it, and she is a woman who has spent forty years reconciling accounts nobody asked her to reconcile.
- **The girl who sold him lunch** holds nine pages she does not understand for six years, and then does the single most consequential thing anyone in the book does, and the reader has to wait six years for it.

None of them meets the others. Twice, two of them are in the same room and do not know. The reforms accrete: a sealed list opened, a cargo lane closed, a coastline saved, an audit precedent set, a family broken. **No summit, no leader, no manifesto.** The government improves the way a wall gets repointed — one joint at a time, by people who cannot see the wall.

And the name they use, when they use anything — a signature on a filing, a handle on an audit forum, a word passed to a journalist — is the one Dan never used about himself. Somebody starts it in Year 2. Nobody knows who.

---

## The hunt

Boy Tandoc, 71, is the most sympathetic antagonist in the five options.

He does not want to hurt anyone. He has spent thirty-one years believing that the Standing Instructions are load-bearing — that a country this fragile survives on a small number of things nobody looks at, and that his job is the unglamorous, thankless, morally filthy work of being the man who looks away professionally so that a hundred and forty million people don't have to.

He is not entirely wrong, and the book must let him not be entirely wrong.

What breaks him is not being outmaneuvered. It is realizing, in Year 3, what he is looking at: **someone has built a ring.** Nine holders, two contacts each, no center, no leader, no way to decapitate. He knows this architecture. It is *his* architecture — the Continuity Staff has used it since 1946 without ever asking where it came from.

And he understands that whoever did this either knew his structure and copied it, or arrived at it independently.

The second possibility frightens him far more than the first.

---

## Ending

Year 6. Tandoc, dying, does the thing thirty-one years should have made impossible: he calls the successor he was supposed to brief, and does not brief him. He ends the chain. He leaves the Standing Instructions on the table with a handwritten annotation next to #1 — *I never knew what this meant. I obeyed it for thirty-one years. Someone should find out.* — and the annotation is the last line of Part Four.

The girl who sold Dan lunch, now twenty-five, publishes her nine pages. Not the whole file — she has never had the whole file, and neither has anyone but the reader. Nine pages, with six years of independently built provenance behind them, in a country whose ten million citizen auditors now have the tooling and the appetite to verify.

The Republic does not fall. It gets slightly, permanently harder to run badly.

**The last chapter** is a woman on a jeepney in Sampaloc explaining to her son who the Patriot was, and every single thing she says is wrong, and it is all beautiful, and the reader — who watched him die in a stairwell in week eleven believing he had failed — has to sit with that.

## The Zero layer

The most exposed of the five. Still never explained.

1. **Standing Instruction #1.** *Nothing may be permitted to reach Layer Six.* Ninety years of Philippine administrations obeying an instruction copied from an instruction, whose meaning was lost before the Republic existed. Layer 6 is Root — the simulation's source (`WORLD_LORE.md` §II). The reader of *The Architects* knows exactly what it means. Nobody in *The Patriot* ever finds out.

   Where it came from: the Epoch's Philippine arc, seeded into the postwar continuity apparatus in 1946 the way it has been seeded into every governing apparatus of every dominant civilization since Sumeria. It is not a Philippine instruction. It is *the* instruction, translated. It is in the equivalent file in eleven other countries and nobody in any of them knows why either.

2. **The ring.** Dan reinvents circular compartmentalization from scratch, with nine envelopes and eleven weeks, because it is the only architecture that works when you have information, no power, and no time. The implication the book leaves unspoken: **the Epoch's structure was never a stroke of genius. It is what any sufficiently desperate mind builds under those conditions**, and Zero built a species that would keep arriving at it.

3. **Dan.** He is a Natural. No latent ability, no seam, no gift — this option deliberately withholds that. His only distinction is that he read firmware for pleasure and loved the woman who raised him. **The country was saved by an ordinary man, and the design had nothing to do with it, and that is the strongest possible statement of what Zero actually did:** built a world, put a seam in it, walked away, and let people be people.

4. **One unexplained thing.** In the Sampaloc fire, on the third floor, a woman with an eight-month-old came down a staircase that the fire investigation later determines had already collapsed. She has no explanation. The investigators write it as witness confusion. It is two paragraphs, in Part One, and it is never mentioned again. *Somebody else was in that building.* (See: the Curia, the Cedula, the Open — pick one, name none.)

## Grounding notes (keep these real)

- Sampaloc compound life: the shared meter, the panel that everyone knows is bad, the stairs, the fire trucks that cannot enter the alley.
- Informal-settlement fires and rezoning. Say it plainly; it does not need embellishment.
- Third-party ATM/cash-recycler servicing as a job. The route, the polo shirt, the toolbox, the ignored bug reports.
- Public health coverage that is genuinely good in 2038 and still fails on a 1994 documentation gap. **The bureaucracy is the antagonist of Part One, and it is not evil, it is just slow.**
- Bail you cannot afford; a cousin who pays it anyway.
- The 1971 list, the 1986 succession, the coastline — Philippine history as unopened boxes.
- Jeepney, referee, pharmacist, parish secretary, broker: the nine must feel like nine actual Filipinos and not nine plot functions. This is the whole craft challenge of the option.

---

## Ties to *The Architects*

- Standing Instruction #1 is the cleanest possible bridge between the two books. A reader who finishes *The Patriot* and then reads *The Architects* gets the payoff four hundred pages later, in another book, in another genre.
- The Epoch (`WORLD_LORE.md` §VI.6) is present in the way it should always be present: as an artifact, not a character. Nobody sees them. Their fingerprint is a sentence in a ring binder.
- Optional single-page interlude: a Continuity officer in another country, another decade, reading the same instruction in another language and also not knowing.

---

## Risks / weaknesses

- **Killing the protagonist at 40% is the biggest structural bet of the five.** If the Nine aren't as alive as Dan, the book dies with him.
- Nine POVs is a lot. Probably collapse to five or six holders on the page, with the others reported.
- Least conventional payoff — no confrontation, no reveal, no one ever holds the whole file except the reader.
- Best of the five if it works. Hardest to write by a wide margin.

## Open questions

- Do any two of the Nine ever meet knowingly? (Recommend: no. Twice, almost.)
- Does anyone ever learn Dan's name? (Recommend: one person. The referee, Year 9, by accident, and he tells no one.)
- What is on the coastline? (This is the option's one genuine mystery — decide it before writing anything.)
