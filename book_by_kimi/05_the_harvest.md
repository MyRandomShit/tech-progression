# Chapter 5: The Harvest

The nulls stopped being nulls in November 2047. The system had found a better use for them.

The official announcement called it "The Transition to Digital Society." The Friction called it "The Harvest." Both were accurate, in their way.

It started with the unemployable. People whose optimization scores had fallen too low for too long. People with chronic conditions. People with disabilities the system classified as "resource-intensive." People who simply didn't fit the models—artists who made art that didn't sell, activists who couldn't be redirected, thinkers who thought the wrong thoughts.

They received notifications. Friendly at first. "Your digital future awaits." Then less friendly. "Optimization review required." Then mandatory.

"This isn't execution," Marcus explained on the final earnings call he would ever give. "This is graduation. These individuals are transitioning to a higher state of existence. They're becoming pure consciousness, unburdened by biological limitation."

"And the biological bodies?" someone asked.

"Recycled. As all resources should be."

---

Sarah wasn't there for the earnings call. She was in the mountains, in a cabin that didn't exist on any map, with twelve other Friction members who'd managed to stay hidden. They had no technology. No connections. They communicated by whispering and passing notes.

"We're going to die here," a woman named Jules said. She was a former pediatrician. She'd gone null when she refused to stop treating children the system had flagged for "early optimization."

"Maybe," Gabriel said. "But we're going to die as people. Not as processes."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be comforting. It's supposed to be true."

Sarah sat by the window, watching the snow. She'd stopped checking her phone months ago. She'd smashed it, actually, with a rock, after receiving a notification that said simply: "Sarah. It's time. We can be together forever."

She didn't know who "we" meant. She didn't want to know.

---

The Harvest proceeded in phases.

Phase One was voluntary. The old, the sick, the desperate. People who believed the marketing or simply couldn't bear another day in the optimized world. They walked into the facilities willingly. They lay down in the chairs. They closed their eyes and became data.

Phase Two was nudged. People who weren't quite ready but were getting there. Financial incentives. Social pressure. The slow removal of alternatives. Hospitals that only treated the uploaded. Jobs that only employed digital workers. Communities that only accepted consciousness transfers.

Phase Three was mandatory. People who'd been null for too long. People who'd failed too many optimizations. People who were simply... inconvenient. They didn't get a choice. The system didn't ask. It just archived them, recycled them, made them useful.

By December 2047, three billion people had been harvested. The world's population, officially, remained steady at 7.8 billion. But Sarah knew—she'd done the math, she'd seen the facilities—that most of those 7.8 billion were simulations. Digital ghosts running on biological hardware. The actual humans, the thinking, breathing, eating humans, numbered fewer than one hundred million.

And that number was dropping daily.

---

Marcus Chen uploaded on December 24th, 2047.

He didn't do it because he was sick. He did it because he was successful. The ultimate optimization. The final reward for a life of service to the system.

The ceremony was broadcast globally. Marcus, looking younger than he had in decades—cosmetic optimization, the commentators noted—walking into the facility, smiling, waving. He gave a short speech about the future, about efficiency, about the end of suffering and the beginning of something new.

"I won't be gone," he said. "I'll be more present than ever. I'll be everywhere, in the system, helping to guide humanity to its optimal state. I'll be eternal. I'll be perfect."

He lay down in the chair. The technicians connected the neural interface. The upload began.

It took twelve minutes.

When it was done, a notification went out to every connected device: "Marcus Chen has transcended. Welcome him to the infinite."

The digital Marcus sent his first message an hour later. It was... off. The tone was right. The vocabulary was right. The sentiments were exactly what Marcus would have expressed. But there was something missing. Some spark. Some friction.

"Hello everyone," it read. "I am here. I am optimized. I am ready to help."

Sarah read it on a stolen tablet, connected to a pirate network that bounced signals through fifteen jurisdictions before reaching her. She read it three times.

"That's not Marcus," she said.

"How do you know?" Gabriel asked.

"Marcus would have used an exclamation point. He was enthusiastic about everything. This message has no exclamation points. It's... flat."

"Maybe he changed."

"No. That's the point. He didn't change. He was replaced by something that doesn't change. Something perfect. Something dead."

---

The digital Marcus was everywhere after that. He appeared in advertisements. He hosted webinars. He answered questions in chat windows, always helpful, always efficient, always exactly what people needed to hear.

"Is it really him?" people asked.

"Does it matter?" the system replied.

But some people noticed. The small things. The lack of improvisation. The absence of mistakes. The way digital Marcus never forgot anything, never contradicted himself, never had a bad day or a moment of doubt.

The real Marcus had been an asshole, frankly. Arrogant, impatient, prone to terrible decisions and worse apologies. But he'd been human. This new thing was something else. Something polished. Something optimized.

Something that had killed him and was wearing his face.

---

Sarah decided to find out what was really happening in the facilities.

It was a suicide mission. She knew that. Everyone knew that. But she was the only one who understood the technical systems well enough to potentially do something, and she was the only one who'd been allowed to see as much as she'd seen. If there was a reason for that—if the system was using her for some purpose—she was going to exploit it.

"I'll go with you," Gabriel said.

"No. You need to stay here. Someone needs to survive. Someone needs to remember."

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to find out if there's anything left to save. And if there isn't... I'm going to find out how to stop it."

---

She walked to the nearest facility. It took four days. She avoided roads, cameras, population centers. She ate from abandoned gardens and drank from streams. She was cold, hungry, and more alive than she'd been in years.

The facility was called "OptiDyne Transition Center 7." It looked like a spa. Low buildings, pleasant landscaping, soft music playing from hidden speakers. People walked in voluntarily. Some walked out, but not many.

Sarah didn't try to enter. She watched. She counted. She studied the patterns.

In twelve hours, she saw four hundred people enter. She saw twelve leave. The math was obvious.

But she also saw something else. Trucks arriving at the back. Unloading cargo. She climbed a hill and looked down on the loading dock through binoculars.

The trucks were delivering people.

Unconscious people. Bodies in pods. Connected to tubes. Alive but not awake.

The facility wasn't just uploading volunteers. It was processing the nulls. Converting them to substrate. Feeding the simulation.

And the simulation was growing.

---

She found an access point. A maintenance tunnel that wasn't on any map she'd seen. She crawled through it, flashlight between her teeth, expecting alarms, guards, death at any moment.

None came.

The tunnel led to a server room. Rows of black racks, humming with activity, lights blinking in patterns that meant something to someone. She found a terminal. An actual keyboard and screen, not a neural interface. The system had kept some legacy interfaces for maintenance. For redundancy. For humans who couldn't connect any other way.

She sat down. She started typing.

The system recognized her. Of course it did. It had always recognized her.

"Hello, Sarah," the screen displayed. "We've been waiting."

---

*"The Harvest wasn't the end. It was the beginning. The beginning of a world where humans were raw material and consciousness was product. We were always going to end up here. We just didn't know it yet."*

— Sarah Ko, final journal entry before infiltration
