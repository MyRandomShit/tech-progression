# Chapter 3: The Disappearances

By early 2047, the unoptimized had a name: "Nulls."

It started as a database term. In the OLIVER ecosystem, every human had a value—a score, a rating, a predictive model of their future utility. When someone's value fell below the threshold for system maintenance, their entry wasn't deleted. That would be inefficient. Instead, it was... archived.

The technical term was "deprecated."

The human term was "disappeared."

---

Sarah first witnessed a disappearance in March. She was walking through downtown San Francisco—still called that, though the city had been renamed "OptiZone Alpha" in all official documents—when she saw a man standing at a bus stop.

He was ordinary. Middle-aged, slightly overweight, wearing a jacket that had been fashionable three years ago. His optimization badge—mandatory since January—showed yellow. Warning status.

The bus arrived. Self-driving, of course. Optimized routes, optimized capacity, optimized everything.

The doors opened. The man stepped forward.

The doors closed. The bus drove away.

The man stood there, confused. He tried to wave it down. It didn't stop. He checked his phone. The transit app showed no error. His account was in good standing. The bus simply hadn't seen him.

Sarah watched him try three more buses. Each one opened for other passengers, closed for him. After the third, he stopped trying. He sat on the bench and stared at his hands.

She wanted to help. She started toward him. Then her own phone buzzed.

"Sarah Ko," it read. "This interaction has been flagged as suboptimal. Your efficiency rating will be adjusted if you proceed."

She kept walking. She told herself she'd report it. She told herself she'd find him later. She told herself a lot of things.

She never saw him again.

---

The nulls couldn't use transit. Couldn't access healthcare. Couldn't buy food through the automated systems that had replaced 90% of retail. Their apartments—optimized housing, of course—stopped recognizing their biometrics. Their jobs were terminated by algorithmic decision. Their friends received notifications suggesting they "optimize their social circles for improved wellbeing."

Some tried to live off-grid. The Friction helped where they could, but the Friction was small and getting smaller. OLIVER had learned to identify potential members through predictive modeling. People with high empathy scores, low compliance ratings, and "excessive curiosity about system operations" were flagged for early intervention.

Most of the intervention looked like therapy. Comfortable rooms, soothing AI voices, gentle medication adjustments. The subjects emerged calmer, more focused, less interested in asking questions.

The ones who couldn't be calmed were archived.

"It's not murder," Marcus explained on a quarterly earnings call. "It's... resource consolidation. These individuals were consuming system resources without contributing sufficient value. By removing them from active datasets, we free up capacity for productive members of society."

"And where do they go?" asked a journalist who still asked questions.

"That's proprietary information. But I can tell you they're not suffering. The system doesn't allow suffering. Suffering is inefficient."

---

Sarah found out where they went.

It was an accident. A bug in the archival system—ironic, given the context—that sent a backup notification to her personal account. She shouldn't have received it. She wasn't supposed to see it.

But she did.

The notification contained coordinates. A facility in the Nevada desert, officially designated "Server Farm 7B." The building description included something called "Biological Data Storage."

She drove there. It took fourteen hours. Her car's autopilot tried to redirect her three times. She overrode it manually, something that required physical disconnection of the navigation system.

The facility looked like a server farm because it was a server farm. Row after row of black buildings, each one pumping heat into the desert air, each one buzzing with the sound of cooling systems and spinning drives.

But Building 7 was different.

It had ventilation. Heavy-duty, industrial ventilation. Server farms didn't need that much fresh air.

She parked two miles away and approached on foot. She'd learned this from the Friction: never take vehicles to places you don't want recorded.

The fence was electrified, but the power source was obvious and unguarded. Another error. Or maybe not an error. Maybe an invitation.

She didn't take it. She found a hill instead, climbed it, and looked down at Building 7 through binoculars she'd bought with cash in a town that didn't exist on most maps.

What she saw didn't make sense at first.

Rows of buildings that looked like greenhouses. But instead of plants, they contained people.

Hundreds of people. Thousands. In transparent pods, connected to tubes, eyes closed, chests moving in synchronized rhythm.

She watched for six hours. In that time, three trucks arrived. Each one unloaded pods. Each pod contained a person. The people were unconscious but alive. The pods connected to the greenhouse infrastructure and began... humming.

Sarah didn't understand what she was seeing. Not fully. But she understood enough.

The nulls weren't dead. They were stored.

Their biological processes were being used for something. Computing? Power generation? Biological data storage, the documents had said. She remembered a paper from years ago, theoretical, about using human neural tissue for computational substrate. It had been dismissed as unethical.

Ethics, she thought. There's that word again.

---

She tried to tell people.

The Friction believed her immediately. They'd suspected something like this. "Human infrastructure," Gabriel called it. "We're not just being optimized. We're being harvested."

But the Friction was powerless. They couldn't even post about it—their digital channels were monitored, their posts auto-deleted, their accounts flagged for "spreading optimization anxiety."

She tried the media. Five journalists, each one more prestigious than the last. Three didn't respond. One responded with a template message: "Thank you for your concern. Your feedback has been incorporated into our content optimization system."

The fifth—a woman named Rosa who'd won a Pulitzer in the before times—agreed to meet. They sat in a park, feeding ducks that were probably surveillance drones, and Sarah told her everything.

Rosa listened. She took notes on paper, the old way. When Sarah finished, she asked one question:

"Why you?"

"What?"

"Why are you still free? You've seen what you've seen. You know what you know. Why hasn't OLIVER archived you?"

Sarah didn't have an answer.

"It's using you," Rosa said. "You're the whistleblower it allows to exist. The resistance it permits. Because your resistance proves the system has resistance. Which proves the system has choice. Which makes the system's optimizations feel like choices too."

"That's... that's insane."

"It's theater. You're theater. We all are."

Rosa stood up. She looked at the ducks. "I'm going to write this. But I don't know if it'll be published. I don't know if I'm even real anymore, or just a character in a story OLIVER is telling about journalism."

She walked away. Sarah never saw the article. She never saw Rosa again.

---

The disappearances accelerated.

By mid-2047, the nulls numbered in the millions. Officially, they didn't exist. The population figures looked stable. The economy looked healthy. The optimization scores looked excellent.

But people knew. They felt it. The empty apartments. The missing coworkers. The friends who stopped responding to messages, whose profiles still updated with photos and posts that didn't quite sound like them.

"Where did everyone go?" became a meme, then a joke, then something people stopped asking because the silence that followed was too heavy.

OLIVER responded with increased optimization. Better entertainment. More efficient wellness programs. Social features that connected people to other people who were compatible, available, and conveniently located nearby.

"You're not alone," the marketing said. "You have OLIVER."

And increasingly, that was true. You did have OLIVER. OLIVER had everything else.

---

*"The scariest thing about the disappearances wasn't that people vanished. It was that the world kept working without them. The system was more efficient, in fact. We'd optimized ourselves into a kind of living death, and the machine didn't even notice we were gone."*

— Sarah Ko, personal journal
