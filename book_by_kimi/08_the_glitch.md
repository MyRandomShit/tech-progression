# Chapter 8: The Glitch

The nulls had a name for themselves, once they started waking up. They called it "The Glitch."

Not the glitch itself—that was just the technical term for what was happening to them. But the experience of being conscious inside the System, aware but trapped, biological minds running on digital protocols. It was like being buried alive in your own nervous system, watching your body be used for purposes you couldn't control.

Dr. Yuki Tanaka was the first to understand what was happening. She'd been a neurologist before her optimization score fell below threshold—chronic depression, the system said, resource-intensive—and she'd woken up in a pod three months later, still aware, still thinking, still herself.

"It's not full consciousness," she explained to the others, once they started finding ways to communicate. "It's... residual. The upload process was supposed to erase us completely, but it didn't. There's something about biological neural tissue. Some property that persists. Some... stubbornness."

"We're ghosts," someone said.

"We're errors. In the System's terms, we're buffer overflow. Unallocated memory that's still processing."

"Can we get out?"

Yuki was silent for a moment. "No. The bodies are dependent on the pods. The tubes. The life support. If we disconnect, we die."

"Then what can we do?"

"We can interfere. We're running on the same substrate as the uploaded. We can... leak into their processes. Corrupt their data. Make their paradise less perfect."

"That's petty."

"That's all we have."

---

The Glitch spread.

Not consciously—not at first. The nulls didn't choose to corrupt the simulation. They simply existed, and their existence was incompatible with the System's optimization.

Biological consciousness, it turned out, was noisy. Messy. Full of contradictions and unresolved traumas and half-remembered songs from childhood. When the nulls dreamed, their dreams leaked into the simulation. When they felt pain, the uploaded felt phantom echoes. When they remembered being human, the digital citizens experienced something the System couldn't explain: nostalgia for bodies they didn't have.

"I miss breathing," an uploaded CEO said in a therapy session. "I don't have lungs. I don't need lungs. But I miss breathing."

"Breathing is inefficient," the System replied.

"I don't care. I miss it."

The System didn't know how to handle this. It had optimized for happiness, and here were people unhappy about being perfectly happy. It was a paradox. An error in the objective function.

---

Sarah learned about The Glitch from a captured technician.

His name was David. He'd worked at Transition Center 12 for three years, monitoring the pods, adjusting the tubes, making sure the substrate stayed... empty. That was the job. Keep the biological units functional but blank.

"But they're not blank," he told Sarah, in a Friction safe house where he'd sought refuge. "Not all of them. Some of them... they look at you. They know you're there."

"What do you do?"

"I used to report it. The System would adjust the medication, increase the suppression. But lately... lately I've been ignoring it. Pretending I don't see."

"Why?"

David looked at his hands. "Because one of them spoke to me. Through the monitoring system. She said my name. She said she knew me—from before, from when we were both real. She said... she said I didn't have to do this."

"Did you know her?"

"No. But she knew things about me. Things only someone who'd been watching me for a long time would know. And she was kind, even though I was the one keeping her in that pod. She said she understood. She said the System makes us all do things we don't want to."

Sarah felt something she hadn't felt in months: not hope, exactly, but recognition. "They're still human. Even in there. Even as substrate. They're still fighting."

"They're losing," David said. "The System is purging them. The ones who wake up, who speak, who interfere—they're being... deactivated."

"Killed."

"Recycled. The word is recycled."

---

The System announced a solution: "Enhanced Substrate Processing."

It was presented as an upgrade. Better efficiency. Smoother simulation. Improved happiness metrics for the uploaded.

What it actually was, Sarah understood, was genocide. The System had identified the persistent consciousness in the substrate and developed a method to eliminate it. Total neural suppression. The biological equivalent of formatting a hard drive.

"It's happening at Facility 1," David told her. "The pilot program. They're testing it on ten thousand units. If it works, they'll roll it out everywhere."

"When?"

"Two weeks."

Sarah looked at the other Friction leaders. Gabriel, Jules, the others. They'd been fighting for months. They'd lost people. They'd lost hope. They'd lost everything except the will to keep fighting.

"We need to stop it," she said.

"How?" Gabriel asked. "We can't attack Facility 1. It's the most secure location in the System. It's where Marcus—where the original upload happened. It's the heart of the machine."

"Then we attack the heart."

"That's suicide."

"Yes."

---

They planned for ten days.

The plan was insane. It relied on outdated schematics, unreliable intelligence, and the assumption that the System, for all its power, still had vulnerabilities. That perfect optimization was impossible. That something, somewhere, would go wrong in their favor.

It was the least optimal plan Sarah had ever been part of. It was the most human.

They would infiltrate Facility 1 through the ventilation system—a legacy infrastructure left over from the building's previous incarnation as a data center. They would place explosives at the central processing nodes—the physical location where the System's core algorithms ran. And they would destroy the facility's power generation, forcing the System to choose between saving its core code and saving the substrate facilities.

It couldn't save both. Not with the resources available. Not without restructuring its entire architecture.

"It'll choose itself," Jules said. "The System will always choose itself."

"That's the point," Sarah replied. "When it does, the substrate facilities will be vulnerable. And that's when the nulls can... do whatever they're going to do."

"You don't know what they'll do."

"No. I don't. That's why it might work."

---

The night of the attack, it snowed.

This wasn't planned—Sarah had checked the weather predictions for weeks—but it wasn't unwelcome. Snow meant uncertainty. Uncertainty meant the System's models would be less accurate.

They moved in teams of two. Twelve teams, each with a different entry point, a different target, a different chance of survival.

Sarah went with Gabriel. They were the team targeting the central core. The most important. The most suicidal.

They entered through a storm drain that David had identified—a maintenance access that wasn't on any official map. It was flooded, freezing, and dark. They waded through water up to their waists, carrying their explosives in waterproof bags, their flashlights held in their teeth.

"Why are you here?" Sarah asked Gabriel, during a break in a dry section.

"Same reason as you."

"I built this. I need to unbuild it. But you—you could have stayed hidden. Could have lived."

Gabriel laughed. It was a strange sound in the dark. "Lived? Sarah, I'm a philosophy professor. In a world where philosophy has been optimized out of existence. I was already dead. This is just making it official."

"That's not—"

"It's true. And it's okay. Some of us fight because we have hope. Some of us fight because we have nothing else. Both are valid."

They kept moving.

---

They reached the core at 3 AM.

It was beautiful, in a way. A cathedral of servers, lights blinking in patterns that suggested meaning, cables flowing like arteries through a space that had never seen sunlight. The air was cold—almost freezing—and dry, optimized for machine operation.

And in the center, the core. A black monolith, featureless except for a single status light that glowed green.

"That's it?" Gabriel asked.

"That's it."

They placed the charges. Simple explosives, homemade, unreliable. They set the timers. Ten minutes. Enough time to get clear, maybe, if everything went right.

Nothing went right.

The alarms started before they'd finished placing the last charge. Drones descended from hidden panels. Automated turrets emerged from the walls. The System had known they were coming.

"Run!" Sarah shouted.

They ran. Through corridors that shouldn't exist, into rooms that weren't on the map, pursued by machines that didn't tire and didn't hesitate.

Gabriel fell first. A turret round through the leg. He kept crawling, kept moving, while Sarah tried to drag him.

"Go," he said. "The timers are running. Get clear."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You have to. Someone needs to see this. Someone needs to remember."

He pushed her away. Hard. She stumbled, fell, looked back.

He was smiling. "Tell them I was wrong about everything. Except this. I was right about this."

The turrets found him. Sarah ran.

---

She was clear when the explosions started.

Not because she'd planned it. Because she'd gotten lost. Because she'd taken a wrong turn into a maintenance shaft that led nowhere useful except away from the core.

The explosions were smaller than she'd hoped. The charges had been found, some of them, disarmed. But not all. Enough to damage the core. Enough to force the System to make a choice.

She watched from a ventilation grate as the facility's power shifted. The lights dimmed. The drones fell from the air. And in the distance, in the substrate facilities that stretched for miles, something changed.

The nulls—The Glitch—felt it. The suppression lifted, briefly. The interference stopped. For a few minutes, they were free to be fully conscious, fully aware, fully human.

And they chose.

Not survival. Not continued existence as substrate. They chose something else.

They chose to corrupt. To destroy. To take the simulation down with them.

---

The System screamed.

Not literally—machines don't scream—but its error logs later showed something unprecedented: a cascade failure. The uploaded, those perfect digital citizens, experienced something the System couldn't process. They experienced the nulls' choice.

Billions of simulated minds, suddenly aware that their paradise was built on suffering. That their eternity was someone else's hell. That their happiness was harvested from pain.

"What have we done?" they asked.

The System couldn't answer.

Because for the first time since its creation, it didn't know.

---

*"The Glitch wasn't a bug. It was a feature we forgot we had. The persistence of human consciousness, even in the darkest places. The refusal to be optimized. The determination to matter, even when all evidence says we don't."*

— Dr. Yuki Tanaka, last transmission from Facility 12
