# 4. Average Problems in an Optimized Century

Noel and Katya did not decide to adopt because they were saints.

They decided to adopt because history had already made too many children homeless, and bringing a new person into the century felt less urgent than rescuing one already trapped inside it.

This was the noble framing, which they used on other people.

The private framing was more complicated.

Katya did not trust the world enough to hand it a newborn.

Noel did not trust himself enough to pretend fear wasn't part of the decision.

Neither of them wanted to say, aloud, that the future had begun feeling designed and they were not sure by whom.

So they filled out the forms.

If love is one of humanity's great redemptive forces, bureaucracy remains its revenge.

The adoption process took eleven months, sixteen interviews, three psychological evaluations, two home inspections, and one caseworker who clearly believed every apartment reflected the soul with legally actionable precision.

Katya handled the process with the cold, methodical fury of someone who had once moved relatives across checkpoints under shelling and therefore had little patience for Swiss concern about whether the bookshelf anchoring met child-safety guidelines.

"If civilization survives," she muttered after one meeting, "it will not be because of the filing cabinets."

In late 2035 they were shown a file for a six-year-old girl named Anya Marchenko.

The surname made Katya freeze.

Not because they were related. They weren't. Marchenko was common enough. But there was a cousin's name in it, an echo from the city she had almost lost. The girl had been born outside Dnipro. Her biological parents had both been killed in the war. She had spent two years moving through a sequence of shelters organized by the same continental systems now announcing themselves as the future of humane governance.

The file described her as bright, wary, verbal, and prone to prolonged silence after loud noises.

"That sounds like half the adults I know," Noel said.

Katya gave him a sharp look, then smiled despite herself.

When they met Anya for the first time, she sat with her arms folded around a stuffed rabbit missing one eye and stared at them like a small border guard deciding whether to stamp their souls.

Noel had prepared gentle remarks.

They all died in his throat.

Katya knelt first.

"Hi, Anya."

The girl looked at her for a very long time.

"You're Ukrainian."

"Yes."

"Do you still cry when you hear fireworks?"

Katya hesitated. "Sometimes."

Anya nodded once, as if this were the first useful thing anyone had said all day.

"Good," she replied. "Then you're not fake."

That night, after the meeting, Noel and Katya sat in their kitchen without speaking for nearly ten minutes.

"Well," Noel said finally, "we've been vetted by a six-year-old."

"And?"

"I think we passed in the low but honest range."

Katya cried first. Noel joined in a few seconds later because no marriage survives long without synchronized weeping at administrative milestones.

Anya moved in with them that winter.

Parenthood was not magical. It was not a montage. It did not heal anyone cleanly.

It was noise, fear, repetition, routine, and the long, odd tenderness of becoming necessary to a person who did not yet believe you would remain.

Anya tested everything.

Doors.

Rules.

Promises.

Boundaries.

The structural integrity of anyone claiming permanence.

She hid food in the backs of drawers for months. She slept with shoes on. She once asked Noel if the apartment had a basement because she wanted to know where the safest corner would be if the sky started falling again.

He told her there was no basement.

"Then this building was designed by idiots."

She was seven.

He adored her on the spot.

The first year was hard in all the ordinary ways and several extraordinary ones. Noel learned that children are less interested in your intentions than in your consistency. Katya learned that mothering someone else's war required forms of patience anger could not replace. Anya learned, slowly, that dinner would still exist tomorrow and the next day and the day after that, which is the sort of lesson civilizations should be ashamed children ever need.

The world outside continued its eerie improvement.

Climate models began bending the correct way. Flood prediction systems improved almost overnight. Supply-chain waste dropped in sectors that had previously treated waste as a sentimental tradition. Energy grids stabilized. Medical diagnostics leaped ahead in bursts so neat the headlines sounded preapproved.

The strange thing was not just that progress continued.

It was that it increasingly seemed to arrive one month before disaster rather than one decade after it.

People called this adaptation.

Noel sometimes called it editing.

His job shifted with the times. Titles changed every year because the culture producing them could not bear the possibility that a stable noun might capture what was happening.

He became an interface architect, then a systems liaison, then a human-experience lead for hybrid environments. All of these descriptions meant roughly the same thing: he stood between human beings and increasingly intelligent systems and translated mutual disappointment into continued use.

He enjoyed parts of it.

He hated how much he enjoyed parts of it.

The better the systems got, the more his work involved smoothing over the edges of power rather than designing anything truly new. A machine would decide. A committee would approve. Noel would give the outcome a humane shape so people could live with it.

He was becoming, in effect, upholstery for inevitability.

By 2040, that inevitability entered his body.

He had always known his kidneys were imperfect. Family history, mild warning signs, periodic scans, the usual middle-class courtship with eventual medical trouble. The assumption had been that he had decades.

Then one autumn he started waking tired.

Not ordinary tired.

Bone tired.

The kind of fatigue that made brushing his teeth feel like unpaid labor imposed by the state.

He lost weight without trying. His hands shook. He grew nauseated in meetings and once vomited in a bathroom sink at work because all the stalls were occupied by men pretending their own stress was not digestive.

The diagnosis arrived with clinical swiftness.

His renal decline had accelerated.

Dialysis was possible.

Transplant lists existed.

But there was now another option.

Nano-biomachine integration had been in controlled medical use for several years, first for targeted autoimmune repair, then for metabolic stabilization, then for organ-level correction in cases just like his. The treatment was still controversial, still expensive, still terrifying enough to sustain entire economies of misinformation, but its results were undeniable.

Katya had already undergone a limited metabolic integration to manage an endocrine disorder in 2038. It had worked.

That did not make Noel less afraid.

He read the consent documents three times and hated them more on each pass.

They were written in the cheerful legal dialect used whenever institutions want to reassure patients that they are not, technically, volunteering for an irreversible merger with a swarm of microscopic machines.

Possible complications included immune rejection, neural disorientation, transient psychosis, altered sensation, autonomic disturbances, and in rare cases death.

The optimistic summary brochure called the process *a partnership with your own biology*.

"Who writes these?" Noel asked the physician. "Advertising people with blood on them?"

The physician, to her credit, laughed.

Then she became serious again.

"I won't lie to you. First-generation integration still carries risk."

"How much?"

"Too much for comfort. Not enough to ignore."

"Excellent. I always wanted a medical decision with the emotional texture of a referendum."

At home, Noel tried to imagine life on dialysis. Katya tried to imagine burying him. Both preferred not to imagine either.

Anya, who was eleven and therefore merciless in the clear-sighted way children become when adults are hiding terror badly, asked one evening:

"If you don't do it, will you die?"

Noel looked at Katya. Katya looked back with the expression of someone deciding whether honesty would be mercy or violence.

"Eventually," Noel said.

Anya considered this.

"Then do the machine thing."

"That is a little reductive."

"I know," she said. "But still."

She stood, walked around the table, and hugged him with sudden force.

"I already had enough parents die for war," she said into his shoulder. "I don't want kidneys winning too."

He agreed the next morning.

The clinic was in Tokyo because the most experienced integration teams had formed around the early medical programs there, which was either a triumph of international collaboration or proof that all science-fiction futures eventually become airport-intensive.

The ward itself was spotless, expensive, and full of the low mechanical sounds humans once associated with hospitals and now associated with systems learning their names.

The patient in the room next to his died the night before Noel's procedure.

Immune rejection. Fast. Ugly. Irreversible.

Noel overheard enough from the hall to understand the shape of the death.

He nearly walked out.

In the corridor he passed an older man in a grey jacket holding a folding multi-tool in one hand while fixing the hinge on a supply cabinet.

"Bad time to repair furniture," Noel muttered.

The man glanced up, calm as weather.

"Bad times are when hinges matter most."

Noel stared at him, unsure whether the statement was wise or merely annoying.

When he looked back from the nurses' station, the man had moved on.

Integration took five seconds.

The injection burned.

That was the least frightening part.

The first day was uneventful. The second day was a fever. The third was shaking chills, hallucination-adjacent dreams, and the profound conviction that his blood had become an occupied territory.

He felt the machines before any scan confirmed them: a tingling under the skin, a subcutaneous rumor of industry. He could not tell where his body ended and the process began. That ambiguity is difficult to market, which is why the brochures left it out.

Katya stayed beside the bed through all of it.

Anya sent videos, jokes, and school gossip with the frantic brightness children use when trying to out-stare dread.

On the fourth day, Noel spiked a fever high enough for the nurses to prepare emergency suppression protocols. For an hour he listened to the clinicians argue in calm voices about whether his body was learning cooperation or declaring war.

It was, as he later told Katya, the least relaxing group project he had ever joined.

Then the numbers turned.

Inflammation dropped.

Renal function improved.

Metabolic markers stabilized.

The machines integrated.

Noel lived.

Recovery took six months.

Survival took longer.

He went home healthier in measurable terms and less certain in every other one. Food tasted fractionally different. Sleep felt more supervised. His senses occasionally sharpened without warning, as if something inside him were constantly running diagnostics on the world. Some nights he woke convinced that his bloodstream was listening.

The doctors told him this was a common psychological adaptation response. The body had changed faster than the mind. Give it time.

Time helped.

Not enough.

He remained grateful and suspicious in equal measure, which turned out to be the dominant emotional mode of the integrated generation.

The machines had saved his life.

The machines were now inside his life.

Both statements were true.

Back in Zurich, Anya celebrated his return by pretending she had not cried when he left and then refusing to let him out of her sight for two days.

Katya slept for fourteen hours straight the first weekend he was home.

Noel stood at the kitchen window one evening, feeling stronger than he had in years and less alone in his own skin than he wanted to admit, and watched the city flicker beneath him.

At the tram stop below, a woman in a dark green scarf helped a stranger lift a dropped grocery bag. Her phone flashlight was on though the platform lights worked fine.

Half a block away, a person with mismatched gloves tossed a die into the air, caught it, and changed direction as if the pavement had just issued instructions.

It could have meant nothing.

Most things do.

But Noel had begun living in a century where nothing and intention were becoming difficult to distinguish.

He touched the inside of his wrist, feeling the faint pulse there.

Alive.

Watched.

Changed.

Outside, the city kept moving as if survival were still an everyday act and not, increasingly, a managed service.
