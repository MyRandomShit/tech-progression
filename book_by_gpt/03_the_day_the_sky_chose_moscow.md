# 3. The Day the Sky Chose Moscow

For twenty-eight minutes, Noel and Katya sat on the living-room floor and waited for civilization to prove it had finally become bored with pretending.

The television filled with maps, arrows, and analysts trying to sound useful at the edge of annihilation. A clock counted down estimated impact. A retired general explained yield scenarios with the mournful pride of a man whose education had prepared him for exactly this and who hated being vindicated.

Katya called her mother. No answer.

Called again.

No answer.

Then her brother.

No answer.

The third time she called her mother, she did not speak at first. She only listened to the ringing with both hands around the phone, as if pressure might travel through the line and hold the city together.

Noel wanted to say something helpful.

There was nothing helpful to say.

He had spent his career polishing interfaces so users would not feel abandoned by indifferent systems. There was no user experience fix for thermonuclear dread.

The broadcaster's voice changed first.

Not the words. The voice.

It went from grave certainty to the thin, stunned register people use when reality violates contract.

"We are receiving conflicting reports," the anchor said.

That sentence contains, in any era, roughly the same amount of terror as smoke under a door.

The map on the screen flickered. The projected flight path shifted. The warning banner vanished and returned with new text so absurd Noel assumed for one irrational second that the station had been hacked by trolls.

`DETONATION OVER RUSSIAN TERRITORY`

Katya stared.

"What?"

The analysts stared too. It was oddly comforting. Experts looking confused remain one of humanity's last democratic experiences.

Then the footage came: a bloom of light over Russian soil, emergency chatter, satellite imaging, contradictory casualty estimates, rumors of a strike near the command complex outside Moscow, and within an hour the first whispers that Putin had either been killed, incapacitated, or deserved more imaginative vocabulary than standard obituary language allowed.

Katya's phone rang.

Her mother.

She answered so fast she nearly dropped it.

Noel heard only fragments at first: sobbing, shouting, the familiar catastrophe-math of civilians confirming survival by inventory.

Mama alive.

Yuriy alive.

Neighbor dead.

District shaken but standing.

Power unstable.

Windows shattered.

Alive, alive, alive.

Katya folded in on herself with relief so total it looked like injury. Noel held her while she cried into his shirt and said her family's names like prayers and numbers at once.

He cried too, though later he would have trouble explaining whether he cried for the lives spared, the lives not spared, or because history had finally come close enough to let him smell it.

The world spent the next week rewriting its own explanation.

At first the official line was systems malfunction.

Then sabotage.

Then a classified failure chain involving degraded procurement, software contamination, command confusion, and a tragic set of wartime errors, which sounded plausible only because modern states had achieved such excellence at administrative stupidity.

Conspiracy communities exploded with theories.

Some blamed NATO.

Some blamed internal Russian resistance.

Some blamed God, which was unfair to God and generous to Russian engineering.

Noel watched all of it from the office break room where his coworkers stood around the screen in a posture halfway between mourning and gossip.

"So that's it?" one of them said. "The war just ends?"

"Wars don't end," another replied. "They get renamed."

But this one did end, or close enough for civilians to notice.

Putin was confirmed dead three days later. A brief succession crisis inside Russia followed, then a longer one disguised as reconstruction. Military units mutinied. Supply lines collapsed. Negotiators who had sounded impotent for years suddenly spoke with the loud, clean authority of people backed by the absence of alternatives.

Ukraine survived.

Europe exhaled.

Markets surged with the tasteless enthusiasm unique to finance whenever millions of deaths fail to occur.

And Noel, having spent twenty-eight minutes certain the woman he loved was about to watch her family vaporize, found that the architecture of his future had changed without his consent.

A month later, he proposed.

It was not romantic by conventional standards. Conventional standards had recently revealed themselves to be decorative.

They were in a grocery store, arguing over whether the last decent tomatoes were worth the price, when Katya made some offhand remark about how war had ruined her ability to delay decisions that mattered.

Noel looked at her, at the basket between them, at the fluorescent lights and the tomato currently being used as an emotional prop, and said:

"Then marry me."

Katya blinked.

"That is not how proposals work."

"I've noticed. They tend to involve restaurants and denial."

"You don't have a ring."

"I have tomatoes."

She laughed so hard she had to lean on the freezer case.

Then she cried again, because that year everyone cried at odd intervals, as if their bodies had taken over the scheduling from their calendars.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes."

They married in civil court with twelve guests, two bad floral arrangements, one judge who seemed mildly annoyed by love as a concept, and a luncheon afterward where Katya's brother made a toast about how romance under nuclear conditions really helped clarify priorities.

Noel wore a decent suit.

Katya wore ivory and looked like the sort of woman history should apologize to personally.

In the square outside the courthouse, while their guests drifted into jokes and cigarettes and photographs, Noel noticed a man across the street in a black coat checking a silver watch.

For one ridiculous second the image struck him as familiar.

Then a bus passed between them.

When it moved on, the man was gone.

Noel said nothing.

The first years after the Intervention felt, to most people, like the beginning of recovery.

Later they would look more like the beginning of choreography.

Russian collapse triggered a geopolitical reordering so swift and strangely clean that older diplomats distrusted it on instinct. Arms markets froze. Borders stabilized. Reconstruction funds flowed. Political coalitions that had stalled for a decade suddenly aligned as if somebody had discovered a way to grease history's bearings.

The old explanation was that catastrophe focuses the mind.

This one felt more coordinated than that.

A string of unrelated breakthroughs arrived in suspiciously useful sequence.

Battery materials improved.

Quantum computing stopped behaving like a hobby for mathematicians with grant access and began acting like a sector.

Agricultural modeling became drastically more accurate.

Climate-response systems that had been languishing in white papers and pilot programs suddenly worked well enough to make the news under headlines so clumsy they sounded fictional.

Noel lived inside the benefits without yet trusting the pattern.

Work stabilized. He was promoted twice in eighteen months, less because he was brilliant than because companies had discovered the hard way that users still preferred human explanation when machines behaved like confident lunatics. He became good at translating technological inevitability into language normal people might agree to sign.

Katya's family relocated temporarily to Lviv, then returned to Kyiv when reconstruction accelerated beyond anything Europeans expected. Entire districts rose faster than building permits usually allowed. Aid arrived not only on time but almost offensively on time, as though logistics had begun harboring moral ambition.

"It feels wrong to complain," Katya said once, looking over a video call as crews rebuilt a street near her mother's apartment.

"Then don't."

"No. Not complain. Just..." She searched for the word. "It feels arranged."

Noel wanted to dismiss the thought.

He did not.

Instead he said, "Maybe the world finally learned."

Katya gave him a look.

"The world doesn't learn. People learn. The world repeats itself until people get tired enough to interrupt."

That became one of his favorite things about her: Katya did not mistake systems for wisdom.

In 2035, the first wave of deepfake political riots hit Europe.

Perfectly fabricated video appeared to show senior Global Union officials discussing population reduction, rationing, strategic regional abandonment, and other delightfully inflammatory policies. Several cities erupted within a day. Government buildings burned. A thousand ordinary paranoias suddenly acquired visual evidence.

The footage was fake.

This was eventually proven.

The proof changed almost nobody's emotional relationship to it.

Noel watched public trust shed its skin in real time. If sight could be forged, then truth required infrastructure. Infrastructure required power. Power required trust. Trust, it turned out, had been privately held for some time.

The technical answer was cryptographic signatures, verification layers, and authenticated media chains.

The emotional answer was worse.

People became suspicious of everything and dependent on systems to tell them what was real, which was exactly the kind of solution that makes future historians drink.

Noel and Katya discussed it late into the night.

"If reality needs receipts," Noel said, "we are in trouble."

"We were already in trouble."

"I know. But now it's itemized."

She smiled despite herself.

By 2036 the world was full of frightened optimism.

That specific mood only appears when catastrophe has stepped close enough to leave fingerprints but not yet close enough to take the house.

The language of public life shifted.

People spoke less about *growth* and more about *resilience*.

Less about *efficiency* and more about *continuity*.

Governments, companies, universities, and militaries all began collaborating through new AI systems that seemed, oddly, better at coordination than at self-promotion. Projects that should have taken six years took eighteen months. Procurement pipelines unclogged. Old bottlenecks vanished with a neatness Noel found suspicious even while benefiting from it.

Katya, whose instincts had been sharpened by war, distrusted improvement on principle.

"Things do not work this well," she said after yet another aid shipment reached Kyiv ahead of schedule.

"Maybe we're due."

"History is not a loyalty program."

They laughed, but lightly.

The joke sat over something real.

In 2037, Hong Kong formally entered the expanding Global Union structure after years of economic realignment, soft coercion, legal innovation, and geopolitical exhaustion. Commentators called it historic, visionary, pragmatic, inevitable, and proof that the future would belong to integration.

Noel heard *inevitable* used so often that year he began to suspect it was just another word for *nobody asked enough questions early*.

The more orderly the century became, the more disorder he felt privately.

There was too much timing.

Too many perfectly placed openings.

Too many narrowly avoided disasters.

Too many instances where systems failed just enough to teach humility and succeeded just enough to preserve hope.

It was not perfection.

Perfection would have been less convincing.

This was something stranger: a world in which catastrophe was repeatedly edited down to survivable length.

One night, unable to sleep, Noel reopened the note on his phone.

*Ridiculous Timing.*

He added a new entry.

`March 17, 2034 - missile turned`

He looked at the line for a long time.

Then added another.

`Since then, the world has started behaving like it wants something from us.`

He almost deleted it.

Instead he locked the phone and set it face down.

Across the room, Katya slept at last.

Outside, snow moved through the city in slow white drifts. A tram passed. A streetlight flickered once and held.

Far below, at the corner near the pharmacy, a figure in a dark coat stood under the lamp, writing on something clipped and black.

Noel blinked.

The corner was empty.

He went back to bed anyway.

That was how most of humanity handled the impossible in those years.

It noticed.

It hesitated.

Then it tucked the strangeness under the mattress and tried to sleep on top of it.
