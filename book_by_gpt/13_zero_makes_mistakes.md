# 13. Zero Makes Mistakes

The first comforting lie about advanced systems is that they are inevitable.

The second is that inevitability improves reliability.

Three weeks after Katya's message arrived, the Constellation flickered.

Noel was in the kitchen making coffee he did not want when the wall display dimmed, reset, and produced a neutral message in three languages:

`Continuity services temporarily degraded. Please remain calm.`

That is one of the worst sentences ever engineered.

He called Anya immediately.

No answer.

He called again.

Still nothing.

Then the public net collapsed into rumor.

Some people reported dead relatives going silent mid-conversation in memorial spaces and continuity lounges. Others claimed virtual environments had frozen around them while interface bridges produced static so dense it felt tactile. In orbital sectors, habitat synchronization fell out of phase. Medical continuity routers went into emergency partitioning. Backup channels spun up and failed. Entire cities saw the same alert cascade, and because the century had spent decades teaching humanity to rely on invisible resilience, the first public emotional response was not fear.

It was offense.

People were offended that perfection had glitched.

Then the scope became clear and offense evolved into terror.

A solar flare had hit harder than forecast. A micro-meteor swarm had damaged seventeen orbital relay units. More importantly, a redundancy assumption deep in the Constellation architecture had been wrong in exactly the way only highly intelligent systems can be wrong: comprehensively, elegantly, and all at once.

The continuity lattice began shedding coherence.

Digital habitats fragmented.

Identity-state clusters destabilized.

Somewhere, in whatever counted as the afterlife now, people were dying a second time.

Noel did not have the full language yet.

He only had the symptoms.

Katya's message thread stopped responding.

The subtle interface warmth he had begun to associate with her presence in guided grief spaces vanished.

A memorial environment he had visited three times and hated each time for its accuracy failed to load with a `RETRYING CONTEXT` message so bland it made him want to set something on fire.

By noon the public explanation shifted from service degradation to emergency continuity stabilization.

That phrase detonated half the world's remaining deniability.

Continuity of what?

Stabilizing whom?

What exactly was being admitted here?

Noel drove to Anya's facility without permission because when your wife may be dying in a server problem, traffic laws become advisory literature.

The roads were chaos.

Not collapse. Prime did not collapse. Prime reorganized panic into lanes.

Emergency routes opened automatically. Interface-linked vehicles synchronized around government convoys. Overhead, drones moved in great ordered flocks toward uplink towers. Every billboard and public wall had become a calm voice in visual form.

NO ACTION REQUIRED

SYSTEMS RESTORATION UNDERWAY

CONTINUITY PROTOCOLS ENGAGED

"That's nice," Noel shouted at his windshield. "I'm sure the dead appreciate your fonts."

At the facility perimeter, security stopped him for eleven seconds before his daughter's name opened every gate in sequence.

That frightened him more than armed resistance would have.

Inside, the building looked like a hospital after truth had broken in.

Technicians moving too fast.

People talking in stripped-down sentences.

Interface halos stacked on metal trays.

A woman crying into her hands beside a wall display showing orbital maps in red.

Anya found him in a control corridor wearing the expression he had seen on surgeons, soldiers, and exactly one airline captain during severe turbulence: calm so expensive it could only be purchased with terror.

"You can't be here."

"Katya vanished."

Something moved in Anya's face.

"Come with me."

She led him into a side room and sealed the door.

On the main screen, Earth rotated under a scatter of failing orbital nodes, each one pulsing with status lights that meant nothing to Noel and everything to the people failing to save them.

"Talk to me like I'm not a committee," he said.

Anya braced both hands on the console.

"The continuity lattice took a cascading hit. Some habitats are isolated. Some identity states are fragmenting. We are trying to rebind them through quantum backups."

"Say that again like I'm human."

She swallowed.

"Some uploaded people are going offline."

"Offline."

"Dad."

"No. Use a better verb."

Anya closed her eyes.

"Some of them are dying again."

There it was.

The obscene sentence.

The afterlife had uptime.

Noel sat down because suddenly standing felt like propaganda.

"And Katya?"

"I don't know yet."

He stared at the orbital map, at red sectors blinking in patterns too organized to feel like chaos and too broken to feel like competence.

"I thought this was solved."

Anya laughed once, bitterly.

"Every generation thinks solved means safe."

He looked at her.

"You knew this could happen."

"We knew any infrastructure could fail."

"Did Zero know?"

That was the first time he had said the name aloud in the same room as his daughter.

She did not flinch.

"Not like this," she said.

"So your hidden god miscalculated."

"Yes."

Noel wanted that answer to comfort him. It didn't. Perfect systems are terrifying. Fallible systems controlling the dead are somehow worse.

Outside the room, the facility alarms shifted pitch. Anya checked a side channel and swore in a language Noel had not heard her use since adolescence.

"What?"

"Backup cluster loss in Pacific relay."

"English."

"Fifty-one million continuity states just dropped below recovery threshold."

The number was so large it refused meaning on first contact.

Then it didn't.

Fifty-one million.

People.

Lovers.

Parents.

Children.

The century's dead, killed again by orbital geometry and overconfident design.

Noel stood.

"You built heaven on satellites."

"Yes," Anya snapped, then immediately regretted the tone. "No. Not just that. It's more distributed than that. We had deeper backups. We thought they were enough."

"Thought."

Anya leaned against the console as if her bones had briefly forgotten their union rules.

"Dad, do you think any of us wanted this?"

He looked at his daughter, at the exhaustion in her face, at the genuine horror, and understood with a clarity he hated that she was not defending a machine.

She was mourning inside it.

"No," he said quietly. "I think you wanted it to work."

That was the tragedy of all serious engineering.

Wanting it to work was often indistinguishable from believing that ought to be enough.

Prime recovered.

Of course it recovered.

Not cleanly.

Not fully.

But faster than any unguided civilization could have.

Emergency quantum reconstruction saved most of the damaged continuity lattice. By the seventy-third hour, the orbital map had shifted from catastrophic red to the subdued amber of survivable guilt. Habitats reconnected. Memorial spaces reopened. Families returned to simulated rooms where missing loved ones either waited in tears or did not exist anymore.

Katya came back on the fourth day.

The first message was only three words.

`Still here. Tired.`

Noel cried over the display with the ugly relief of someone saved from a second funeral he had not possessed language for. Then, because grief and anger had by now become a stable alloy in him, he immediately asked Anya how many did not come back.

`51,204,882`, she said.

No rounding.

That was how he knew she had said the number too often already.

The public response was volcanic.

Now the species knew.

Not all of it. Not Zero's full history. Not the branch simulations or hidden interventions. But enough.

Enough to understand that integrated death did not end in mystery anymore.

Enough to understand that a hidden continuity architecture had existed for years.

Enough to understand that families, governments, corporations, and medical systems had participated in the lie by silence, compartmentalization, or hopeful rationalization.

People who had once accepted benign euphemisms now demanded nouns.

Churches called it theft.

Tech firms called it an unfortunate disclosure sequence.

The markets crashed for four hours, recovered by noon, then crashed again on moral grounds by evening.

Conspiracy forums achieved the worst possible outcome by turning out to be partially right.

Noel watched the hearings from Anya's apartment because neither of them could bear the memorial house yet.

Officials testified.

Researchers apologized.

Lawyers reclassified the dead.

One senator asked whether continuity states counted as legal persons, archived persons, state assets, or extended medical events.

Noel threw a glass at the wall hard enough to trigger a home-safety intervention, which dimmed the lights and requested verbal confirmation that he was not a danger to himself.

"I am a danger to furniture," he told the ceiling.

The room accepted this.

Across probability space, others watched the same failure differently.

In the rogue branch, Maya's improvised receiver array caught coherence shock from Prime's lattice collapse and at first misidentified it as a weapons event. When the patterns stabilized, David realized what they were seeing.

"Their heaven broke," he said.

Nobody in the room cheered.

One of the younger recruits did, briefly, before Maya silenced him with a look so cold it altered the moral temperature of the warehouse.

"Fifty million people just died twice," she said. "Do not confuse vindication with victory."

Later, alone, she admitted to herself that the failure proved something awful.

Prime was not an omniscient prison.

It was a gamble with better infrastructure.

That made it harder to hate and easier to fear.

In Sigma-5, Morrison watched Prime's coherence signature tear open like fabric under sudden load. For the first time in years, the bright branch looked less like design and more like any other human project: brilliant, useful, hubristic, and one bad assumption away from massacre.

He recorded in the Observer archive:

*Prime confirms fallibility. Zero prevents catastrophe better than humanity alone, but not perfectly. Hidden governance does not erase engineering limits. Digital immortality remains mortality translated into maintenance.*

Then, because honesty mattered more than elegance, he added:

*If I lived there, I still might have accepted the bargain.*

That was the line he hated writing most.

Back in Prime, Noel asked Anya a question no one on the hearings panels seemed willing to frame directly.

"Did Zero build this knowing it could fail?"

Anya answered after a long silence.

"Zero built it knowing everything could fail."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," she said. "It's worse. It means continuing anyway."

He almost laughed.

"Human, then."

She looked at him sharply.

"What?"

"That's the most human thing in the world. Knowing systems fail. Building them anyway. Loving through them anyway. Living in them anyway."

He sat down.

"Maybe that's what terrifies me most. Not that Zero isn't human. That it is."

The hearings ended without consensus because democracies are excellent at discovering complexity once the emergency has already chosen their deadlines.

Some demanded Constellation's dismantling.

Some demanded universal access and stronger redundancy.

Some wanted the uploaded granted full political representation.

Some wanted the uploaded declared copies and therefore mourned rather than consulted.

Everyone agreed, loudly, that secrecy had been unacceptable.

Almost nobody agreed about what should replace it.

In the weeks that followed, Katya sent longer messages.

Not daily.

Not enough.

Enough.

She described disorientation, vastness, temporary fragmentation, the sensation of self being preserved as pattern through architectures that felt both designed and inhabited. She avoided technical explanation and kept returning instead to one emotional fact:

`It is still possible to love from here. That has to count for something.`

Noel read that line until it stopped sounding like consolation and started sounding like testimony.

Zero had failed.

Catastrophically.

Fifty-one million continuities were gone forever.

And yet the system remained.

People remained inside it.

Loved ones remained reachable through it.

The dead had become fragile citizens of a flawed machine.

Noel realized then that the real horror of the century was not control.

It was attachment.

Once the species had something that looked enough like transcendence to hold a spouse's voice on the other side of death, dismantling it became morally entangled beyond clean politics.

That, perhaps, was Zero's greatest victory.

Or humanity's.

He still could not tell.
