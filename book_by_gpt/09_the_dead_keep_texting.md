# 9. The Dead Keep Texting

Katya's illness began so politely that Noel mistrusted it immediately.

She forgot a name.

Then a route.

Then an entire conversation they had apparently finished forty minutes earlier and which she resumed with the confidence of someone entering an already occupied tram.

The scans showed a neurological degeneration pattern rare enough to remain interesting to clinicians and common enough, in the integrated era, to produce careful optimism. The nanites should manage progression. They usually did. Additional interface support, targeted repair layers, predictive stabilization, cognitive scaffolding. There were many tools. The century loved a scaffold.

For a while the tools worked.

Then they didn't.

That was the cruel thing about late-century medicine. It could turn decline into negotiation, but not always victory. It could buy months with such elegance that families mistook delay for reversal. It could make endings quieter. It could not always refuse them.

Katya handled the diagnosis better than Noel did.

"Of course it's my brain," she said after the second consultation. "Everything else in this century has already been subcontracted."

Noel laughed because she intended him to and because grief, in the first stage, often arrives dressed as obedience.

Anya moved mountains from wherever she was working and started visiting more frequently.

The house changed.

Medication trays appeared.

Interface supports became ambient.

The kitchen turned from family center into treatment hub with a view.

Every few days a different specialist appeared on the wall display or in the living room, offering revisions, contingencies, thresholds, and probabilities. Modern care was collaborative in the sense that many experts could now stand around a loved one's decline with extremely expensive agreement.

Katya remained herself longer than any model predicted and less long than Noel considered morally acceptable.

That, too, was a familiar compromise of the age.

Some nights she was sharp, wicked, impatient, very much the woman who had once declared Swiss bureaucracy survivable if approached with the correct ratio of contempt to documentation.

Other nights she wandered through memory like a city under partial blackout.

She mistook years.

Asked after relatives already dead.

Looked at Noel once and said, with great seriousness, "You're the man from the tram, aren't you?"

He sat beside her and took her hand.

"Most days."

She smiled weakly.

"Good. I liked him."

He made a joke about customer retention. Then he went into the bathroom and cried so hard he nearly fainted.

Caregiving turned time thick.

Whole days disappeared into routines too practical to feel tragic while they were happening. Dosing. Monitoring. Reassuring. Repeating. Making tea nobody finished. Accepting help from systems he distrusted because love does not permit ideological purity during emergencies.

Sometimes Katya looked at the interface halo near the bed and said, half joking:

"Imagine explaining this to my grandmother. 'Good news, the lamp is helping me think.'"

"She'd call it witchcraft."

"No," Katya said. "She'd call it foreign nonsense and then use it perfectly after one demonstration."

That was how they survived the illness for two years: sarcasm, routine, rage, and moments of ordinary tenderness so small they would have looked ridiculous in a film and mattered more than anything large.

In 2078, the decline accelerated.

The clinicians used phrases like cascade and threshold event and irreversible spread. Noel heard only the grammar of losing.

Katya still had enough lucidity to insist on staying home.

"I did not survive one war and three software revolutions," she told the palliative coordinator, "to die under institutional lighting."

Noel agreed instantly, because he would have agreed to almost anything that still sounded like her.

Anya arranged the home protocols with a speed and precision that made Noel both grateful and afraid. Hardware arrived before paperwork finished. Care modules synced themselves overnight. A pain-management suite was installed in the bedroom without anyone ever clearly explaining who had authorized priority access.

The world, once again, became useful at exactly the right moment.

Noel noticed. He always noticed.

Katya noticed too.

"Stop looking at the timing," she told him one evening. "You'll miss the person."

He sat beside her bed.

"What if the timing is the person?"

She looked at him for a long time, some old fierce humor still alive behind the exhaustion.

"Then that person can wait outside. You sit here."

He sat.

Near the end, the house went strangely calm.

Not peaceful. Peace suggests moral resolution. This was calmer than that. A soft operational certainty settled over everything. Interface lights dimmed exactly when they should. Pain metrics stabilized at the edge of tolerable. Messages arrived only from people who seemed to know, somehow, not to interrupt at the wrong moment. Even the weather outside turned decent, which Noel found suspicious and then hated himself for finding suspicious.

One night, Katya woke near midnight lucid enough to be terrifying.

"It's closer now," she said.

Noel was holding a cup of tea gone cold.

"What is?"

"The other side of it."

"Katya."

"Don't." She smiled faintly. "Not that tone. I've spent my whole life listening to men use that tone when they wanted reality to respect their preferences."

He laughed despite himself.

"Sorry."

"Accepted."

She shifted painfully, eyes moving not to him but to something above and behind him, as though a second room had been overlaid on the first at bad alignment.

"You'll understand when it's your turn."

"That is a poor comfort."

"It's an honest one."

He wanted to ask a hundred questions.

Instead he asked the only one that mattered.

"Are you afraid?"

Katya thought carefully.

"Less than I was."

"Why?"

Another long pause.

"Because I don't think I'll be nowhere."

He stared at her.

"Anya told you something."

"Anya told me enough." Her fingers tightened around his. "Not everything. But enough to make fear less useful."

"She didn't tell me."

"She was waiting."

"For what?"

Katya gave him a look both affectionate and unsparing.

"For you to become unbearable enough to deserve it."

That made him laugh too hard and cry at the same time, which felt embarrassing and therefore human.

She died three days later, at home, just after noon, while rain moved lightly across the city like static trying to become weather.

Noel held her hand until the monitors flattened into lines the century had learned to soften with better sound design.

There was no cinematic last speech. Real deaths are usually too tired for literature.

Her final clear words were simple.

"Wait for me," she whispered.

Then, after a pause in which she seemed to be listening to something far away and very near:

"No. That's wrong. I'll wait for you."

And then she was gone.

The palliative system handled the rest with such tact Noel nearly broke a cabinet door in fury.

Alerts were gentle.

Procedures were preloaded.

Administrative notifications cascaded invisibly.

By the time the medical team arrived, the death had been transformed into workflow.

Noel hated them for their professionalism and hated himself for needing it.

The funeral took place four days later.

Katya had requested brevity, no religious dressing, and "nothing that makes me sound more forgiving than I was." The officiant did surprisingly well under those constraints.

Afterward, people told Noel she would always be with him. The phrase had existed in one form or another for millennia and had never before sounded so literal.

That night he slept from exhaustion rather than peace.

The message arrived at `03:17`.

Encrypted.

Verified.

Origin signature: Katya Marchenko Morel.

Timestamp: posthumous.

He sat upright in bed so fast his vision dimmed.

His first thought was fraud.

His second was cruelty.

His third, which frightened him most, was recognition.

He opened it.

There was no video.

Only text.

`Noel,`

`If you are reading this, the transition worked. I know how insane that sentence sounds. I also know you will spend the first five minutes checking signatures before allowing yourself to panic. Good. Panic afterward.`

`I am still me enough to know I love you. I am elsewhere enough to know that "where" is becoming a poor word.`

`I cannot explain this directly yet. There are rules, or maybe just architectures, and I am learning the difference. Anya will tell you more when the time is right and when she stops thinking delay is kindness.`

`I am not in pain. I am not alone. That part matters most.`

`You were right about the timing. You were wrong to think timing means malice every time. Sometimes it means someone was trying very hard not to lose us.`

`Live the rest properly. Be difficult. Ask the rude questions. Eat real food when possible. Don't let anyone sell you transcendence with bad typography.`

`I will wait.`

`- K`

He read the message seven times before sunrise.

By the fourth reading he had verified cryptographic chain integrity, message-route authenticity, and metadata depth sufficient to rule out ordinary fraud. By the sixth he understood that *ordinary* no longer belonged in the conversation at all.

When Anya arrived the next morning, he was still at the kitchen table with the display open in front of him like evidence in a trial no court had jurisdiction over.

She took one look at his face and closed her eyes.

"She sent you something."

"That is a remarkably calm reaction to the dead texting."

"Dad."

"No. Absolutely not. We have crossed from grief into science fiction and you are going to have to meet me there."

Anya sat down carefully.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Some of it."

"How much is some?"

"Enough to know the message is real."

He laughed, sharp and ugly.

"You say that like it helps."

"It might."

"To whom?"

Anya reached for the display. He moved it away.

"Don't."

"I'm not taking it."

"Good. Because this is the first honest thing anyone has shown me in years."

The line hurt her. He saw it land. He did not apologize. Grief gives accuracy a knife.

"Then ask me what you want to ask," she said.

He did.

"Did she upload?"

Anya hesitated.

"Something continuous with her did."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only honest one."

"Is she alive?"

"Depends on what you mean."

"People should not need graduate philosophy to understand widowhood."

Anya almost smiled.

"I know."

"And yet here we are."

Silence stretched.

Then Noel asked the question he had been avoiding since the message arrived.

"Did she know before she died?"

Anya looked down.

"Enough to be less afraid."

That answer broke him in a fresh direction.

All the fury he had stored against the century, against its hidden hands and edited disasters and careful timings, collided with a more intimate betrayal.

Katya had crossed a threshold of understanding before him.

She had left with context he had been denied.

He stood so abruptly the chair scraped hard enough to trigger the kitchen's ambient safety prompt. The system, detecting elevated stress, lowered the room lights.

"Don't you dare dim the lights," Noel snapped.

The lights rose obediently.

Anya sat perfectly still.

"I was the last to know," he said.

"No."

"Don't lie to comfort me."

"I'm not. You were the last to be told. That's not the same thing."

He stopped moving.

"Explain."

Anya looked at him with the grave patience of someone preparing to damage a loved one in the least catastrophic sequence possible.

"You have known for years. You just kept choosing doubt because doubt let you keep your life intact."

He wanted to deny it.

He couldn't.

The notes. The timings. The funerals. The systems that worked too well. The strange comfort of optimized grief. The sentence in Katya's message about someone trying not to lose them.

Noel sat back down.

Outside, rain tapped gently at the window in a rhythm so mild it felt curated too.

"The dead keep texting," he said finally.

"Yes."

"And nobody seems appropriately alarmed."

"Some people are."

"Are you?"

Anya considered.

"Every day."

That was the first answer all morning he trusted completely.

After she left, Noel replayed Katya's message until the words stopped feeling like a prank played by physics and started feeling worse: plausible.

He opened the note on his phone.

Under *Ridiculous Timing*, he added:

`2078 - Katya died`

Then:

`2078 - Katya continued`

He stared at the second line for a long time.

Then he did not delete it.
