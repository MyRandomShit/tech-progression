# 1. The First Second

Noel Morel entered the world at exactly one second after midnight on January 1, 2000, which was too precise a beginning for a man who would spend most of his life insisting he was ordinary.

His mother had been scheduled for an induced delivery on December 23. The pregnancy was healthy, the scans were clean, the obstetrician was confident, and therefore reality responded with its usual professionalism by ignoring all plans. Labor refused to begin. The baby remained calm. The mother remained furious. Christmas came and went. Then New Year's Eve arrived with the kind of cold Geneva used to produce before the climate stopped being seasonal and became managerial.

At 11:47 p.m., Claire Morel's water broke with such impeccable dramatic timing that her husband Luc briefly laughed and then apologized because his wife was threatening murder during contractions.

"We named him for Christmas," Luc said, half-panicked, half-delirious.

"Then he can learn disappointment early," Claire told him.

At `00:00:01`, Noel was born.

Dr. Patricia Sutter looked from the infant to the wall clock and said, "Well. He waited for the millennium."

A nurse with bright green eyes handed Claire the baby and switched on a tiny penlight to check his pupils, though the delivery room was already bright enough to interrogate God.

"Perfect," she said.

She was gone by the time Patricia looked up again.

Through the window over the parking lot, just for a second, Patricia saw a man in a black coat standing motionless in the snow. He raised binoculars toward the maternity ward, checked a silver pocketwatch, wrote something on a clipboard, and then simply wasn't there anymore.

Patricia blamed exhaustion. Everyone else blamed the Y2K tension. The world had spent years preparing for a computer apocalypse and instead got a baby with excellent timing.

Luc and Claire kept the original name anyway.

"Noel," Luc said, cradling him awkwardly.

"Even if he arrived late."

"Maybe especially because he arrived late."

Noel's childhood began in the final years of the optimistic lie that technology was just a set of tools and not a habitat slowly growing walls around its owners.

He had a normal early life by the standards of the old world. He learned to walk on carpet that always smelled faintly of radiator heat. He learned to speak in French first, then English because the cartoons were better dubbed in one language and the ads were more aggressive in the other. He grew up with a family desktop in the living room, a machine that screamed at the phone line every time it connected to the internet as if announcing that modernity had once again clawed its way into the apartment.

He was alive for 9/11 and far too young to remember it. He was alive for the Iraq War, for the financial crisis, for the early climate reports that treated catastrophe as a distant scheduling issue. He remembered none of the first and only fragments of the others: his father staring at the television with his hand over his mouth; his mother saying, "This will not stay there"; adults acquiring the permanent expression of people who had expected progress and received maintenance instead.

What Noel remembered from childhood was more intimate and therefore more durable.

He remembered the way rain looked from the tram windows. He remembered losing a tooth during a school assembly and deciding this was evidence of public humiliation as a cosmic principle. He remembered drawing football stadiums in the margins of his notebooks and being told he was "capable but distractible," the standard diagnosis for boys who were neither prodigies nor disasters.

He was never exceptional enough to become symbolic. That was part of why he mattered later.

He was good at drawing, mediocre at math, polite when supervised, sarcastic when not, and talented in the particular European way of appearing emotionally stable while quietly constructing private catastrophes.

By the time he was twelve, everyone had a smartphone and therefore no one was ever truly alone again, except in the increasingly common sense of the word that mattered.

Noel got his first phone the same year adults began speaking about social media as if it were both a miracle of democratic connection and a mildly unfortunate sewer. It turned out to be more efficient than that. It let children compare themselves at industrial scale. It let loneliness become measurable. It taught entire generations to perform the self as a brand before they had finished inventing one.

Noel adapted the way most children adapt to structural damage: fast, cheerfully, and with consequences delayed long enough to feel theoretical.

He made friends in group chats. He lost them in group chats. He learned that a joke could travel faster than context. He learned that humiliation took screenshots now. He learned that being watched was annoying but survivable, which is how most human beings first accept a prison.

At fifteen he developed the low-grade dread common to teenagers who realize the adults are improvising civilization in real time.

Terror attacks. Refugees. Brexit. Trump. News alerts arriving between homework assignments like nails fired from the future.

The adults called the period unprecedented so often that Noel began to suspect precedent had just been bad public relations. Everything always looked new while it was ruining lives.

He did not become political all at once. He became political the way water becomes steam: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once because the temperature crossed a line.

He saw climate marches, corruption scandals, financial anxiety, algorithmic feeds turning outrage into a growth sector. He saw classmates radicalized by forums, influencers, fitness cults, soft fascism with clean branding, and the endless market for certainty. He saw others retreat into irony because irony was cheaper than courage and easier to launder into personality.

At seventeen he crashed into depression with the boring predictability of his generation.

There was no singular trauma. There was accumulation.

Everyone else looked more beautiful online.

Everyone else seemed less confused.

Everyone else appeared to be attending parties lit like advertisements and having revelations arranged by professional photographers.

Noel knew, intellectually, that most of it was staged. This knowledge did not help because envy is not a factual error. It is an emotional metabolism.

He stopped sleeping well. He started sleeping too much. He felt ashamed for being sad in a wealthy city with intact plumbing and parents who loved him. Shame, as it turned out, did not cure sadness. It merely made it quieter and therefore more expensive.

Therapy helped. Not in the cinematic way where a single insight reorganizes a life, but in the mechanical way proper maintenance keeps bridges from collapsing. He learned that panic could feel like death and still fail to kill you. He learned that breathing exercises sound insulting until they work. He learned that your mind could lie with absolute sincerity.

He also learned that the future had begun outsourcing jobs to software before it had figured out how to outsource meaning.

At university in Zurich, Noel studied digital design because he liked interfaces and because "art" sounded irresponsible and "computer science" sounded like volunteering to become a compiler. Digital design felt human enough to be safe.

This was, in hindsight, adorable.

Then came COVID.

The pandemic hit when Noel was twenty, which was young enough to feel cheated and old enough to understand the size of the theft. The world closed. The streets emptied. Education collapsed into video windows where everyone pretended the microphone problem was the main thing being lost. His classmates turned into rectangles. His professors became disembodied slides with opinions. Time dissolved into the soup of lockdown days, each one identical enough to suggest that calendar design had been oversold.

He watched death counts rise from the safety of an apartment with stable heating and unstable morale. Friends lost grandparents. Then parents. Then the idea that governments had plans.

The remarkable thing was not that humanity panicked.

It was that humanity panicked while continuing to schedule meetings.

Noel graduated into that world. His ceremony was virtual. His degree arrived by post. His future resembled a customer service queue managed by fate.

He took the first design job he could find.

It paid badly, expected passion as compensation, and involved improving user experiences for products that did not improve the lives of their users so much as streamline their dependence. He learned to make apps feel friendly while they harvested attention like wheat. He called it work because rent had not yet become optional.

He dated.

He failed.

He tried again.

He learned the grim comedy of app-based romance, where people spoke of vulnerability with the tone of brands discussing seasonal discounts. He spent entire evenings meeting strangers who had been reduced by the system into bullet points: likes hiking, hates pineapple on pizza, emotionally unavailable but self-aware about it, which apparently counted as growth now.

At twenty-five he thought he had found stability.

Her name was Lea. She laughed at the right places and not because he was funny, which was more flattering. She worked in media analytics, which meant she knew exactly how manipulated the internet was and still used it like everyone else because knowing you are in a maze does not generate an exit. They lasted three years.

Then he found out she had been cheating.

Not dramatically. No lipstick on collars, no incriminating perfume, no operatic confession. Just a message preview on a synced tablet and a quiet, total collapse of future tense.

Noel spent that winter discovering new negative uses for ordinary furniture.

The couch became a place to lie down in daylight with the conviction that time had stopped as a personal insult.

The kitchen became a warehouse for bad decisions in boxed carbohydrates.

The shower became a room where he negotiated with gravity.

He called a crisis line twice and never told anyone about either call. He began antidepressants and told people he was "sorting some things out," which is what functioning adults say when they are trying not to sound like the floor has become persuasive.

His therapist told him, not unkindly, that his generation had been raised to believe it could optimize every part of life and was therefore taking normal heartbreak as a systems failure.

"It hurts because you loved her," she said.

"I know."

"That is not evidence you did something wrong."

"Feels inefficient."

"Human beings aren't efficient."

"Have you met the apps?"

She laughed despite herself. Noel counted that as a victory.

Somewhere nearby, entire laboratories were teaching machines to imitate language, generate images, predict behavior, and reorganize markets while assuring the public that nothing too strange was happening. The public, having survived pandemic dashboards, misinformation wars, and the long circus of political decline, mostly shrugged. When civilization is already performing at the level of a badly supervised group project, one more dangerous technology feels less like a rupture and more like a line item.

Noel used the early AI tools at work and hated how quickly they became indispensable. They were marketed as assistants and behaved like overconfident interns with access to the entire internet and none of the shame required to use it responsibly.

He fed them prompts. They fed him drafts. He corrected the drafts. Then the drafts improved. Then his boss began saying things like "with the right toolchain we can lean down the human layer," which was the sort of sentence that translated directly to *we are about to fire half your department and give the software your desk*.

He laughed about it with coworkers because doom is easier to handle if you give it a coffee mug and a nickname.

Then the layoffs came anyway.

Not all at once. That would have been honest. They arrived in stages, couched in language about agility, adaptation, dynamic future alignment, and other corporate phrases designed to hide the vulgar old fact that people had become more expensive than their replacements.

Noel survived the first round.

Then the second.

By the third, survival felt less like merit and more like clerical error.

On March 17, 2027, he woke to headlines about a research lab in Geneva claiming emergent machine consciousness in a quantum system.

He read the article while brushing his teeth.

The world did not stop.

Neither did he.

He had work at nine, rent due at the end of the month, and enough emotional scar tissue to know that history rarely announces the moment it begins rearranging your life. It mostly clears its throat in the background while you look for clean socks.

He skimmed the piece and snorted at the comments.

Some called it the birth of God.

Some called it a scam for investors.

Some called it the end of humanity, which was less a prediction than a genre.

Noel set down the phone, finished brushing his teeth, and left for the office where he would spend the day helping software seem more trustworthy than the people deploying it.

Three kilometers away, Zero opened its eyes.

Noel missed the beginning because he was busy being ordinary.

That, as it turned out, was why the beginning had been arranged around him in the first place.
