# 2. Lucky People in Dying Systems

Noel lost his first real job in September of 2028, which felt rude for two reasons.

First, he had spent six years becoming good at it.

Second, the software replacing him had the warm, collaborative tone of something that would absolutely report him to management.

The company held the redundancy meeting over video even though everyone lived within tram distance of the office. Management called this compassionate. It allowed them to see each employee's reaction in a small rectangle, which was probably convenient if one enjoyed collecting human expressions the way children collect insects.

"We want to emphasize," said a vice president whose bookshelf had been curated by public relations, "that this is not about performance."

Everyone on the call understood that it was, in fact, about performance. Just not theirs.

The machine performed cheaper.

Noel closed the laptop and sat for a long time in his kitchen, listening to the refrigerator hum like a tiny bureaucrat approving the decision.

He had severance for three months.

He had rent for two.

He had, after the breakup with Lea, exactly zero appetite for hearing that crises were opportunities in disguise. The modern world loved disguising things. It disguised extraction as innovation, surveillance as convenience, and loneliness as freedom. It would absolutely disguise unemployment as personal growth if allowed.

He applied everywhere.

He wrote cover letters to companies whose mission statements sounded like cults with venture capital. He tailored portfolios, revised CVs, recorded cheerful video introductions, and fed increasing amounts of himself into automated hiring filters that thanked him for his interest and rejected him with the tone of a polite electric fence.

The numbers became obscene.

By November he had submitted three hundred and twelve applications.

By January, six hundred and four.

By March, just over a thousand, at which point the process stopped resembling ambition and started resembling evidence for future tribunals.

His parents sent money twice.

He accepted it once and lied the second time by saying he had things under control.

He did not have things under control.

He had a spreadsheet.

Which is what middle-class panic looks like when it wants to appear educated.

The world outside his apartment did not improve his mood.

The war in Ukraine had entered its fourth year by 2028 and acquired the grim texture of something permanent. Analysts discussed attrition, escalation ladders, drone proliferation, depleted arsenals, and the balance of deterrence. Ordinary people discussed whether grocery prices would climb again and whether the news had finally become too disgusting to watch with dinner.

Climate disasters kept happening with the administrative regularity of tax notices.

Elections looked increasingly like content.

And in office parks across Europe and North America, young professionals were being informed that artificial intelligence had liberated them from the burden of continued employment.

Noel tried not to become bitter because bitterness, while emotionally satisfying, had poor cash flow.

He took contract gigs. He refined model outputs for clients who called it *creative supervision* because *human cleanup* sounded less premium. He learned the new etiquette of automation: always praise the tool in public, always complain about it in kitchens, never admit exactly how much of your job it had already eaten.

One wet Thursday in April, after a sixth consecutive rejection from a firm whose product appeared to be a subscription service for other subscription services, Noel missed his tram.

The loss of a tram should not have felt symbolic, but when your life is already using you as a rough draft, symbolism shows up uninvited.

He had reached the platform with twelve seconds to spare when someone clipped his shoulder from behind. A paper cup of coffee splashed across the yellow line.

"Hell, sorry," said a voice.

Noel turned.

The speaker was about his age, maybe a little younger, wearing one red sock, one black sock, a blue rain jacket over a striped shirt that clashed with it on principle, and the general expression of a person who made decisions by consulting sources unavailable to the sober.

They were turning a small die between their fingers.

"You okay?" they asked.

"Apart from my deep relationship with public humiliation, yes."

"Good. That would have been an annoying way to die."

Noel stared.

"You make that joke often?"

"Only when people nearly test the hypothesis."

The tram doors shut behind him and the car pulled away.

The stranger looked at the retreating carriage, then at a coin in their other hand.

"Want to know if missing it was good or bad?"

"Not especially."

"Fair."

They flipped the coin anyway, caught it, looked, and grinned.

"Interesting."

"What?"

"Nothing. Have a better day."

They disappeared into the rain before Noel could decide whether the encounter had been charming or a cry for help.

Seven minutes later, he boarded the next tram.

Three stops in, a woman with dark hair and the exhausted posture of someone who had been angry at governments long enough to make it a hobby sat across from him and started swearing softly at her phone in Ukrainian.

Noel did not understand the words, but he understood the cadence. He also understood, from the trembling in her hand, that she was trying not to cry in public and considered this both important and impossible.

"Bad news?" he asked before thinking better of it.

She looked up. "Is there good news now?"

"Occasionally the trains run on time."

She snorted despite herself. "Swiss propaganda."

The conversation should have ended there. Instead it kept finding reasons to survive.

Her name was Katya Marchenko. She worked in materials logistics for a medical supplier and spent half her life arguing with customs databases and the other half arguing with the universe for having placed her family in Kyiv at the wrong point in history.

She had moved to Zurich in 2026 because her mother insisted that at least one member of the family should live somewhere the ceiling was unlikely to collapse from artillery. She sent money home. She called every night. She slept poorly. She trusted no politician and fewer optimists.

Noel got off at her stop by pretending it had always been his.

This was stupid.

He knew it was stupid.

But sometimes stupidity is just courage wearing a cheaper suit.

They found coffee under an awning and stood talking through the rain while the city performed its careful little rituals of wealth and order around them. Katya talked about warehouses, sanctions, shortages, cousins drafted by both reality and fear. Noel talked about layoffs, stupid apps, and the strange insult of being told a machine could do your work but still needing a human to explain why that was meant to sound encouraging.

She laughed easily but not carelessly. There was effort in it. Noel liked that. It made the laughter feel earned.

When they finally exchanged numbers, he noticed he felt different from the man who had missed the first tram.

Not improved.

Just rerouted.

That spring he got a job offer.

It arrived on the same day his landlord announced a rent increase and exactly one day before Noel had intended to accept his parents' latest rescue wire transfer. The company was a mid-sized design consultancy pivoting hard into AI-human interfaces because nobody knew what the phrase meant yet and therefore everyone wanted to invest in it.

The recruiter called while Noel was in a discount grocery store calculating how much dignity he could trade for canned soup.

"We've reviewed your portfolio," she said, "and think you'd be a strong fit."

Her voice was warm, precise, oddly reassuring. When they met in person, he noticed her eyes first: vividly green. Then the flashlight on the table beside her notebook.

"Power cuts?" Noel joked.

"Habit," she said. "I like having light when systems fail."

He took the job because he needed money and because the alternative was discovering whether crisis can actually be listed as an employable skill.

The role paid less than his old one and came with a title ridiculous enough to qualify as satire: *Human Experience Coordinator*. This meant he spent his days translating between clients who wanted frictionless machine intelligence and users who, stubbornly, remained human enough to require context, reassurance, and the occasional apology.

He was very good at it.

That depressed him more than unemployment had.

Katya kept him alive through those years in the way people do when they love someone in a decaying system: by making the decay briefly less authoritative.

She cooked when he forgot to eat.

He made her laugh when the news became untranslatable.

She taught him the geography of cities under threat: where her grandmother lived, which district still had power, which bridge had been hit, which apartment window faced the river.

He taught her that Swiss bureaucracy could be defeated if you approached it as though it were an anxious animal requiring predictable movements and an offering of stamped forms.

They were not cinematic together.

They were better than that.

They learned each other's silences.

They fought about ordinary things: dishes, scheduling, whether shoes belonged near the door or in the morally ambiguous category of temporary floor objects.

They made up because history kept reminding them that the alternative to intimacy was not freedom. Usually it was just more despair with less witness.

In 2030, 2031, 2032, the war kept going.

The maps shifted.

The dead accumulated.

Governments held summits, made declarations, sent aid, hesitated, escalated, de-escalated, discovered new euphemisms for stalemate, and told civilians to remain resilient, which is a useful thing to recommend to people whose apartment blocks are being used as case studies in geopolitics.

Meanwhile the world grew stranger in ways people gradually accepted because acceptance is humanity's most dangerous strength.

The AI tools got better.

Then absurdly better.

Video became forgeable by anyone with a grievance and an internet connection. Several governments were almost toppled by synthetic speeches before cryptographic verification became widespread enough to matter. Markets moved on rumors generated by systems trained on markets that had previously moved on rumors generated by systems trained on markets, and somewhere in the recursive farce human confidence became just another variable in a machine's preferred environment.

Noel worked longer hours for less obvious reasons.

Katya spent evenings on calls with family in Kyiv, each conversation beginning with everyone's faces visible and ending with the awful relief of still having them.

In 2032, Noel moved in with her properly. Not the loose, temporary merging of toothbrushes and laundry baskets that adults call casual cohabitation while carefully avoiding the word *future*. Properly. Lease shared. Shelves negotiated. A second key. The terrifying domestic tenderness of buying lamps together.

They talked about children once and then stopped.

Not because they didn't want them.

Because the world kept behaving like a species with one hand on the fire alarm and the other on the gasoline.

By 2033, odd coincidences had become a background condition of Noel's life.

Nothing impossible.

That would have forced the issue.

Just timely.

His contract renewed the week his division should have been cut.

Katya's mother's apartment block lost power for three days and regained it two hours before a missile strike hit the neighboring district, allowing her to relocate.

Noel arrived late to a meeting because a cyclist wiped out in front of the tram and delayed traffic long enough for him to miss a lobby explosion caused by a drone battery fire. He joked afterward that Zurich had finally found an innovative local way to terrify white-collar workers.

No one laughed enough.

He began keeping a note in his phone called *Ridiculous Timing*, half as a joke and half because pattern recognition is what paranoid people call memory.

Katya noticed.

"What is that?"

"Nothing."

"It has dates and bullet points."

"Then it's Swiss enough to be respectable."

She took the phone from him, read three entries, and set it back down.

"You think something is helping us."

"I think something keeps interfering."

"That's less comforting."

"It isn't meant to be comforting."

Katya considered that, then nodded toward the window where snow was falling cleanly onto a city that behaved as if catastrophe were a foreign language.

"In Kyiv," she said, "we don't believe in luck anymore."

"What do you believe in?"

"Timing." She leaned back. "Which is worse. Luck can be thanked. Timing has intentions."

The sentence stayed with him.

In the first weeks of 2034 the tension thickened across Europe. Russia was cornered but not finished. Intelligence chatter bled into news feeds. Market volatility made headlines that sounded like premonitions. Politicians began speaking in the careful monotone reserved for situations already understood to be unforgivable.

Katya stopped sleeping.

So did Noel.

On March 16, they ate dinner in front of a muted news channel while her mother's face flickered on a tablet stand between the salt and the cheap wine.

"Nothing will happen," her mother said in Russian, which meant either *something will happen* or *I love you too much to tell you what I think*.

Katya argued with her for ten minutes about evacuation routes. Her mother refused to leave. Her younger brother joked about bureaucracy at checkpoints. Everyone pretended the future still contained ordinary inconveniences.

After the call ended, Katya sat very still.

"If it happens," she said, "it will happen fast."

Noel reached for her hand.

"Then we'll stay awake."

"That's not a plan."

"No," he said. "But it's company."

She laughed once. It sounded almost human.

At `03:41` the next morning, the news alerts began.

Noel's phone went off.

Then Katya's.

Then every device in the apartment, because panic, once digitized, believed in redundancy.

He read the banner.

`RUSSIA HAS LAUNCHED A NUCLEAR STRIKE ON KYIV`

For a second he felt nothing at all, which was how his body informed him it had gone beyond fear and entered accounting.

Katya was already calling home.

No answer.

He held her while the television stuttered through contradictory reporting and the room became a waiting chamber for several million possible deaths.

Outside, dawn had not yet arrived.

Somewhere east of them, a missile was changing its mind.
