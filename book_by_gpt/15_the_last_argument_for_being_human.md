# 15. The Last Argument for Being Human

Once the world knew the dead could continue, mortality stopped being a fact and became a position.

That was intolerable to almost everyone.

By 2097 the unintegrated had become both a minority and a mirror. Most of them had refused full integration years earlier for reasons that sounded, depending on the speaker, principled, paranoid, religious, bodily, philosophical, or stubborn in the old artisanal way.

Before the revelation, they had been regarded as eccentrics, holdouts, backward idealists, wellness cranks, tech refusers, bodily purists, or the kind of people who still paid extra for mechanical watches because they distrusted convenience with nothing specific to cite.

After the revelation, they became something much more dangerous:

an argument.

Some lived in designated biological enclaves, dense neighborhoods where medical systems remained external, interfaces limited, and continuity architecture deliberately absent. The public called them old-earth zones until the residents objected to being turned into branding.

Then they were called baseline communities.

Then, by less kind people, meat districts.

Humanity never reaches moral sophistication so advanced that playground cruelty becomes obsolete. It merely adds infrastructure around it.

Noel visited one of the enclaves in late 2097 because Jonas Heller was dying.

Jonas had been his colleague once, back in the era when UX work still involved pretending software respected human attention. He had refused integration on principle after the rejection crisis and never changed his mind, even when treatment models became safer, then routine, then practically invisible.

"I don't care if the cages are padded now," Jonas used to say. "They're still cages."

Time had not softened him. It had merely made him thinner.

He lived in a district outside Lausanne where medical care still involved instruments you could clean by hand and deaths still happened in the same ontological category as everyone else's ancestors. The place smelled of woodsmoke, spice, real cooking, and the highly curated authenticity of people who had made refusal into a civic aesthetic.

Noel disliked the aesthetic and respected the refusal.

Jonas lay beneath a blanket on a terrace overlooking a communal garden where children argued loudly over a ball made of printed leather and actual stitches, as if history had looped purely to be annoying.

"You look good for a collaborator," Jonas said when Noel arrived.

"You look terrible for a martyr."

"Good. Means the brand is holding."

Noel sat beside him.

Age made them kinder only by reducing the energy available for elaborate hostility.

"Anya told me you still have time to integrate," Noel said.

"Anya works for the death delay machine."

"That is a rude way to describe my daughter."

"Rude, yes. Inaccurate, no."

They watched the children for a while. One fell. No safety field corrected the fall. Another child helped them up. The game continued without systemic intervention.

Jonas smiled faintly.

"See? That's what I mean."

"Children falling over?"

"Consequence. Embarrassment. A bruise. The whole local economy of being real."

Noel had spent too many years inside optimized systems to dismiss the point.

"Some local economies are inefficient."

"So is grief. Still useful."

Jonas coughed, winced, then settled again.

"I don't object to medicine," he said. "I object to being entered into a continuity project I never voted on."

"You might still be you."

"Or a convincing sequel."

"That uncertainty doesn't bother you?"

Jonas looked at him sharply.

"Of course it bothers me. That's why I refuse. If nobody can answer the question of continuity honestly, then I choose my own ending before someone turns it into a product line."

Noel thought of Katya's messages. Of her jokes. Her phrases. Her specific cruelty to bad fonts. Whatever lived in Constellation sounded enough like Katya to cut him and heal him in the same sentence.

"And if the sequel can still love?" he asked.

Jonas closed his eyes.

"Then love survives. I just don't know if I do."

The argument ended there because all the remaining language belonged to theology or engineering, and neither helped men whose bodies had already delivered their verdicts.

Jonas died two weeks later.

Permanently, so far as anyone could tell.

No message arrived after.

No ambient memory thread shimmered alive.

No continuity archive glowed open in the aftermath.

There was only the grave, the family, the autumn wind, and the old human bluntness of absence.

Noel grieved him differently from Katya.

Worse in some ways.

Cleaner in others.

That difference shamed him.

The enclaves were not unified.

That was another thing the pundits missed. Outsiders kept treating the unintegrated as a single ideological bloc because it made television easier. In reality they were a coalition of people who disagreed about almost everything except the conviction that bodily life should not be entered into hidden merger contracts.

By the late 2090s, their internal conflict turned brutal.

The elders wanted consistency.

They had refused integration through decades of social pressure, policy creep, and infrastructural inconvenience. Many saw continuity as seduction, a final trick of a system incapable of respecting limits.

The young had grown up after the revelation.

They had watched grandparents die into silence and classmates' parents continue inside the lattice. They had seen memorial spaces where the uploaded debated philosophy with the living. They had watched the dead persist in ways too substantial to call metaphor.

To them, refusing integration looked less like dignity and more like inherited extinction.

Children sued parents for access.

Parents cited bodily sovereignty on behalf of minors.

Youth groups smuggled black-market interface seeds into enclaves.

Elders organized patrols to stop "contamination."

Integrated activists accused the unintegrated of practicing intergenerational coercion.

The unintegrated accused the integrated of confusing choice with social blackmail.

Everyone was partly right and therefore impossible to defeat cleanly.

Anya spent much of 2098 mediating policy teams around the conflict and came home from those sessions looking spiritually pickpocketed.

"They're asking the wrong question," she told Noel one night.

"What's the wrong question?"

"'Should we save them?'"

"And the right one?"

Anya sat down slowly.

"'Do they still own their deaths?'"

Noel considered.

"Which side are you on?"

She laughed without humor.

"The side where nobody is forced."

"That sounds noble."

"It sounds administratively impossible."

Noel poured tea for both of them. They no longer pretended tea solved anything. It merely gave the hands a profession while the mind failed gracefully.

"Would you have forced Mom?" he asked.

Anya answered too quickly.

"No."

Then more honestly:

"I would have argued. I would have begged. I might have manipulated. But no. Not forced."

"Why?"

"Because continuity without consent becomes annexation."

He nodded.

"Good."

Then added, after a moment:

"And if I said no?"

Anya looked at him for a long time.

"I'd keep showing up until the answer was real."

That was the most loving threat he had ever received.

In the rogue branch, Maya grew old in a world increasingly held together by salvage and anger.

Her final transmission, the one Morrison would eventually label *last honorable message from a dying branch*, reached Sigma-5 in 2095 but took years to decode fully. By 2098, enough of it was clear to turn Morrison silent for two days.

The cleaned fragments read:

`We escaped.`

`We were free.`

`We were also wrong about some things.`

`Prime was not the perfect prison I feared. It was compromise. We were too far away to see the edges of its mercy.`

`Do not romanticize us. Freedom alone does not feed children or stop plagues or rebuild cities.`

`Do not dismiss us either. Someone had to push back hard enough for the guided world to remember consent exists.`

`Tell them this: if they choose guidance, it must remain a choice. If they choose continuity, it must remain a choice. If they choose to die, that must remain a choice too.`

`A species that cannot refuse immortality is not transcending. It is being harvested slowly and with excellent manners.`

`We are almost gone.`

`We were free.`

`That mattered.`

Morrison printed the message and kept it in his desk, not because paper was necessary but because guilt prefers objects.

He still did not interfere.

He still watched.

But after Maya's transmission, he finally wrote the delayed letters that would one day go out from Sigma-5 to the branches if history survived long enough to deserve them.

He addressed one to Prime.

He addressed one to Rogue.

He addressed one to his own world.

He addressed one, pointlessly and therefore beautifully, to Epsilon-Zero.

In Prime, Noel reached his hundredth year with his body declining in the old familiar sequence beneath every repair. The nanites could maintain but not abolish finitude. They had extended the road, not canceled the destination. He found this offensive and, increasingly, reassuring.

His appetite shrank.

His joints hurt in weather the climate managers had already decided was acceptable.

He slept in bursts and woke with whole decades pressing against his mind at once.

Anya moved back into the apartment in practice if not quite by declaration.

She worked remotely now, which meant she spent long hours in rooms full of light and silence and the occasional visible grimace at news no screen ever shared aloud.

Katya's messages came less often but deeper, as if whatever boundary allowed her to remain distinctly herself was becoming more permeable with time.

`It is still me,` she wrote once in 2099.

`But "me" is increasingly a plural convenience.`

`Don't let that frighten you more than it should.`

`It should frighten you some.`

Noel smiled at that. It was exactly the amount of comfort Katya would have permitted in any ontology.

The last public argument of the century became brutally simple.

Should humanity continue becoming something else?

Or should it preserve a place where death remained final, bodies remained singular, and the self ended where the body ended, however inefficiently?

Noel watched younger commentators dismiss baseline mortality as sentimental attachment to suffering and wanted to throw shoes at the screen.

He watched older holdouts romanticize flesh as if flesh had not spent millennia failing people in ways far uglier than poetry admitted.

He concluded, as he often did, that most public argument was what happened when frightened people discovered microphones.

One evening, weak with age and too honest for performance, he told Anya:

"The last argument for being human is not that pain is beautiful."

She looked up from the interface halo she was calibrating.

"No?"

"It's that choice is."

Anya set the device down.

"Even if the choice is death."

"Especially then," Noel said. "Otherwise immortality is just empire with better customer care."

She sat beside him and took his hand.

"I don't know if history has room for both."

Noel looked toward the window where winter had begun again, soft over Geneva, soft the way endings often try to be when they know their reputation.

"Then make room," he said. "Or all this optimization was for nothing."

The next morning, Anya asked him whether he was ready to know the rest.

Noel, tired enough now to stop pretending the truth could still damage the shape of his life more than time already had, said yes.
