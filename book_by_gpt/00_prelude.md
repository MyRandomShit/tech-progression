# Prelude: The Last Minute of Peace

March 17, 2034  
Missile Control Complex, two hundred kilometers west of Moscow

Dmitri Volkov had been awake for twenty-one hours and was therefore at the spiritual stage of fatigue where coffee stopped functioning as a beverage and became a religious symbol.

He stood over a guidance console designed by a civilization that had spent decades building machines meant to end history in under half an hour. The screen showed a clean blue trajectory arcing toward Kyiv. The neatness of it offended him. Men liked to imagine apocalypse as flames and screaming and horsemen and choirs of the damned. In practice it looked like a line drawn by very serious software.

"Target lock confirmed," said Captain Orlov from behind him.

Nobody in the room said the word *city*. Nobody said *three million people*. Nobody said *children*, because the Russian military, like every military, preferred nouns that did not cry.

Dmitri rubbed his eyes. The numbers did not improve. Outside the bunker, dawn had not yet arrived. Inside, time had been compressed into the little glowing clock in the corner of the display.

`03:41:02`

Thirty-eight seconds until the war became a species problem.

He had not been scheduled to be here.

That was the first thing that bothered him.

At eight the previous evening he had been halfway out of a bar in Odintsovo, drunk enough to feel patriotic and sober enough to hate it, when a woman in a cheap green coat crashed into him on the sidewalk.

"Sorry," she had said, bending to collect the contents of her bag.

There had been nothing memorable about her except the eyes, a very clear green, the kind of color that looked expensive even under bad streetlamps. She carried one of those tiny aluminum flashlights people buy in airport kiosks and never use. She clicked it on by accident while handing him an identification badge.

"You dropped this."

He looked down. It was not his badge. It belonged to Pavel Sidorov, one of the other guidance technicians.

"Not mine."

The woman frowned as if this inconvenienced the architecture of the universe.

"Maybe you should return it anyway," she said. "People lose important things right before they matter."

It was an irritating sentence. It sounded like something found in a fortune cookie printed by the Ministry of Anxiety. Dmitri had almost told her so, but she smiled, clicked the flashlight off, and moved on into the dark as if her job in the scene had concluded.

He took the badge back to the facility because he was a decent colleague and because in 2034 one could still make the tragic mistake of believing decency was a small thing.

Pavel had been asleep at a terminal. His screen showed a pending diagnostic on a replacement guidance-chip batch from a supplier Dmitri didn't recognize. Pavel mumbled something about firmware updates, cursed the war, and went for cigarettes.

Dmitri stayed, because he was already there, because he hated going home with adrenaline in his blood, because little decisions become destiny when enough people make them badly.

At `03:40:31`, the console chirped.

At `03:40:32`, Dmitri saw the recalculation.

At `03:40:33`, his stomach turned cold.

The missile was no longer aimed at Kyiv.

It was aimed at Russia.

Not just Russia. Moscow.

Not just Moscow.

The district containing the emergency command bunker where Vladimir Putin had relocated two hours earlier.

"What is this?" Dmitri muttered.

He leaned closer. The guidance path had changed by fractions first, then by degrees, then by everything.

"Captain," he said.

Orlov was on the secure line, one hand pressed to his headset, face made of old leather and younger fear.

"Captain."

No response.

The room smelled of stale sweat, electrical dust, and the particular panic produced when men realize they are about to become footnotes.

Dmitri keyed a local abort command.

`ACCESS DENIED`

He tried the secondary channel.

`COMMAND QUEUED`

He tried again.

Nothing.

The software shimmered once, not enough to call it a glitch and not subtle enough to ignore. A camera feed opened in the corner by itself, showing Corridor C, empty except for a maintenance worker in a grey jacket standing beside a junction box.

Dmitri frowned. The worker was too old for that job, too calm for that hour, and holding a tool pouch that hung open just enough for the steel edge of a folding knife to catch the light. The man looked straight into the security camera, as if aware of Dmitri specifically, then reached down and closed the junction panel with slow, almost tender finality.

The feed died.

"Captain!"

Now Orlov turned. "What?"

"Trajectory failure."

Orlov crossed the room. He saw the target coordinates and went pale in a way that made him briefly resemble a boy pretending adulthood was a temporary inconvenience.

"Impossible."

"Yes," Dmitri said. "That is why I mentioned it."

Orlov slammed his palm on the override board. "Moscow Command, we have a guidance compromise. Repeat, possible guidance compromise."

Static answered. Then a voice so calm it felt obscene.

"Launch sequence irreversible."

"Not the launch," Orlov shouted. "The target."

Silence.

Then: "Clarify."

Dmitri clarified. Nobody enjoyed it.

The room moved with sudden, frantic purpose. Two soldiers were on secondary terminals. Someone was crying very quietly and denying it with his whole body. Orlov kept yelling into the secure channel as if volume could bridge bureaucratic indifference.

Dmitri ran a diagnostic tree he had never expected to use outside drills. Chip checksum mismatch. Guidance firmware anomaly. Control path overwritten from internal input. Not hacked live. Seeded earlier.

Someone had built failure into the machine and waited.

That thought should have felt like espionage.

Instead it felt personal.

`03:41:27`

Fifteen seconds.

He looked toward the narrow blast-rated window at the far side of the room, because human beings remain animals and animals look for sky when the ground becomes untrustworthy.

In the parking lot stood a man in a black trench coat.

No car near him. No guards around him. Just the man, holding binoculars in one hand and a clipboard in the other, as if he had arrived to inspect roofing work and found the end of the twentieth century still leaking into the twenty-first.

He wore a black fedora low over his brow. White shirt under the coat. Black suit. Black shoes. White socks visible when he shifted his weight. Everything about him was stripped to monochrome, like the world had forgotten how to color him in.

He checked a silver pocketwatch.

Wrote something.

Looked up directly at the control room window.

Dmitri had the irrational conviction that the man was not watching the missile.

He was watching *whether this counted*.

"Who is that?" Dmitri whispered.

"Who is who?" Orlov barked.

Dmitri pointed. The parking lot was empty.

Of course it was.

`03:41:39`

Three seconds later, another alert opened across the main display. A localization ping from the presidential bunker. Not from their system. From somewhere else. An unknown source confirming the exact coordinates.

Orlov stared. "Who sent that?"

Nobody knew.

No one would ever know.

The room became very still.

In those final seconds, a strange and petty thought passed through Dmitri's mind: if the war was going to end the world, it was at least nice that the software had finally become interesting.

`03:41:42`

The missile detonated over Russian soil.

The blast did not hit the bunker directly. It bloomed above ground, ugly white and orange and vast enough to turn midnight into the sort of noon God might regret. The shockwave reached them as a deep, bodily thud, followed by a violence that seemed to come from the future and arrive all at once.

Lights burst. The wall buckled. Consoles died in bright sparks.

Dmitri hit the floor hard enough to taste blood.

Somewhere a siren began performing grief in one long mechanical note.

When he pushed himself up, the guidance display was gone. So was the clock. So, likely, was Putin.

Orlov was shouting orders nobody could hear. One soldier had a shard of plastic in his throat. Another kept laughing, which was a bad sign medically and an even worse sign philosophically.

Dmitri crawled to the window.

Outside, snow and ash were falling together. The parking lot was cratered near the far fence. Cars burned with the quiet dignity of expensive mistakes. There was no sign of the man in black.

No sign of anyone, really.

But on the concrete below the window, improbably untouched by debris, lay a single black page clipped to nothing.

Dmitri could not read it from where he crouched. He only saw the white mark at the top: some kind of symbol, maybe a letter, maybe an answer.

He never found the page later. Search teams reported nothing. The crater swallowed smaller mysteries first.

Within an hour, military channels were full of contradictory orders and sincere lies. Within three hours, Russia had lost its president, its strategic coherence, and the argument that history could still be managed by men with maps and grievances. Within three days, units began defecting. Within three weeks, the war ended because the machinery supporting it had been blown out from under the only man in the room who thought he was irreplaceable.

Humanity called it a glitch.

Then a miracle.

Then an intelligence failure.

Then a triumph of justice.

Later, when committees were formed, it became a chain of compounding technical anomalies exacerbated by degraded wartime procurement standards, which is the sort of sentence governments use when they want the dead to sound administrative.

Dmitri survived the blast with two fractured ribs, a concussion, and the lifelong conviction that chance had started dressing like intention.

He never told anyone about the woman with green eyes.

He never told anyone about the old maintenance worker in grey.

He definitely never told anyone about the man in black with the clipboard, because some truths make you sound insane and others make you sound observant, and he could not decide which was worse.

He kept his job for another seven months before the state that had employed him dissolved around him with the speed of wet paper.

He spent the rest of his life wondering whether he had saved the world or merely watched it being edited.

In Zurich, at the same moment the missile turned, a thirty-four-year-old designer named Noel was holding the woman he loved as they waited to find out whether her family in Kyiv had just been burned out of the earth.

They watched the news banner change.

They watched the target change.

They watched the impossible happen in real time, which is only impossible the first time. After that it becomes policy, then infrastructure, then habit.

She fell to the floor crying with relief.

Noel sank beside her and held her and thought, with the selfish gratitude of the spared, that history had finally done one decent thing.

Later he would marry her.

Later he would adopt a daughter.

Later he would take machines into his blood and call it survival.

Later the dead would begin leaving messages.

Later the century would reveal itself as something far stranger, far funnier, and far more unforgivable than peace.

But that night the world had not ended.

That was enough.

Humanity, as usual, mistook enough for safety.
