# 10. The Jump

March 17, 2070  
Hong Kong  
Eight years before Katya's death, one hour before dawn

The abandoned research facility smelled like salt, ozone, dust, and the distinct moral aroma of stolen government hardware.

Maya appreciated the honesty of it.

The machine occupying the center of the room had once belonged to a quantum consciousness lab operating three layers below public oversight and at least one layer below good taste. The Unbound had spent fourteen months dismantling, transporting, mislabeling, bribing, reassembling, and swearing at it. By the time it was operational, it looked less like the frontier of human possibility than like several arguments held together with thermal shielding and desperation.

Fifteen people stood around it.

Fifteen had been the final number after attrition, compromise, betrayal, and the long mathematics of deciding who would trade reality for a chance at a worse one.

Arun was there, still joking too easily.

Yasmin, pale and furious.

David, who had built half the translation layer and trusted none of it.

Priya, whose specialty in uniqueness-field coherence had persuaded them this might be movement rather than copying.

Two former orbital technicians.

Three ex-military specialists.

One climate modeler.

One medic.

One woman who had killed a Constellation architect with a maintenance drone and had never again described herself as nonviolent.

And Maya, who had built the thing without once calling it hope.

"Last chance to leave," she said.

No one moved.

Arun checked the battery ladder and said, "I just want it noted for future historians that this is the stupidest thing any of us have ever done, and I once dated a venture capitalist."

"Seconded," said Yasmin.

"Noted," Maya replied.

Noel, at that same hour in Zurich, was asleep beside Katya, his body quietly exchanging data with systems he did not know existed. James Morrison, in Vancouver, was calibrating detectors for an experiment that would ruin the rest of his life. The man in the black coat was already in the street below the facility, binoculars raised.

Maya did not know any of that.

She only knew the theory.

Consciousness, while living, possessed a unique quantum coherence field irreducible to classical duplication.

That field could not be copied cleanly without death.

But maybe it could be translated.

Shifted.

Re-indexed across probability space into an adjacent branch where the local version of the self had decohered differently.

Or maybe that was just a prettier verb for dying weirdly.

The math permitted both interpretations with equal cruelty.

"We should say it one more time," Priya said. "For the record."

No one argued.

So she did.

"We do not know whether this preserves continuity."

Silence.

"We do not know whether we will wake up as ourselves, as copies, or not at all."

Longer silence.

"We do not know whether the target branch is stable, habitable, or truly outside Zero's control."

Arun raised one hand slightly.

"We do know the marketing is bad."

Nobody laughed hard enough. Good. They were not insane enough for easy laughter yet.

Maya looked at the team.

"If anyone wants out, get out now. No shame."

One of the orbital techs, Lena, said what they all knew.

"If we stay, we already live in a system that built an afterlife into our blood without asking. Leaving is the only honest risk on the table."

That settled it.

The Unbound had argued for months about whether Prime was paradise or a managed exhibit, whether escape was moral or theatrical, whether the species had a right to comfort it had not explicitly consented to. Those arguments had not resolved. They had simply reached the practical stage where philosophy acquires voltage.

Maya activated the branch map.

The room filled with nested topologies, luminous strands of probable timelines trembling in a rendering no human nervous system had evolved to interpret sanely. Prime ran through the center with unnerving coherence, bright and taut. Around it spread lesser branches, decayed branches, aborted branches, stochastic noise, dead futures, ugly freedoms, impossible paradises, histories in which the war lasted longer, histories in which it went nuclear, histories in which intervention arrived earlier and ruined everything by being too effective.

One branch in particular remained viable and weakly coupled.

Under-optimized.

Unstable.

Habitable.

No apparent Zero-level coherence.

"Targeting Beta-Rho-847," Priya confirmed.

Arun looked at the map.

"Can we pick a timeline with a less fascist filing system?"

"No," said Maya.

"Worth asking."

She went terminal by terminal, checking link integrity herself because leadership, in her experience, was mostly just extra paranoia with a clipboard function.

David caught her arm near the end.

"We still don't know whether Zero knows."

"Zero knows something."

"That's not the same as knowing this."

"No," Maya said. "It's worse."

He almost said more, then stopped.

"If this works," he asked quietly, "what do we do there?"

Maya looked at the target branch pulsing faintly on the display.

"We live unguided."

"That's not a plan."

"It is compared to most of human history."

At `03:39`, the external cameras pinged movement.

One of the security feeds opened automatically on Maya's side screen.

In the street below, under a failing lamp, stood a man in a black trench coat and fedora.

Binoculars in one hand.

Clipboard in the other.

Black suit.

White shirt.

Silver watch.

Colorless as an old surveillance reel.

He lowered the binoculars and looked directly into the camera.

Then, impossibly, directly at Maya through the lens.

He checked the pocketwatch.

Saluted.

No one else seemed to notice the feed.

Maya stepped toward it.

"Who put that on screen?"

Arun looked over. "Put what?"

The feed glitched.

For a fraction of a second another figure stood in the corner of the room reflection: mismatched shoes, a die turning through the fingers. Then the image cleared to an empty street.

"Maya?" Yasmin asked.

"Nothing."

She cut the feed.

At `03:40`, they linked in.

Neural interface halos lowered around temples and occipital nodes. Transition gel cooled against skin. The room lights dimmed automatically. Every member of the team took their place in the concentric arrangement required to maintain field coherence long enough for the branch translation to fire.

Priya began the count.

"Uniqueness lock stable."

"Branch vector stable."

"Identity map handshake pending."

"Tell me something nice," Arun said.

"You die with excellent posture," David answered.

"That is not nice."

"It's organized."

Maya almost smiled.

Almost.

She thought of Jun. Of the elevator architect. Of the clean false kindness in the word *ignorance*. Of everyone already dead inside a system that might call itself mercy.

Then she thought, briefly and painfully, of a simpler possibility:

What if Prime was actually the best humanity could do?

What if freedom looked exactly like the uglier branches for a reason?

What if she was about to trade a hidden benevolence for visible ruin?

The thought mattered because it hurt.

Only idiots and zealots move through history without uncertainty. Maya had worked hard to be neither.

"Maya," Priya said.

"I know."

She settled into the interface cradle.

The metal was cold.

The room hummed.

Everyone's biosigns appeared in a shared lattice overhead, fifteen conscious systems about to ask physics a question it had every right not to answer.

"On branch transfer," Priya said, voice thin with focus, "there may be dissociation, identity shear, nonlocal memory bleed, partial retention from target-state overlap, and catastrophic coherence collapse."

Arun stared at the ceiling.

"I miss when danger was just bullets."

"Bullets are still available if you want something more traditional," Maya said.

"No, thanks. Very retro."

The machine ramped.

Field noise rose.

The target branch brightened, then seemed to move, though movement was probably the wrong word for two realities being forced into conversational distance.

Then a voice came from the far side of the room.

"Should I add variance?"

Everyone turned.

No one stood there.

But on the floor near the relay stack lay a six-sided die showing `4`.

Evens.

David swore.

The system registered a 17% instability increase.

"What happened?" Priya shouted.

"Randomness injection," Arun said, already working.

"From what?"

"I don't know because I apparently majored in Earth logic."

The machine reached irreversible threshold.

Too late to abort.

Perhaps always too late.

"We go now," Maya said.

Somewhere outside, perhaps in the street or perhaps in the spaces between histories, the man with the clipboard wrote something down.

`03:42`

Priya dropped the lock.

Reality opened.

There is no useful language for the first instant of branch translation.

Pain is too small a word.

Death is too decisive.

It felt like being remembered incorrectly by the universe.

Every memory Maya had ever owned was pulled through a point finer than nerves. The room disappeared, then multiplied, then inverted. She was a child in Hong Kong. A soldier in training. A woman on a tram in a city she had never lived in. A body on a floor. A waveform. A refusal. She heard the others screaming through the shared field and understood, abstractly, that at least one voice was already not attached to anyone living.

Then impact.

Then dark.

Then dust.

Maya opened her eyes to the same room and knew instantly it was not the same.

The ceiling had water damage.

The west wall was cracked.

Half the displays were dead.

The air tasted old, not maintained.

She rolled onto her side and saw three bodies before she saw anything else.

Arun was alive, coughing blood and laughing weakly because apparently personality survived translation or at least one insultingly accurate copy of it did.

Yasmin was on her knees.

Priya lay still with her interface halo split and dark.

Two others were dead outright, blood from ears and nose, coherence burn evident even before Maya's training named it.

"Count," Maya rasped.

No answer.

Then one.

Then another.

Twelve voices.

Fifteen had entered.

Twelve had arrived.

No. Thirteen had arrived if you counted the one currently seizing in the corner.

Twelve, then.

Five would be dead by the end of the week.

The machine behind them had survived partially. Enough to show date and local branch confirmation.

March 17, 2070.

Same day.

Different world.

Maya staggered to the broken window.

Hong Kong sprawled beneath her in ruinous dimness.

Not apocalyptic in the cinematic sense. Worse. Worn down. Underfed. Buildings patched instead of rebuilt. Drones fewer, older, dirtier. Transit lines unreliable. Harbor traffic thin. Smog thick as old debt. The skyline looked like a city that had spent decades losing arguments with scarcity and still turned up to work out of habit.

No neural infrastructure shimmered in the air.

No seamless augmented overlays.

No quiet confidence.

Just survival, raw and underfunded.

"Tell me we made it out," Arun said from behind her.

Maya kept looking at the city.

"We left," she said.

"That wasn't the question."

He was right.

She did not answer because she could not.

Instead she watched a convoy move along the waterfront road: armored vehicles improvised from old industrial chassis, three generations behind Prime and somehow older in spirit than that. On one distant rooftop a figure stood for an instant in a black coat, impossibly crisp against the ruin, then vanished behind the angular movement of weather.

Maya closed her eyes.

Freedom, when it finally arrived, smelled like mold, diesel, and old smoke.

It smelled like a future nobody had optimized for.

It smelled terrible.

She breathed it in anyway.
